Why fit hearts stop

Every few weeks a young runner who looked perfectly healthy collapses near a finish line. This brief pulls together race registries, autopsy studies and cardiology guidelines to explain how often that happens, what is usually wrong inside the heart, and the specific steps that lower your risk or save the person running beside you.

The short version

  1. It is rare. In US marathons and half-marathons from 2010 to 2023, about 1 in 167,000 finishers had a cardiac arrest during the race. Men had about six times the rate of women, and the full marathon about twice the rate of the half.
  2. Running usually reveals a hidden problem. Nearly every runner who arrests turns out to have one: thickened or scarred heart muscle, a wiring defect, a coronary artery in the wrong place, or cholesterol plaque. Hard effort supplies the trigger.
  3. Age changes the culprit. Under about 35, the causes are mostly inherited or structural, and many hearts look normal even at autopsy. After 35, coronary artery disease dominates, and fitness does not cancel it.
  4. Good vitals don't rule these out. Blood pressure, resting heart rate and a routine blood panel miss most of these conditions. Finding them takes the right test for your age, symptoms and family history.
  5. Warning signs often come first. Chest pain or pressure, fainting, unusual breathlessness, or a racing, irregular heartbeat during exercise means stop and get checked, even if an earlier test was normal.
  6. In India, the data point at coronary disease and behaviour. Coronary artery disease is the leading identified cause of sudden death in 18–45-year-olds. Binge drinking, unaccustomed vigorous exercise, a family history of sudden death and severe COVID raised the odds. COVID vaccination did not.
  7. Bystanders decide survival. Once US races made CPR and on-course defibrillators routine, survival after a race cardiac arrest rose from 29% to 66%.

§1 · Incidence

How rare is it, really?

Rare enough that most race directors will never see one. Common enough that every big race plans for it.

The most reliable numbers come from RACER, the Race Associated Cardiac Event Registry, which tries to capture every cardiac arrest during sanctioned US marathons and half-marathons. Its 2025 report in JAMA covered 29.3 million finishers from 2010 to 2023 and found 176 arrests: 0.6 per 100,000 finishers, or about one in 167,000.2 The previous decade's rate was 0.54 per 100,000, so the risk has barely moved.1

Risk is not spread evenly. Men had 1.12 arrests per 100,000 finishers and women 0.19. The full marathon carried 1.04 per 100,000 and the half 0.47.2 The study's senior investigator framed it simply: take the Chicago and New York marathons in any given year, and about one runner across the two will arrest. That number hasn't fallen, which tells you doctors are still not good at spotting who is at risk before race day.3

What did change is survival. In 2000–2009, 71% of runners who arrested died. In 2010–2023, 34% did, and deaths per runner halved, from 0.39 to 0.20 per 100,000.2 The investigators credit race emergency plans: volunteers trained in CPR, defibrillators spread along the course and at every medical tent, and a target of getting a defibrillator onto a collapsed runner within about three minutes.3

Why it feels like it's happening all the time

Participation has exploded, collapses now spread on social media within hours, and the victims are young and fit, which makes each story stick. In the first ten months of 2026 alone, news outlets reported runners aged 24 to 36 collapsing in races in Portland, Milwaukee, Cardiff, Seoul, Manchester, Carlsbad, Indore and the Nandi Hills near Bengaluru.31 Each one is a tragedy. Together they fit a rare event spread across millions of runners.

Two caveats. RACER recorded a small rise in arrests in 2020–2023 that its authors could not yet explain; §8 covers what is known. And the first RACER report concluded that a long-distance race carries the same or lower risk than other vigorous physical activity, which is reassuring for runners but says nothing about any one runner's heart.1

§2 · Definitions

Heart attack or cardiac arrest?

News reports call almost every collapse a “heart attack”. Cardiologists use two different terms, and the difference decides what bystanders do in the first minute.

A plumbing problem

Heart attack

A coronary artery blocks, usually when a cholesterol plaque cracks and a clot forms on it. The muscle downstream is starved of oxygen and starts to die. The person is usually conscious, often with chest pain or pressure, sweating, nausea or breathlessness. Call for help at once. A heart attack can tip into a cardiac arrest.

An electrical problem

Cardiac arrest

The heart's rhythm collapses into ventricular fibrillation. The pumping chambers quiver instead of squeezing, blood pressure drops to zero, and the person collapses within seconds, unresponsive and not breathing normally. Without CPR and a shock, the brain starts to die within minutes.

In runners under about 35, most race collapses are cardiac arrests without a classic heart attack. In older runners, a narrowed or blocked coronary artery is often what sets the arrest off. Either way, a runner who is down, unresponsive and not breathing normally needs CPR and a defibrillator immediately (§10).

§3 · Mechanism

A hidden fault meets a trigger

Cardiologists describe sudden cardiac death as the meeting of two things: a substrate, an abnormality that is usually silent for years, and a trigger that pushes it into a lethal rhythm. A race supplies several triggers at once.

Neither ingredient is usually enough on its own. Plenty of people carry a hidden fault and run for decades without trouble, and millions of normal hearts handle hard races every weekend. Prevention therefore has three parts: find the fault before race day, avoid the triggers you control, and make sure that if the worst happens, a defibrillator is close.

§4 · Causes

The usual suspects under 40

Which hidden fault turns up most depends on age, and on how carefully the heart is examined after death.

The clearest modern picture comes from a UK cardiac pathology centre where expert pathologists examined the hearts of 357 athletes who died suddenly between 1994 and 2014. Their average age was 29.4

Older US data put hypertrophic cardiomyopathy first: 36% of cardiovascular sudden deaths in young competitive athletes from 1980 to 2006, followed by coronary artery anomalies at 17%.7 Part of the difference is method. When specialist cardiac pathologists review every heart, many deaths once filed as HCM turn out to be a normal heart, or an athlete's heart misread. The six conditions below cover almost all of it.

Inherited · heart muscle

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)

An inherited thickening of the heart muscle, usually worst in the septum. Under the microscope the muscle cells are disorganised and mixed with scar, which makes the tissue electrically unstable. It affects about 1 in 500 people, and most have never been diagnosed.8 It was the leading cause of sudden death in young competitive athletes in the long-running US registry.7

Why exercise matters
Thick, stiff muscle needs more oxygen than its blood supply easily delivers and can block blood leaving the heart. Adrenaline and dehydration make both worse.
Clues
A heart murmur, breathlessness or chest pain on exertion, fainting, an abnormal ECG (most people with HCM have one), a relative with HCM or an early sudden death.
Athlete's heart or HCM?
Training thickens the walls too, but rarely past 12–13 mm. HCM is usually diagnosed at 15 mm or more. The grey zone in between needs a sports cardiologist, often with MRI, genetic testing or a supervised break from training to see whether the thickening shrinks.
Found by
ECG and echocardiogram; cardiac MRI to confirm.

Inherited · heart muscle

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ARVC)

An inherited weakness in the proteins that glue heart cells to each other. Over time, muscle in the right ventricle is replaced by fat and scar that conduct electricity unevenly. In the UK registry it caused 13% of athletes' sudden deaths and was one of the two findings most tied to dying during exertion.4

Why endurance matters
This is the one condition where heavy endurance training clearly speeds the disease. In a Johns Hopkins study of 87 people carrying ARVC gene variants, endurance athletes developed symptoms about a decade earlier (around 30 rather than 41) and were more likely to meet diagnostic criteria. All 13 who had a first dangerous arrhythmia during follow-up were endurance athletes, and those who cut back lowered their risk.9
Clues
Palpitations, fainting, frequent extra beats, inverted T waves in the right-sided ECG leads, a relative with ARVC or an unexplained sudden death.
Found by
ECG, Holter monitor, echocardiogram and cardiac MRI; genetic testing within families.

Present from birth · arteries

A coronary artery in the wrong place

One of the heart's own arteries starts from the wrong part of the aorta and runs between the aorta and the pulmonary artery. During hard exercise both great arteries swell with blood and can pinch it, and its slit-like, sharply angled opening can narrow further. The muscle it feeds starves mid-race.

Why it's treacherous
It is invisible on a standard ECG and often on a treadmill test. In a review of 27 young athletes who died of it, all 9 who had an ECG in life and all 6 who had a maximal stress test had normal results.10
The giveaway
Symptoms. Of the 12 athletes whose histories were known, 10 had warning signs, usually fainting or chest pain during exercise, in the two years before death.10
Found by
CT coronary angiography or cardiac MRI, ordered because of symptoms. Surgery can correct it.

Acquired · arteries

Coronary artery disease, earlier than expected

Cholesterol plaque in the coronary arteries. It is the leading cause of exercise-related sudden death after 35, and in US races from 2010 to 2023 it was the most common cause among arrests where a cause was found (40%).3 In India it already leads among 18–45-year-olds who die suddenly (§7).

Two ways it kills
A soft plaque cracks and a clot blocks the artery. Or a fixed narrowing that is harmless at rest can't deliver enough blood at race pace, called demand ischaemia. The first RACER study found mostly the second pattern in the arrests it could examine; the 2025 update found both.13
Why fitness isn't enough
LDL cholesterol, lipoprotein(a), blood pressure, smoking, diabetes and family history build plaque whatever your VO₂ max. The RACER investigators put it bluntly: no one is fit enough to outrun traditional risk factors.3
Found by
A risk assessment and blood tests first; then an exercise test, a coronary calcium score or CT coronary angiography, depending on age, risk and symptoms.

Often inherited · wiring

Electrical faults and the normal-looking heart

In 42% of the UK athletes, the heart looked normal even under the microscope. These deaths are labelled sudden arrhythmic death syndrome (SADS) and are thought to be electrical: faults in the ion channels that time each heartbeat, which leave nothing to see at autopsy.4

Main types
Long QT syndrome (the heart recharges too slowly between beats), Brugada syndrome (more common in men and in East and Southeast Asia; fever can unmask it), CPVT (adrenaline-triggered, with a normal resting ECG) and Wolff-Parkinson-White (an extra electrical pathway).
Clues
Fainting during exercise, excitement, a sudden loud noise or swimming; seizures without a clear cause; relatives who died suddenly, drowned unexpectedly or crashed for no reason.
After a SADS death
Close relatives should be screened, because many of these conditions run in families. Post-mortem genetic testing of UK athletes found a clinically actionable variant in 17% of cases, mostly in cardiomyopathy genes.6

Acquired · heart muscle

Myocarditis and scar

Inflammation of the heart muscle, usually after a viral infection. Active inflammation makes the muscle electrically irritable, and it can leave patchy scar that stays irritable. Racing or training hard during or just after a viral illness is a classic setup. The UK registry also found unexplained left-ventricular thickening or scarring in 16% of athletes, and that scarring was strongly linked to dying during exertion.4

The rule
European guidelines rule out sport while inflammation is active and call for a full evaluation, usually 3–6 months later, before a return to competition.19
Clues
Chest pain, palpitations, unusual breathlessness or fatigue after an illness; a racing heart with fever; an abnormal ECG or troponin blood test.
Found by
ECG, troponin and echocardiogram; cardiac MRI shows inflammation and scar as bright patches.

Less common

The rest of the list

Aortic dissection (in Marfan syndrome or with a bicuspid aortic valve), dilated cardiomyopathy, mitral valve prolapse with scarring, and deaths linked to anabolic steroids, stimulants or cocaine. In contact sports, a blow to the chest at exactly the wrong moment in the heartbeat (commotio cordis) can trigger fibrillation in a normal heart; it is not a running risk.

§5 · Dose

Can running itself hurt the heart?

The question most runners actually ask. The answer has two halves: a hard effort raises risk for a short time, and fitness lowers it over a lifetime.

During a hard effort, risk rises briefly

Vigorous exertion temporarily raises the chance of a heart attack or cardiac arrest. Among 21,481 US male physicians followed for 12 years, the risk of sudden death was about 17 times higher during and just after vigorous exercise than at other times. The absolute risk stayed tiny: one sudden death per 1.5 million bouts of exercise. Men who exercised regularly had a much smaller spike.12

How big the spike is depends on how used to hard effort you are. A landmark study of 1,228 heart-attack patients measured it directly.

The lesson for racers: the riskiest effort is the unaccustomed one. A runner racing well beyond their training, or coming back from months off and going straight to race pace, carries the biggest spike. India's national study of young adults found the same signal: vigorous activity in the 48 hours before death came with 3.7 times the odds of unexplained sudden death, and its lead author pointed out that habitual exercise lowers this risk while unaccustomed effort raises it.24

Over a lifetime, fitness protects

Among 122,007 adults who did treadmill tests at the Cleveland Clinic, the fittest 2.3% had 80% lower mortality over about eight years than the least fit, and the researchers found no fitness level beyond which the benefit reversed.13

The real concerns at the extreme end

Decades of very high-volume endurance training do change the heart in ways worth knowing. None of them makes running dangerous for most people, but each matters for some.

More coronary calcium in lifelong male athletes. Calcified plaque is thought to be more stable than soft plaque, which may be part of why endurance athletes still live longer. A larger 2023 Belgian study, though, found lifelong athletes also carried more non-calcified plaque, so the “it's only stable calcium” reassurance isn't settled. Specialists' advice to a runner with a high calcium score is to treat the risk factors and keep exercising, because fitness lowers the rate of heart attacks at every calcium level.15

Temporary right-heart strain after very long events. In 40 well-trained athletes, the right ventricle pumped less well straight after races lasting 3 to 11 hours, and longer races hit harder. Function mostly recovered within a week. Five of 39, the ones with the most years of competition, had small patches of scar in the septum.16

Atrial fibrillation. Long-term endurance athletes, especially middle-aged men, develop atrial fibrillation more often than non-athletes. It is rarely what causes sudden death in runners, but new palpitations deserve a check.19

The ARVC exception. For people carrying ARVC gene variants, endurance volume clearly accelerates the disease (§4).9

Bottom line

The danger is rarely the amount of running. It is a hidden fault meeting a hard, unaccustomed effort, stacked with triggers you can control.

§6 · Race day

Race day: where and why

Arrests cluster in the final stretch, when effort, adrenaline, heat and fluid shifts all peak together.

Race medical teams plan around this. The RACER investigators note that the risk of an arrest is greatest near the end of a race, and big races put extra defibrillators and medics there.3 Most of the collapses reported in 2026 follow the same pattern.

Heat, the trigger India gets most of

Exertional heat stroke is a different emergency from cardiac arrest, but it fells runners in the same final kilometres and puts a heavy load on any vulnerable heart. Race medicine judges heat with the wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), which folds humidity, sun and wind into one number.

At a hot US road race studied over 15 years, the runners who developed heat stroke ran faster than everyone else, even after accounting for age, sex, ability and conditions at the start. They did not ease off as the flags turned red.21 The good news is that heat stroke is survivable: when aggressive cooling, ideally an ice-water tub, starts within about 30 minutes of collapse, survival is close to 100%. Race medics call this “cool first, transport second”.22 Watch for confusion, odd or aggressive behaviour, stumbling, or collapse in a hot runner. They may still be sweating.

Water: more is not safer

In the 2002 Boston Marathon, 13% of runners tested at the finish had low blood sodium (hyponatraemia), and 0.6% had dangerously low levels. The strongest risk factor was gaining weight during the race, meaning they drank more than they sweated out. Slower finishers (over four hours) and people at either extreme of body weight were also at higher risk.20 Drink to thirst, use electrolyte drinks on long efforts, and never force fluids.

The 48 hours before: alcohol, stimulants, sleep

  • Alcohol. Binge drinking in the previous 48 hours carried the highest odds of any factor in India's study of unexplained sudden deaths, about 5.3 times.24
  • Stimulants. At the Mayo Clinic, 7 of 144 cardiac-arrest survivors with genetic heart conditions (5%) had drunk energy drinks shortly before arresting, usually alongside other stressors such as lost sleep, dehydration or fasting.23 Pre-workout powders carry similar stimulants; recreational drugs and anabolic steroids add risk on top.
  • Sleep and nerves. Sleep loss was one of the co-factors in those Mayo cases, and race-morning nerves add more adrenaline.

None of these is likely to harm a normal heart on its own. Stacked on a hidden fault, on a hot morning, in the last kilometre, they are exactly what you don't want.

§7 · India

The India lens

Most sudden deaths of young Indians don't happen on race courses, but the Indian data show clearly who is at risk and why.

What autopsies show: AIIMS, New Delhi

Over one year, from May 2023 to April 2024, forensic specialists at AIIMS New Delhi examined 2,214 bodies and identified 180 sudden natural deaths. More than half (57%) were adults aged 18–45. In the 94 young cases with complete examinations, the average age was about 34, men outnumbered women 4.5 to 1, 57% smoked and 52% drank alcohol. Across all ages, 55% died at home and 40% at night or in the early morning, so these were mostly not deaths during exercise.25

What raised the odds: ICMR's national study

ICMR's National Institute of Epidemiology compared 729 apparently healthy 18–45-year-olds who died suddenly and unexplained between October 2021 and March 2023 with 2,916 matched living controls, across 47 hospitals in 19 states and union territories.24

For runners, two findings stand out. Vigorous exercise in the previous 48 hours raised the odds, and the lead author stressed that the risk comes from unaccustomed effort, not regular training. And a family history of sudden death more than doubled the odds, which is why knowing your family's story matters (§9).

Why South Asian hearts need earlier attention

South Asians have heart attacks about ten years earlier than people of European descent, and more often with disease in several arteries at once.27 About a quarter of Indians have high lipoprotein(a), a cholesterol particle set by your genes that raises coronary risk two- to three-fold and barely responds to diet or exercise.26 Lp(a) is missing from most standard health-check packages, even though guidelines now suggest measuring it at least once in adulthood.27 US cholesterol guidelines count high Lp(a) as a risk-enhancing factor, as they do South Asian ancestry.26

The pattern shows up in athletes too. In the UK registry, coronary artery disease was twice as common among Asian athletes who died suddenly (15%) as among white athletes (7%), and it explained 18% of the Asian athletes' deaths during exercise.5

What this means for a runner in India

  • Get a lipid profile and a one-time Lp(a) test in your 20s or 30s, not your 50s.
  • Treat smoking and binge drinking as cardiac risk factors, not lifestyle footnotes. Both were common among young Indians who died suddenly.
  • Respect heat and humidity on race morning; go by the WBGT flag (§6).
  • Don't jump into a race after months of little training. Unaccustomed effort is where the spike lives.
  • Know your family's heart history (§9).
§8 · Evidence check

COVID, vaccines and “died suddenly”

Since 2021, social media has linked almost every young sudden death to COVID vaccines. Here is what the studies designed to test that idea found.

India · case-control · 2023

Vaccination did not raise the risk

In ICMR's study of 729 young adults who died suddenly and 2,916 matched controls, one vaccine dose was linked to lower odds of unexplained sudden death (0.58) and two doses to lower still (0.51). Past hospitalisation for COVID raised the odds (3.8).24

India · autopsy series · 2025

Coronary disease led the causes

At AIIMS New Delhi, vaccination history did not differ between younger and older sudden-death victims (83% and 88% vaccinated), and coronary artery disease was the leading cause. This design can't prove safety on its own, but it found no signal.25

US · athletes after infection · 2021

Heart involvement after COVID was uncommon

Among 789 professional athletes, 0.6% had heart inflammation on imaging after COVID. When every one of 1,597 college athletes had a cardiac MRI, 2.3% met criteria for myocarditis, most of them without symptoms.2928

US · race arrests · 2025

A small rise, cause unknown

US race arrests ticked up slightly in 2020–2023. Myocarditis did not appear among the arrests with complete records from that period. The RACER authors list possible explanations, including delayed medical care during the pandemic, a wave of new runners, or chance, and say the cause is not known.3

Known side effect

Vaccine myocarditis is real, and rare

Myocarditis after mRNA vaccines is a recognised side effect, mostly mild and mostly in young men in the days after a dose. India's programme relied mainly on non-mRNA vaccines (Covishield and Covaxin).

What to do with this

After any significant viral illness, COVID included, rebuild training gradually. Chest pain, palpitations, unusual breathlessness or fainting when you return means stop and see a doctor. An ECG, a troponin blood test and an echocardiogram are the usual first steps, with cardiac MRI if needed.

§9 · Prevention

How to protect yourself

You can't screen your way to zero risk, and the RACER investigators argue against blanket testing of everyone who runs. But most of the risk sits in identifiable people and identifiable moments.

Never run through these five warning signs

  • Chest pain, pressure or tightnessincluding in the arm, jaw, neck or upper back, during or after effort
  • Fainting or nearly faintingduring exercise or just after stopping
  • Unusual breathlessnessout of proportion to the effort, or new for you
  • A racing or irregular heartbeatpounding, fluttering or skipping that isn't normal for you
  • An unexplained drop in performanceor unusual exhaustion that training doesn't explain

These signs matter because sudden cardiac arrest is often less sudden than it looks. In a ten-year Oregon study of 839 middle-aged adults who had a sudden cardiac arrest, 51% had warning symptoms in the previous four weeks, mostly chest pain or breathlessness, and in 93% of them the symptoms came back in the final 24 hours. Only 19% called for help.17

Symptoms outrank old test results. A normal ECG or treadmill test months ago does not explain away chest pain or fainting today. Young athletes who died of misplaced coronary arteries had normal stress tests on record (§4).10 If a sign appears mid-race, stop, sit down and tell a marshal. The race will be there next year.

Know your family's heart story

Ask your parents, siblings, aunts and uncles:

  • Did anyone die suddenly or unexpectedly before 50, including drowning, a single-vehicle crash or an unexplained “seizure”?
  • Has anyone been told they have cardiomyopathy, a thickened heart, long QT, Brugada or “an electrical problem”?
  • Does anyone have a pacemaker or implanted defibrillator from a young age?
  • Did a parent or sibling have a heart attack, stent or bypass young (men under 55, women under 65)?

A yes to any of the first three means a cardiology review with an ECG and echocardiogram, and possibly genetic counselling. A yes to the last means taking your own cholesterol and Lp(a) seriously, early. A family history of sudden death more than doubled the odds of unexplained sudden death in the ICMR study.24

Match the tests to your age and risk

Everyone who races

The baseline

  • Know the five warning signs and stop if one appears.
  • Know your family history.
  • Learn hands-only CPR and where the defibrillators are on your course.
Under 35 · no symptoms or family history

Consider one athlete-read ECG

A 12-lead ECG read by someone who uses athlete-specific criteria picks up most cases of HCM and many electrical faults. Who reads it matters: ordinary readings often flag normal athletic changes as abnormal. An abnormal result leads to an echocardiogram and specialist review. In Italy's Veneto region, ECG-based screening of competitive athletes was followed by an 89% fall in their sudden cardiac deaths; the study was observational, and the US does not mandate it.18

35 and older, or younger with risk factors

Add a coronary risk check

Blood pressure, LDL cholesterol or ApoB, a one-time Lp(a), HbA1c, smoking and family history. European sports-cardiology guidelines say screening of adult athletes should include this risk assessment and an exercise stress test, and that a coronary calcium score can be considered for people at moderate risk.19

Any warning sign, any age

Stop hard training and see a cardiologist

Testing follows the symptom: ECG, echocardiogram, exercise test, heart-rhythm monitoring. Chest pain or fainting during exertion needs CT coronary angiography or MRI to rule out a misplaced coronary artery, even after a normal stress test.

Sudden death or inherited heart disease in the family

Screen the family

A cardiology review with ECG and echocardiogram for first-degree relatives, plus genetic counselling. Some conditions only show up later, so repeat checks may be advised.

Fix the numbers running can't

Endurance training improves blood pressure, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol and insulin sensitivity. It does little for genetically high LDL and nothing for Lp(a). Know your LDL (or ApoB), your Lp(a) once, your blood pressure and HbA1c, and don't smoke. If treatment is recommended, being fit is not a reason to skip it.3

Race-week rules

  • Don't race with a fever, body aches or a chest infection, and come back gradually after a real viral illness.
  • No alcohol binge in the 48 hours before. Skip energy drinks, pre-workout stimulants and anything new.
  • Sleep. Arrive rested, not wired.
  • Check the WBGT flag. In red, slow down; in black, don't race.
  • Drink to thirst, use electrolytes on long efforts, and don't overdrink.
  • Run the race you trained for. A personal-best attempt off a broken training block is exactly the unaccustomed effort from §5.
  • In the last kilometres, a sprint finish is optional. Stop at once for chest pain, faintness or a racing, irregular heartbeat.
  • Write your name, an emergency contact and any medical condition on the back of your bib. Run with a friend if you can, and know where the medical tents and defibrillators are.

§10 · Emergency

If a runner goes down

Most race arrests happen in front of other people. What those people do in the next three minutes decides the outcome.

  1. Step 1

    Check

    Tap their shoulders and shout. Look for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. Gasping or a few jerking movements are not normal breathing; they are signs of cardiac arrest.

  2. Step 2

    Call

    Shout for help. Call 112, or 108 for an ambulance in most Indian states, and send someone running for the nearest defibrillator (AED) and a race medic.

  3. Step 3

    Push

    Heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, the other on top. Push hard, about 5–6 cm, and fast, 100–120 times a minute, the beat of “Stayin' Alive”. Let the chest rise fully between pushes.

  4. Step 4

    Shock

    Switch on the AED and follow its voice. Bare the chest, stick the pads where the pictures show, and stand clear when told. It only shocks a rhythm that needs a shock.

  5. Step 5

    Keep going

    Don't stop compressions until medics take over or the runner starts breathing normally. Swap with another rescuer every two minutes; good CPR is exhausting.

§11 · Myths

Myths and facts

Eight beliefs that get runners, and the people running beside them, into trouble.

Myth

I'm fit, so my heart is fine.

Fitness lowers your average risk, but it doesn't remove an inherited heart condition or cholesterol plaque. In US race arrests from 2010 to 2023, coronary artery disease was the leading identified cause.3

Myth

My annual health check was normal.

Standard packages cover blood sugar, cholesterol and perhaps a resting ECG. They rarely include an echocardiogram, an athlete-trained ECG reading or Lp(a), and none can see a misplaced coronary artery.

Myth

A normal stress test clears me.

Stress tests find blockages that limit blood flow. Young athletes who died of misplaced coronary arteries had normal stress tests on record. New symptoms override old results.10

Myth

Marathons wreck your heart.

For almost everyone, more fitness means a longer life, with no upper limit found.13 Extreme volume adds some coronary calcium, brief right-heart strain and atrial fibrillation risk, and it speeds up ARVC in people who carry the genes.

Myth

Young people are dying suddenly because of COVID vaccines.

India's ICMR case-control study found vaccination was linked to lower odds of unexplained sudden death in 18–45-year-olds. Severe COVID, binge drinking, unaccustomed vigorous exercise and a family history raised the odds.24

Myth

Drink as much as you can during a race.

Overdrinking dilutes blood sodium. In one Boston Marathon study, 13% of runners finished with low sodium, and gaining weight during the race was the main risk factor. Drink to thirst.20

Myth

If someone collapses, wait for the ambulance.

Start CPR and get an AED immediately. Survival falls with every minute, and US race survival more than doubled once bystander CPR and on-course defibrillators became routine.2

Myth

If they're gasping, they're still breathing.

Gasping after a collapse is a sign of cardiac arrest, not normal breathing. Start CPR.
