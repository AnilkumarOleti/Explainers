The animal

What running is, and why a slow, sweaty, two-legged primate turned out to be one of the best long-distance runners on Earth.

Aid 01km 7 · The animal

Running is a chain of small jumps

Walking keeps a foot on the ground at all times. Running adds a moment when both feet are in the air. That small change turns your legs from stilts into springs.

2–3×

body weight goes through the leg at each running step.

35%

of a step's mechanical work can come back from the stretched Achilles tendon.

Ker et al., 1987

17%

comes back from the springy arch of the foot.

Ker et al., 1987

≈2m/s

(about 7 km/h) is where people naturally switch from walking to running.

Deep diveInside the stride

Two gaits, two physics. In walking the stance leg is a nearly straight strut. The body rises over it and falls forward, trading kinetic for potential energy like an inverted pendulum. In running the leg behaves like a spring: on landing, the body's kinetic and potential energy are stored briefly as elastic strain in tendons and the arch, then returned as the leg extends. This is the spring-mass model.

Duty factor. The share of a stride each foot spends on the ground is above 0.5 in walking (so contact overlaps) and below 0.5 in running (so there is a flight phase). Faster running shortens ground contact further.

Why ≈2 m/s. The pendulum stops working when centripetal acceleration over the stance leg approaches gravity. Biomechanists express this as the Froude number, v²/(g·L). People switch to running at about 0.5, which for a leg length of 0.9 m is roughly 2.1 m/s.

Springs in the leg. Using cadaver feet loaded like a running stride at 4.5 m/s, Ker and colleagues estimated that the Achilles tendon returns about 35% and the arch about 17% of the mechanical work of a step. When insoles stop the arch from compressing, the metabolic cost of running rises, which suggests the arch spring saves real energy.

Cost. On level ground the energy cost of running is about 3.4 J per kg per metre, and it barely changes with speed. Going faster costs more per minute, roughly the same per kilometre. As a rule of thumb, a runner burns about 1 kcal per kg of body weight per km.

Landing. Runners who grew up barefoot often land on the forefoot, which produces smaller collision forces than a heel strike. Most runners in cushioned shoes land heel first. Whether changing your foot strike prevents injury is still debated.

Sources: Ker et al. 1987, Nature · Stearne et al. 2016, Scientific Reports · Minetti et al. 2002, J Appl Physiol · Lieberman et al. 2010, Nature

Aid 02km 14 · The animal

Three machines in one body

Every run is a negotiation between three systems: a spring that bounces you along, a furnace that powers it, and a governor that decides how hard you are allowed to go. The rest of this page is about how each is built, trained and, over 100 miles, broken.

Spring

Tendons, muscles and bones that store and return energy each step. Limited by how much pounding they can absorb.

Breaks as: shredded quads, sore feet, stress fractures

Furnace

Heart, lungs, blood and mitochondria turning fuel and oxygen into motion. Limited by oxygen delivery, fuel and heat.

Breaks as: bonking, overheating, an upset gut

Governor

The brain reads signals from the body and sets the pace it predicts you can survive. Limited by fatigue, sleep and motivation.

Breaks as: 3 a.m. despair, hallucinations, bad decisions

Aid 03km 21 · The animal

Built to run slowly, for hours

Humans are poor sprinters. A rabbit outpaces us. Over long distances in the heat, though, few mammals can keep up, and many features of the human body seem to exist for running.

26

body traits that Bramble and Lieberman tied to endurance running.

Univ. of Utah, 2004

≈2million yrs

since our genus started to look built for running.

Bramble & Lieberman, 2004

25–35km

length of recorded Kalahari persistence hunts, run in 39–42 °C heat.

Liebenberg, 2006
Deep diveThe case, and the critics

The idea. Biologist David Carrier proposed in 1984 that human ancestors ran prey to exhaustion, pointing to sweat glands, hairless skin and bipedal breathing. In 2004 Dennis Bramble and Daniel Lieberman listed 26 traits that help endurance running, many visible in fossils, and argued they appear with the genus Homo around 2 million years ago. Long legs relative to body mass show up clearly in Homo erectus.

Why heat matters. A galloping quadruped locks its breathing to its stride, so it must stop to pant. Humans breathe independently of stride and dump heat by sweating. In the Kalahari, Louis Liebenberg documented hunts of 25 to 35 km over two to five hours at midday temperatures of 39 to 42 °C. One hunt covered 25.1 km in 3 hours 50 minutes, about 6.3 km/h, with trackers and runners working as a team.

The critics. Anthropologists Travis Pickering and Henry Bunn argued that early Homo could not have tracked well enough for pursuit hunting in wooded savannas, and that modern persistence hunting is rare. Bunn later argued that the ages of animals butchered at Olduvai Gorge don't fit running prey down. Lieberman's group replied that tracking is ancient and that early humans had no other way to kill large animals without projectile weapons. The debate is unresolved.

A cooler test. In Wales, the annual Man versus Horse race has been run since 1980. Horses usually win in the cool, wet hills, and humans have won only a handful of times. Endurance running is a hot-weather advantage.

Sources: Bramble & Lieberman 2004 · Britannica: persistence hunting · Liebenberg 2006 · Lieberman et al. 2007, reply to Pickering & Bunn · Man versus Horse Marathon · Nature news 2004

Deep diveEndorphins or endocannabinoids?

The old story. For decades the runner's high was credited to β-endorphins, opioid peptides that rise in the blood during long exercise. They are large molecules that cross the blood–brain barrier poorly, which always made the story awkward. Brain imaging has since suggested opioid release inside the brain after a two-hour run, so opioids are probably involved in something.

The mouse result. In mice, the calming and pain-dulling effects of running depended on cannabinoid CB1 receptors and not on opioid receptors.

The human trial. In 2021, 63 people ran 45 minutes at moderate intensity after taking either naltrexone, an opioid blocker, or a placebo. Running raised euphoria, lowered anxiety and increased blood levels of the endocannabinoids anandamide and 2-AG compared with walking. Naltrexone changed none of that, and as many people reported a runner's high on the blocker as on placebo.

Longer runs. A 2026 study followed marathoners and runners in a 100, 160 or 230 km race. Anandamide kept rising through the marathon and was still elevated after the ultras. Ultra finishers reported less anxiety, but their euphoria did not change significantly.

Caveat. Rising levels show the system is active. They don't yet prove it causes the feeling, and the researchers say so.

Sources: Siebers et al. 2021, Psychoneuroendocrinology · Siebers et al. 2026, BMC Medicine

Aid 05km 35 · The engine

The furnace and its three numbers

Muscles make energy two ways. Fast and short without oxygen, or slow and steady with it. Any effort longer than a couple of minutes runs mostly on oxygen, and then your speed comes down to three numbers.

84%

of the energy for an all-out 1,500 m comes from oxygen. For 200 m it's 29%.

Spencer & Gastin, 2001

30%

Elite runners with similar VO₂max can differ this much in economy.

Barnes & Kilding, 2018

≈4%

less energy per km in a carbon-plated "super shoe" than in leading racing shoes, in lab tests on 18 runners.

Hoogkamer et al., 2017
Deep diveFuel, oxygen and lactate

Three energy systems. Muscles run on ATP and hold only seconds' worth. Phosphocreatine refills it almost instantly for about ten seconds. Glycolysis splits glucose without oxygen, fast but short-lived, and produces lactate. Aerobic metabolism in the mitochondria burns carbohydrate and fat with oxygen. It is slower to ramp up and can run for days. All three work at once; the race length sets the mix.

VO₂max is the ceiling on aerobic power. It depends mostly on how much blood the heart can pump each minute, and on how much oxygen working muscle can pull out of that blood. Untrained adults often sit around 30–45 ml/kg/min; elite distance runners reach 70–85.

Fractional utilisation is the share of VO₂max you can sustain. It tracks the lactate threshold. Two people with the same VO₂max can race very differently if one can hold 85% and the other only 70%.

Running economy is the oxygen cost of covering ground, here in ml of O₂ per kg per km. It depends on tendon stiffness, leg mass, technique and footwear, and it varies by up to 30% even among elites. Joyner and Coyle combined the three into one rule: race speed ≈ VO₂max × fraction ÷ cost.

Lactate is a fuel. The acid that makes hard running burn comes mostly from hydrogen ions released as ATP is used, and lactate itself is shuttled to the heart, liver and slow-twitch fibres and burned. The first lactate threshold (LT1) is where blood levels first rise above resting. The second (LT2, close to the maximal lactate steady state, often near 4 mmol/L) is the fastest pace at which production and clearance still balance. Ultra runners spend most of a race below LT1, where fat supplies a large share of the energy.

Super shoes. A stiff carbon plate and a thick, resilient foam returned more energy per step. In Hoogkamer's lab test, energy cost fell by about 4% on average (range 2–6%) compared with two leading racing shoes.

Sources: Spencer & Gastin 2001 · Joyner & Coyle 2008, J Physiol · Barnes & Kilding 2018, Sports Med · CU Boulder on Hoogkamer et al. 2017

Aid 06km 42 · The engine

What every run leaves behind

Training works by stress and over-repair. Each run is a signal. In the days after, the body rebuilds with a little more capacity than it needed. Rest is when you get fitter.

Heart

Chambers grow and pump more blood per beat. Resting pulse falls.

Blood

More plasma within days, and over time more red cells to carry oxygen.

Muscle

More mitochondria, more capillaries, more enzymes that burn fat.

Fuel

More fat burned at the same pace, so the small sugar store lasts longer.

Tendons & bones

Get stiffer and denser, slowly. They need months, and they set the speed limit on how fast you can build up.

Brain

Learns pacing, wastes less movement, and gets better at tolerating discomfort.

Deep diveFrom signal to adaptation

The signal. A long or hard run lowers the muscle's energy charge, raises calcium and creates mild oxidative stress. Sensors such as the enzyme AMPK pick this up and switch on PGC-1α, often called the master regulator of mitochondrial growth. Repeated over weeks, this builds more and better mitochondria.

Supply lines. Low oxygen and higher blood flow in working muscle trigger growth factors such as VEGF, which sprout new capillaries. More capillaries mean shorter diffusion distances and more time for oxygen to leave the blood.

Blood and heart. Plasma volume expands within days of starting training, which lets the heart fill more completely. Over months, endurance training enlarges the heart's chambers ("athlete's heart"), raising stroke volume. That is the main reason trained hearts beat slower at rest.

Fuel use. Trained muscle stores more fat inside the fibres and burns more of it at any given pace. That spares glycogen, the limited carbohydrate store that matters so much in Part IV.

The slow tissues. Tendon collagen and bone remodel over months. Load that rises faster than they can adapt leads to tendinopathy and bone stress injuries. This is the logic behind gradual increases and down weeks.

Supercompensation. Stress, then fatigue, then recovery to a slightly higher level. Too little stress and nothing changes. Too much without rest leads to overreaching and, if it continues, overtraining.

The payoff, and the price

Running is one of the cheapest health investments there is, and a little goes a long way. The costs are real, mostly minor, and the serious ones are rare.

The price

Injuries

The common cost. Most are overuse injuries to shins, knees, feet and the Achilles, usually after doing too much, too soon.

1 in 184,000

race runners had a cardiac arrest in a decade of US marathons and half marathons. Most had undiagnosed heart disease.

Kim et al., 2012

≈2.5× odds

Deep diveReading the health evidence

Association or cause? Nearly all of these numbers come from observational studies. Healthier people may simply be more likely to run, and sick people more likely to stop. Researchers adjust for age, smoking and other factors, but they can't remove that bias completely.

Dose. Pedisic's meta-analysis found no clear dose–response: more frequent, longer or faster running did not significantly increase the benefit. Lee's group estimated that gains plateau around four hours a week, with a cap of about three extra years of life.

Knees. In Alentorn-Geli's meta-analysis, prevalence of hip or knee osteoarthritis was 3.5% in recreational runners, 10.2% in sedentary controls and 13.3% in competitive runners, defined as professional or elite athletes. The risk in competitive runners was higher with more than 15 years of exposure.

Sudden cardiac arrest. Kim and colleagues counted 59 cardiac arrests among 10.9 million US marathon and half-marathon runners between 2000 and 2010, a rate of 0.54 per 100,000. Rates were higher in marathons (1.01) than half marathons (0.27), and in men (0.90) than women (0.16). 71% were fatal. Most deaths came from hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or coronary artery disease.

Atrial fibrillation. A 2021 meta-analysis found athletes had 2.46 times the odds of AF compared with non-athletes, with higher odds in mixed-sport athletes and those under 55.

Mood. In the Dutch MOODHEART study, remission at 16 weeks was 43.3% with running therapy and 44.8% with antidepressants. Adherence was 52.1% versus 82.2%. Running improved weight, waist, blood pressure, heart rate and heart rate variability. Because many people chose their own treatment, the groups weren't fully comparable.

Sources: Pedisic et al. 2020, BJSM · Report on Lee et al. 2017 · Alentorn-Geli et al. 2017, JOSPT · Verhoeven et al. 2023, J Affect Disord · Kim et al. 2012, NEJM · Newman et al. 2021, BJSM

Aid 08km 56 · The running people

The Rarámuri of the canyons

The Rarámuri, also called the Tarahumara, live in the Sierra Tarahumara of Chihuahua, Mexico. Homes are scattered across pine plateaus and the walls of some of North America's deepest canyons. Getting anywhere means a long walk or run, up and down.

≈1,870m

depth of Urique, the deepest canyon in the system.

Geography Worlds

50–120k

Rarámuri people. Estimates vary widely.

"Foot runners"

A common translation of the name Rarámuri. Strictly, rarámuri means the men; women are mukí.

Acuña Delgado, 2009
Deep diveWho the Rarámuri are

Names. Tarahumara is the Spanish-derived name. The people's own word, rarámuri, is usually translated as "foot runner"; it refers specifically to men, and women are called mukí.

Land. The Sierra Tarahumara is part of the Sierra Madre Occidental. Its six major canyons together cover roughly four times the area of the Grand Canyon. Urique, the deepest, drops about 1,870 m from rims at 2,250–2,540 m. The climb from river to rim crosses climate zones, from pine forest to subtropical scrub.

Life. Families farm maize and beans on small, scattered plots and travel on foot. Researchers who measured Rarámuri bodies note how much ordinary walking they do. Sharing, called korima, is a core value. Gatherings often centre on tesgüino, a home-brewed corn beer.

Running's old jobs. In interviews with elderly Rarámuri runners, Daniel Lieberman's team heard about group persistence hunts of deer and other game. Rifles arrived about 40 years before that research and ended most of them. Running also lives on in long ceremonial dances and in races, which runners describe as a form of prayer.

Sources: Lieberman et al. 2020, Current Anthropology · Sapiens · Acuña Delgado 2009 · Geo-Mexico on the Copper Canyon

Aid 09km 63 · The running people

A race with a wooden ball

The great Rarámuri race is the rarajípari. Two teams kick a carved wooden ball around a course, lap after lap, sometimes through the night. Women race the ariwete, flicking a hoop along with a stick. Whole communities come to watch and bet.

≈40m

gained with each flick of the ball.

200+km

"¡Aguanta!"

"¡Quien no aguanta, no vale!" Whoever can't endure isn't worth much. A saying about racing.

Acuña Delgado, 2009
Deep diveRaces as prayer, practice and party

Format. A rarajípari pits two teams against each other on a looping course. Each team chases its own ball, lifting it with the toes and top of the foot and flinging it ahead. Races are small, medium or large. The biggest go on through the night and can exceed 200 km.

Equipment. The ball is carved from oak or madroño root, about 7–8 cm across and some 250 g. In the women's ariwete, a hoop of 10–15 cm is flicked with a stick of about 80 cm, travelling 40–50 m at a time.

Meaning. Spectators bet on the outcome, and races strengthen ties between communities. Elderly runners described footraces to Lieberman's team as a form of prayer. Historically, races also kept men fit for persistence hunts and showed who should go.

Endurance as a value. Anthropologist Antonio Acuña Delgado records the saying "¡Quien no aguanta, no vale!" and reads the race as a symbol of cultural resistance.

Aid 10km 70 · The running people

Sandals, feet and corn

Traditional Rarámuri runners wear huaraches: a thin sole cut from an old tyre, tied on with a leather lace. Decades in thin sandals build stiffer, stronger feet. The fuel is mostly corn and beans.

≈2×

stiffer arches in the Rarámuri who ran in sandals than in those who wore shoes.

Lieberman, 2014

Bigger

foot muscles and higher, stiffer arches in men over 50 who had worn minimal footwear all their lives.

Holowka et al., 2018

Maize & beans

The staple diet. Pinole, toasted ground corn, is the classic trail food.

Wallace et al., 2018
Deep diveWhat the foot studies actually show

Arches. Holowka and colleagues scanned the feet of Rarámuri men over 50. Those who had worn minimal footwear all their lives had higher, stiffer arches and larger abductor hallucis and abductor digiti minimi muscles, which help support the arch.

Landings. In a 2014 study of 23 Rarámuri runners, those in huaraches landed 40% midfoot, 30% forefoot and 30% heel first. Those in conventional running shoes landed heel first about 75% of the time, and had arches about half as stiff. Lieberman cautioned that this does not mean everyone should switch footwear.

Impact. Wallace and colleagues measured Rarámuri farmers walking. Heel-impact forces were higher in huaraches than barefoot, a reminder that even a thin sole changes the forces on the body.

The sandal. A huarache is a tyre sole held by a leather thong that passes between the first and second toes and wraps around the ankle. Before about 50 years ago, soles were made from rawhide.

Food. The staple diet is mostly maize and beans. Runners carry pinole, toasted ground corn mixed with water, and share tesgüino, a corn beer, at gatherings.

Sources: Holowka et al. 2018, Scientific Reports · Lieberman 2014 · Wallace et al. 2018, Royal Society Open Science

Deep diveThe rules of the formats

Backyard ultras. Created by Lazarus Lake. A yard is 4.1667 miles (6.706 km), so 24 yards make 100 miles. Every yard starts on the hour; time left over is rest. The winner must complete one more yard than anyone else, alone. If nobody manages it, there is no winner. Phil Gore holds the record at 119 yards, about 798 km, set in 2025. The women's record is Sarah Perry's 95 yards, set in October 2025.

Fixed time. Track or short-loop races where the clock is fixed and distance is the score. Aleksandr Sorokin's 24-hour record is 319.614 km, an average of 13.3 km/h for a full day.

Stage races. Several days, each a stage, with camps between. Some are self-supported: runners carry their food and kit for a week, as in the 250 km desert races where kidney injury has been studied.

Mountain races. Rated by distance and climb together. The trail-running body ITRA adds 1 km of "effort" for every 100 m climbed (see aid station 17).

The longest certified race. The Self-Transcendence 3100 in Queens, New York: 5,649 laps of one 883 m block within 52 days, about 95 km a day. Ashprihanal Aalto's record is 40 days, 9 hours, 6 minutes (2015).

Deep diveHow a 100 is paced

Even effort, uneven pace. Experienced runners aim for steady effort. They walk steep climbs from the very first mile, run the flats and descents with care, and accept that the night will be slower.

Aid-station time adds up. Twenty stops of five minutes each cost more than an hour and a half. Many runners plan what they need from each station before they arrive.

Origins. Western States grew out of a horse race, the Tevis Cup, on the same trail. In 1974 Gordy Ainsleigh ran the course on foot in 23:42. The first official foot race followed in 1977. The trail climbs out of Squaw Valley, now Palisades Tahoe, crosses snowy high country, drops through hot canyons and fords the American River at mile 78.

The fuel equation

A 70 kg runner burns roughly 15,000 kcal in a mountain 100-miler. The body stores only about 2,000 kcal of carbohydrate. The rest must come from body fat and from food eaten on the move.

33g/h

Deep diveSugar transporters and the rebellious gut

The absorption limit. Glucose crosses the gut wall through a transporter called SGLT1, which saturates at roughly 60–70 g an hour. Fructose uses a different door, GLUT5. Mixing the two lets runners absorb about 90 g/h. With a glucose-to-fructose ratio of 1:0.8 and weeks of gut training, some reach 120 g/h, though a clear benefit over 90 g/h hasn't been shown.

Reality on the trail. In a 24-hour race, runners averaged 33 ± 12 g/h, far below the guidelines. Eating steadily for a whole day while running is hard.

The gut fights back. During hard exercise, blood is diverted away from the gut to muscle and skin. Add jostling, heat and painkillers, and the gut rebels. At Western States, 96% of runners reported gut symptoms; 43.9% of finishers said they hurt their race, and 35.6% of non-finishers gave them as a reason for stopping. Nausea was the most common. In another study, nausea and vomiting were the top reason runners dropped out.

What seems to help. At the Javelina Jundred, runners who took in more fat and more fluid had fewer gut problems. That is an association, but it fits a common tactic: real food, little and often, and slowing down when nausea starts.

Why fat matters. Even a lean 70 kg runner carries perhaps 7–10 kg of fat, around 50,000–80,000 kcal. Fat burns more slowly than carbohydrate, so the pace has to drop as glycogen runs low.

Water, salt and the overdrinking trap

Too little water slows you down. Too much can kill. Drinking well beyond thirst dilutes the sodium in your blood, and salt tablets won't protect you.

15%

of finishers in a 161 km mountain race had EAH over five years. By year it ranged from 4.6% to 51%.

Hoffman et al., 2013

135mmol/L

Blood sodium below this line counts as hyponatraemia.

93.9%

of runners in one study took salt pills. They did not prevent EAH.

Deep diveExercise-associated hyponatraemia

The mechanism. EAH happens when the body holds more water than it can get rid of. Normally, diluted blood switches off the hormone vasopressin (ADH), and the kidneys release extra water. During long exercise, pain, nausea and stress can keep vasopressin switched on anyway, a "non-osmotic" release. The kidneys then hold water even while the blood gets more dilute.

Why it's dangerous. When blood sodium falls, water moves into cells. The skull leaves no room for brain cells to swell. Early signs are nausea, headache and bloating; later come confusion, vomiting and seizures.

What the data show. In one five-year study of a 161 km race, 15.1% of finishers had EAH, more often in hot years. Many affected runners had kept their weight steady or even lost weight, so overdrinking isn't the only route. In another race study, incidence was 6.6%; overhydration was the main feature, and 93.9% of runners had taken sodium supplements, which didn't prevent it.

The practical rule. Drink to thirst. Researchers call drinking beyond thirst ill-advised. Weight gain during a race, puffy hands and a headache with nausea are warning signs. Suspected EAH is a medical emergency; medics check blood sodium before giving fluids.

Sources: Hoffman et al. 2013 · Hoffman & Stuempfle 2015 · Review of vasopressin in EAH

Up, down and thin air

Mountain ultras are mostly walked uphill and run with care downhill. Climbs cost oxygen, descents cost muscle, and altitude takes away part of the oxygen itself.

Deep diveGradients, brakes and oxygen

The measurements. Minetti and colleagues measured running and walking on a treadmill from −45% to +45%. Running cost 3.40 J/kg/m on the level, independent of speed. It rose to 18.93 at +45%, fell to a minimum of 1.73 at −20%, and climbed back to 3.92 at −45%. Walking cost 1.64 on the level and 17.33 at +45%.

Why walk the climbs. On flat ground walking is about twice as economical as running. On a +45% slope the gap shrinks to about 9%, and walking spares the legs. Ultra runners power-hike steep climbs, often with poles.

Lifting and braking. Above +15% and below −15%, efficiency approaches that of pure muscle work: concentric (shortening) work going up, eccentric (braking) work going down. Steep climbs are mostly lifting your body; steep descents are mostly braking it. Eccentric braking causes most muscle damage, which is why quads hurt more after a descent.

Best path gradient. For climbing or descending with the least energy per vertical metre, the optimum is about 20–30%.

Altitude. Thinner air carries less oxygen per breath, so VO₂max falls. In the Wehrlin and Hallén study it dropped from 66 to 55 ml/kg/min between 300 m and 2,800 m. Hardrock averages about 3,350 m and tops out on Handies Peak at 4,281 m, beyond the tested range. Acclimatising for a few weeks helps, but doesn't erase the loss.

Sources: Minetti et al. 2002, J Appl Physiol · Wehrlin & Hallén 2006 · iRunFar: Hardrock

Deep diveWho is in charge of fatigue?

Two models. Tim Noakes's "central governor" theory proposes that the brain limits effort before the body is in danger. Samuele Marcora's psychobiological model says you slow or stop when perceived effort exceeds what you're willing to give. Both put the brain at the centre, and both are debated.

The longer-is-gentler paradox. After UTMB, knee-extensor strength was down 35% and calf strength 39%. A muscle-damage marker, creatine kinase, rose from about 144 to 13,633 U/L. After the 330 km Tor des Géants, strength losses were smaller. Saugy's team concluded that runners pace conservatively from the start in such long races, and sleep loss forces them slower still.

The brain itself. In the 64-day TransEurope FootRace, runners' grey matter shrank by about 6%, returning to normal eight months later with no new lesions. In a 2025 study, myelin signals in ten marathoners' brains dropped after the race and recovered within about two months.

Sleep. Multi-day racers sleep in short blocks. Micro-sleeps, poor judgement and vivid hallucinations are common topics in their accounts, and they are why night-time navigation errors happen.

Aid 20km 140 · The ultra

Training for the long run

Ultra training is mostly slow running, done often, for months. Build in waves, practise race food, and train for the climbs, descents and nights you'll meet on race day.

Mostly easy

Most runs at a pace where you could chat. A small share of hard sessions on top.

Long runs

Time on feet matters more than speed. Back-to-back long days teach running on tired legs.

Climb and descend

Hill repeats, power hiking and poles going up. Controlled descents to toughen the quads.

Strength

Squats, lunges, step-downs and calf raises. Strong legs absorb the downhills.

Train the gut

Practise race food at race pace in long runs, building the carbs per hour gradually.

Recover

Sleep, easier weeks and a taper. Fitness is built during rest.

Deep diveWhy so much slow running

Intensity distribution. Studies of endurance athletes describe most training below the first lactate threshold, with a small share at or above the second. Easy volume builds mitochondria, capillaries and fat burning with little damage, and leaves energy for the few sessions that are hard.

Specificity. Train for the race you'll run. Mountain races need climbing and descending; night sections need practice with a headlamp; hot races need about two weeks of heat exposure beforehand.

Load and tissue. Muscles and the heart adapt faster than tendons and bones (see aid station 06). Rising volume in waves with easier weeks lets the slow tissues catch up.

Taper. Cutting volume for the last two to three weeks, while keeping some intensity, lets fatigue fade before the race.

Red flags: stop and get help

  • ConfusionSlurred speech, stumbling or odd behaviour. Can mean low sodium, hypothermia, heat stroke or low blood sugar.
  • Chest painOr fainting, or a racing, irregular heartbeat. Never normal during exercise.
  • Dark urineCola-coloured, or none for hours. Possible muscle breakdown or kidney injury.
  • Puffy and queasySwollen hands, weight gain, headache and nausea. Possible EAH. Stop drinking and find a medic.
  • Uncontrolled shiveringOr shivering that stops while you're still cold. Hypothermia: get dry, warm and sheltered.
  • Hot and confusedVery hot skin with confusion or collapse. Possible heat stroke: cool the person immediately.
