Gut & health · field note

Whether a germ makes you sick can come down to what else is in your stomach.

The same mouthful of bacteria is often harmless on an empty stomach and dangerous on a full one. One dial explains why: acid.

01  The gate

Your stomach is a chemical checkpoint, not a food bag

Before anything you swallow reaches the gut — where bacteria actually set up and multiply — it has to cross the stomach. On an empty stomach that is a hostile place: a pool of hydrochloric acid sitting at roughly pH 1.5–3.5, acidic enough to denature proteins and kill most microbes within minutes. Physicians call it the gastric acid barrier, and for most of the last century it was treated as the body's first line of defense against swallowed pathogens.

The key number for any given germ is its infectious dose: how many organisms you have to swallow before enough survive the crossing to establish an infection. The acid barrier doesn't have to kill every cell. It just has to kill enough that the survivors fall below the number the gut needs to be colonised.

The pH scale is logarithmic: pH 2 is ten times more acidic than pH 3, and a hundred times more than pH 4. Small moves on this strip are large moves in chemistry.

02  The dial

Food turns the acid down — in two different ways

A meal doesn't just sit on top of the acid. It changes the chemistry of the whole compartment, and it does so by two separate mechanisms that are worth keeping apart.

1 · It buffers the acid

Food — especially protein — soaks up hydrogen ions. A typical meal pushes gastric pH from around 2 up to 4–5 within minutes, and a large protein-rich meal can briefly take it near neutral. The stomach claws its way back down over the next two to four hours as it keeps secreting acid, but during that window the barrier is far weaker. A germ swallowed with lunch meets a very different stomach than one swallowed before breakfast.

2 · It physically shields the germs

This one is easy to miss. Bacteria tucked inside a crumb of ground beef or a droplet of milk fat are not floating freely in acid — they sit in a micro-pocket that the acid penetrates slowly. In lab tests, Salmonella that was wiped out in acid alone survived pH 2.5 when it was riding on ground beef or inside egg white. Fat and solid protein are the best shields; watery food is the worst.

WHY IT MATTERS This is why fatty, protein-rich foods — undercooked meat, dairy, eggs, peanut-based sauces — are such efficient vehicles for food poisoning. They don't just carry the germs; they escort them past the acid, raising the pH and hiding the cells at the same time.

03  The crossing

Watch the barrier work — or fail

Pick a pathogen, set the state of your stomach, and choose how many cells you swallowed. The stomach tints to its current pH; the acid kills a fraction of the dose; the survivors drop into the gut tray. Infection happens only when enough survivors clear the gut's colonisation threshold.

Play with it and a pattern appears. For an acid-sensitive germ like cholera, the empty stomach is a wall: you'd need to swallow an improbable number of cells to get past it, but a meal or an antacid drops that requirement by ten-thousand-fold. For an acid-resistant germ like E. coli O157 or Shigella, the dial barely matters — a dozen cells can infect you fed or fasting. Those are the bugs that spread person-to-person and through tiny contaminated doses, precisely because the acid gate was never much of an obstacle to them.

04  The evidence

Two numbers that made the case

The cleanest proof came from deliberately feeding volunteers live bacteria — studies that would be hard to run today, but which pinned the effect down precisely.

10⁸ → 10⁴

Cholera, with a spoon of baking soda

Volunteers needed about 100 million V. cholerae to reliably get sick. Give them 2 g of sodium bicarbonate to neutralise their stomach acid first, and the dose fell to around 10,000 — a 10,000-fold drop from a single change to pH.

10⁵ → ~50

Salmonella, in food vs water

In clean buffer it can take ~100,000 Salmonella cells to cause illness. In real contaminated food — cheese, chocolate, fatty meat — outbreaks have been traced to as few as 50–100 cells, because the food both buffers and shields them.

The flip side is just as real. People who make too little stomach acid — through antacids, proton-pump inhibitors, surgery, or simply age — carry a measurably higher risk of Salmonella, cholera, C. difficile and traveller's diarrhoea. The barrier is protective exactly to the degree that it is acidic.

05  The roster

Not every germ respects the gate

A pathogen's acid tolerance is roughly its infectious dose in disguise. Acid-sensitive bugs rely on volume or on a buffering meal to get through; acid-resistant ones evolved to survive the crossing and can infect from a tiny, food-independent dose.

06  The model to keep

One picture for all of it

Hold onto the gate. The stomach is a checkpoint whose strength is set by a single dial — pH — and a meal turns that dial down while also smuggling germs through in its pockets. Whether you get sick is a race between how many cells you swallowed and how many the acid can kill before they reach the gut.

That one image ties together a surprising spread of everyday facts: why food poisoning so often follows a big fatty meal rather than a glass of water, why acid-reflux medication carries a small infection risk, why cholera spreads explosively through drinking water that dilutes stomach acid, and why a dozen E. coli O157 from an undercooked burger can do what a million harmless gut bacteria never would.

THE ONE LINE A full or neutralised stomach is a lowered drawbridge. The same germs that your acid would have turned away on an empty stomach walk straight through.
