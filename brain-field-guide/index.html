Before the details

Two rules explain most of the map

Outside view

Six regions on the surface

Four lobes of folded cortex, plus two older parts tucked underneath.

Front · behind the forehead

Frontal lobe

Plans, decides, and stops you acting on your first impulse.

  • Planning and judgement
  • Voluntary movement
  • Speaking
Worth remembering

In 1848 an iron rod went through railway foreman Phineas Gage’s frontal lobe. He lived, walked and talked, but his personality changed so much that friends said he was “no longer Gage”. This lobe is also the last to finish developing, in your mid-20s.

Top · toward the back

Parietal lobe

Feels touch and tracks where your body is in space.

  • Touch, pain, temperature
  • Knowing where your limbs are
  • Judging distance and direction
Worth remembering

Your touch map is sized by sensitivity, not by body size. Lips and fingertips get more brain space than your whole back, which is why a tiny paper cut feels so big.

Side · behind the temple, above the ear

Temporal lobe

Turns sound into meaning and helps you recognise what you see.

  • Hearing
  • Understanding words
  • Recognising faces
Worth remembering

One small patch here is devoted to faces. When it stops working, a person sees perfectly well but cannot recognise their own family by face. About 1 in 50 people are born with a mild version.

Back · the very rear of the head

Occipital lobe

Turns signals from your eyes into what you see.

  • Shapes and edges
  • Colour
  • Movement
Worth remembering

Your eyes are at the front, but seeing happens at the very back. That is why a knock to the back of the head makes you see stars. Some people blinded by damage here can still dodge obstacles they say they cannot see.

Back · tucked underneath

Cerebellum Latin for “little brain”

Makes your movements smooth, balanced and well timed.

  • Balance
  • Fine coordination
  • Learned skills like cycling
Worth remembering

It fills about 10% of the brain’s volume but holds about 80% of its nerve cells: roughly 69 billion of 86 billion. It also predicts the feel of your own movements, which is why you cannot tickle yourself.

Base · joins the brain to the spinal cord

Brainstem

Keeps you alive without asking you.

  • Breathing and heartbeat
  • Sleeping and waking
  • Swallowing and coughing
Worth remembering

It is about the size of your thumb, and every signal between brain and body passes through it. Most movement wiring crosses sides here, so the left half of the brain moves the right half of the body.

Dead centre · on top of the brainstem

Thalamus

Sorts incoming signals and sends each one to the right part of the brain.

  • Sight, sound, touch, taste
  • Alertness
  • Attention
Worth remembering

Every sense passes through it except smell. Smell goes straight to the memory and emotion areas next door, which is why a smell can pull you into an old memory faster than a photograph can.

Centre · just below the thalamus

Hypothalamus

Keeps the body steady.

  • Body temperature
  • Hunger and thirst
  • Hormones and the body clock
Worth remembering

It weighs about 4 grams, as much as an almond, yet it runs your hormones through the pea-sized pituitary gland hanging below it. A cluster of about 20,000 of its cells keeps your 24-hour clock. Jet lag is that clock catching up.

Deep in the temporal lobe · one each side

Amygdala Greek for “almond”

Spots danger and attaches feeling to what happens to you.

  • Fear and alarm
  • Emotional memories
  • Reading emotion in faces
Worth remembering

A woman known as S.M. lost both amygdalae to a rare disease. She handles snakes with curiosity, laughed her way through a haunted house, and was once held at knifepoint without feeling afraid.

Deep in the temporal lobe · behind the amygdala

Hippocampus Greek for “seahorse”

Saves new memories and keeps your mental map of places.

  • Forming new memories
  • Finding your way
  • Filing the day’s memories during sleep
Worth remembering

In 1953 surgeons removed most of Henry Molaison’s hippocampus on both sides. For the next 55 years he could not form a new memory. And London taxi drivers, who memorise about 25,000 streets, have a larger rear hippocampus that grows with years on the job.

Deep under the cortex · around the thalamus

Basal ganglia

Picks which action to start and which to hold back, and runs your habits.

  • Starting and stopping movement
  • Habits and routines
  • Learning from reward
Worth remembering

Once a skill becomes a habit it runs here, which is why you can drive a familiar route and not remember the trip. Parkinson’s disease begins when the dopamine cells feeding this area die. Symptoms usually show only after more than half are gone.

Midline · arching over the thalamus

Corpus callosum Latin for “tough body”

Connects the left and right halves so they work as one.

  • About 200 million nerve fibres
  • Shares what each side sees and hears
  • Coordinates both hands
Worth remembering

In the 1960s doctors cut it in a few patients to stop severe epilepsy. Each half of the brain then knew things the other did not. Shown an object on one side only, patients said they saw nothing, yet their left hand picked it out correctly. The research won a Nobel Prize in 1981.

Keep in mind

What the map leaves out

  • No region works aloneReading this sentence uses the occipital lobe to see the letters, the temporal lobe to get the meaning and the frontal lobe to hold the thought.
  • You use all of itThe claim that we use only 10% of the brain is a myth. Over a day every region is active, and an organ that burns a fifth of your energy has no spare parts.
  • Almost everything comes in pairsThese pictures show one side. You have two of nearly every structure here, a left and a right. Some jobs lean to one side: language usually sits on the left.
The drawings are simplified diagrams, not anatomical scans. Figures such as weight and cell counts are averages for a healthy adult.
