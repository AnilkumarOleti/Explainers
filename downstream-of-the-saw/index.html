What follows a mass clearing, traced downhill

Downstream of the Saw

A forest looks like a pile of timber waiting to be used. It works like infrastructure: a roof, a sponge, a pump, an anchor, a vault and a home, all running for free. Clear it in bulk and none of those jobs stop being needed. They move downhill and downwind, onto people who never touched a tree.

  2. 2Water soaks into porous soil and seeps to the river for months.
  3. 3Trees breathe vapour back up, building clouds for rain further on.
  4. 4The river runs clear and steady. The town stays dry.
  5. 1Rain hits bare, sun-baked ground at full force.
  6. 2Water runs off the surface in hours, carrying topsoil with it.
  7. 3Less vapour rises. Clouds thin and less rain falls downwind.

The mental model

Six jobs a forest does for free

Every downstream effect on this page is one of these jobs going undone. Keep the six in mind and the rest follows.

Roof

Sponge

Follow the water

Seven stations downstream

The damage from a large clearing travels. Follow it the way the rain does: from the bare slope, into the streams and the sky, through the living web and the people who depend on it, and out to the coast.

1
On the slopefirst days → first decade

The ground heats up, then starts to leave

Without shade, bare soil takes the full sun. Cleared tropical land runs several degrees hotter than the forest floor by day, and it dries out fast. Then the first heavy rain arrives. Raindrops hit the ground at full speed, break soil clumps apart and seal the surface into a crust. Water that can't get in runs downhill and takes the loose soil with it.

  1. Shade gone
  2. Soil dries and crusts
  3. Rain runs off
  4. Topsoil washes away

In the tropics the loss is worse than it looks. Rainforest soils are old and leached. Most of the nutrients sit in the living plants. Clear and burn the trees and the ash gives the soil a short burst of fertility. After a few seasons of crops it is used up, and some soils bake into hard, infertile ground.

Landslide risk peaks later than people expect. Roots keep holding the slope for a while after the trees are cut, then rot. Root strength is usually lowest several years to a decade after clearing. In steep monsoon country like the Western Ghats and the Himalaya, slopes stripped of forest fail more often under heavy rain.

2
In the streamsfirst monsoon onward

Floods in the wet season, dry beds in the dry season

Forest soil releases water slowly. That's why forest streams keep flowing months after the rains stop. Clear the forest and the same rain leaves within hours. Flood peaks get higher and arrive faster. And because less water soaked into the ground, the springs that fed the streams fade when the dry season comes.

  1. Sponge gone
  2. Fast runoff
  3. Sharper floods
  4. Less groundwater
  5. Streams dry by summer

The soil from station 1 ends up here. Muddy water blocks light for water plants, clogs the gravel where fish lay eggs, and carries farm chemicals with it. Further down, the mud settles in reservoirs. It fills the storage that cities and farms rely on and shortens a dam's working life. Treatment plants pay more to clean the water.

Close to home. The Krishna and the Godavari, which supply much of Hyderabad's drinking water, both rise in the forested Western Ghats. What happens on those slopes turns up in reservoirs hundreds of kilometres away.

11,000 mm

Rough yearly rainfall at Cherrapunji, Meghalaya, one of the wettest places on Earth. It still runs short of drinking water in winter, because the stripped hills can't hold the rain.

3
In the skyyears → decades

The rain machine slows down

A large tropical tree can move hundreds of litres of water a day from the soil into the air. Across a whole forest, that vapour becomes a major source of rain. In the Amazon, a quarter to half of the rainfall comes from the forest itself. Moisture moves west across the basin, falling as rain and rising again several times. Winds carry the rest south as "flying rivers" that water farms in southern Brazil and northern Argentina.

  1. Fewer trees
  2. Less vapour
  3. Fewer clouds
  4. Less rain downwind
  5. Longer dry seasons

Past a certain point a forest can't make enough rain to keep itself alive. Thomas Lovejoy and Carlos Nobre estimated that point for the Amazon at 20–25% forest loss, combined with climate change. About 13–17% of the original Amazon is gone, depending on how it's counted. In the eastern third the loss is close to 31%. Beyond the threshold, large areas could turn into dry, degraded savanna.

4
In the atmosphereimmediately, and for centuries

Centuries of stored carbon, released in a few seasons

The world's forests hold roughly 860 billion tonnes of carbon, about as much as the whole atmosphere. Clearing releases it in three waves. Burning releases it at once. Stumps, roots and slash rot over the following years. Exposed soil warms and erodes, and loses its carbon over decades. Land-use change, mostly deforestation, causes around a tenth of human CO₂ emissions.

  1. Burn
  2. Decay
  3. Soil carbon lost
  4. Sink gone for good

It counts twice. A standing forest pulls CO₂ out of the air every year. A cleared one releases its store and stops absorbing. Where forests grow on peat, draining and burning release carbon that took thousands of years to build. In Indonesia's 2015 fire season, daily emissions on many days were higher than those of the entire US economy.

Where the maths differs. In snowy northern forests, clearing dark conifers exposes bright snow that reflects sunlight, which can cool the area locally. That doesn't make boreal clearing harmless, since the carbon, water and species are still lost. In the tropics both effects point the same way: more CO₂, and less cooling from evaporation.

of carbon stored in forests worldwide. About 44% is in the soil, which is why a cleared site keeps leaking long after the fire.

5
In the living webat once, then decades of delayed losses

Species don't just leave. They run out of room.

Mass clearing rarely wipes out a forest in one piece. It leaves islands. Edges are hotter, windier and drier. Fire, hunters, grazing cattle and invasive plants get in more easily. So each fragment keeps shrinking from its edges inward, long after the clearing stops.

  1. Fragments
  2. Edge effects
  3. Local extinctions
  4. Seed carriers lost
  5. Forest can't regrow itself

Small islands can't hold big predators, animals that range widely, or enough of any one species to stay genetically healthy. Many species hang on for decades and then disappear. Ecologists call this the extinction debt: the losses from a clearing keep arriving long after the saws have gone quiet.

The knock-on effects reach farms. Hornbills, primates and fruit bats carry the seeds of large forest trees, so without them the forest can't replant itself even if left alone. Birds and bats that eat insects keep crop pests down. Wild bees pollinate crops. In a Costa Rica study, coffee farms within a kilometre of forest got about 20% higher yields from wild-bee pollination.

6
In peoplefrom the first season

Heat, fever, smoke and lost livelihoods

Heat. Between 2001 and 2020, tropical forest loss warmed the affected areas by about 0.45 °C on its own, on top of global warming. That exposed 345 million people to extra heat and is linked to about 28,000 heat deaths a year. Outdoor workers and farmers carry most of it.

Disease. Clearing brings people, livestock and wildlife into new contact, and changes where disease carriers breed. In the Peruvian Amazon, the main malaria mosquito bit people 278 times more often in deforested areas than in intact forest. It breeds in sunlit pools, and clearings make plenty of those. Nipah virus first reached people in Malaysia in 1998–99, a spillover researchers tie to fruit bats that lost forest habitat and fed in orchards next to pig farms.

  1. New contact
  2. New breeding sites
  3. Spillover and outbreaks

Smoke. Burning to clear land fills whole regions with fine-particle haze. Southeast Asia's recurring haze seasons send people to hospital with lung and heart problems hundreds of kilometres from the fires.

Livelihoods. Around 1.6 billion people depend on forests for food, fuel, fodder, medicine or income. For many Indigenous and tribal communities the forest is also land, law and identity. They are often the first to be displaced and the last to share in the timber money.

7
At the coastyears → decades

The mud reaches the sea

Rivers carry the eroded soil to estuaries and the sea. The sediment clouds the water and smothers corals and seagrass, which need light to live. Fertiliser from the farms that replaced the forest feeds algae blooms, and when the algae die and rot they can strip oxygen from coastal water. In Madagascar, where most of the original forest is gone, rivers carry so much red soil that their plumes show up in satellite photos.

  1. Sediment and fertiliser
  2. Murky, nutrient-rich water
  3. Corals smothered, algae bloom
  4. Fisheries decline

Coastal forests matter on their own. Where mangroves are cleared for shrimp ponds or building, the coast loses its buffer against storm surges and waves. Fisheries lose the sheltered nurseries where many fish grow up.

Nursery

Mangrove roots shelter young fish, crabs and prawns that local fisheries depend on.

Why it speeds up

Two loops that feed themselves

The effects don't just add up. Some of them loop back and cause more clearing or more die-off, which is why forest loss tends to speed up once it starts.

The drying loop

Less forest makes less rain, and a drier forest burns and dies back on its own.

The worn-out-land loop

Cleared tropical soil wears out fast, so farming moves on and clears the next patch.

The healing clock

Days to cut, a century to come back

Tropical forests can regrow on their own if the land is left alone and there is intact forest nearby to send in seeds. Different parts come back on very different timescales.

The bill

Who pays for the missing jobs

When a forest stops doing a job, someone else has to do it, usually at a higher cost and usually far from where the trees stood.

JobWhat replaces itWho pays
RoofFans and air-conditioning, heat illness, heat-stressed cropsPeople living and working nearby
SpongeFlood defences, water tankers, deeper borewellsTowns and cities downstream
PumpIrrigation, failed rain-fed harvestsFarmers downwind, sometimes in another country
AnchorRetaining walls, landslide recovery, dredging silted reservoirsHill communities and water utilities
VaultCarbon removal, climate damageEveryone, for centuries
HomeNothing. An extinct species can't be bought back.Every generation after this one

The timber is sold once. The bill for the missing jobs keeps arriving, downstream, for decades.
