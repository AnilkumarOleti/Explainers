Charlie Munger · how he thought

Facts need something to hang on

Charlie Munger was Warren Buffett's partner at Berkshire Hathaway for 45 years. He did not credit his results to brilliance. He credited a frame he had built in his head from the big ideas of every major discipline, and a habit of hanging everything he learned on it.

He called the frame a latticework of mental models. The rest of his philosophy is instructions for building it and for keeping your own mind from wrecking it.

Start at the problem it solves. The lattice comes second.

The problem

A person with one tool sees one kind of problem

Munger's favourite warning was an old proverb: to someone who owns only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail.

Most capable people are trained in one discipline. They get good at it, get paid for it, and start seeing its pattern everywhere. Each of their answers is partly right, which is why the habit is so hard to notice from the inside.

Try it. A neighbourhood bakery's sales have fallen 20% in three months. There are six clues. Ask an expert.

The idea

Learn the big ideas from every field, and keep them joined up

Munger's fix was to carry the whole toolbox. Learn the few central ideas of each major discipline, well enough to use them without looking anything up. He guessed that 80 or 90 models do about 90% of the work, and that a handful do most of that.

The joining matters as much as the collecting. A fact you memorise on its own fades. A fact that attaches to a model you already own stays put and turns up when you need it. That is the lattice: models are the slats, and experience hangs on them.

He set two conditions. The models must come from many disciplines, because real problems ignore the way universities divide knowledge. And you have to use them routinely, or they go.

Habit one

Turn the question upside down

The mathematician Carl Jacobi told his students to invert hard problems. Munger took the advice out of algebra and applied it to everything.

Ask how to succeed and you get slogans. Ask what would guarantee failure and you get a list specific enough to act on. Then you avoid the list.

Munger, paraphrasedAll I want to know is where I'm going to die, so that I never go there.

He once gave a graduating class a speech made entirely of prescriptions for a miserable life: be unreliable, learn only from your own experience, stay down when you are knocked down. The students were meant to do the opposite.

Behind the joke is his most practical claim. Over a long stretch, steadily avoiding stupid mistakes pays better than chasing brilliant moves.

Habit two

Know exactly where your knowledge stops

Everyone has a patch of the world they really understand. Munger and Buffett called it the circle of competence. Its size hardly matters. Knowing where its edge runs matters a great deal.

The damage is done in the band between what you know and what you believe you know. Inside the circle you have an edge. Far outside it you are cautious. In the band you feel like an expert and are not one.

His test for whether an opinion was inside the circle: he should be able to state the case against it better than the people who hold the opposite view.

The enemy inside

Your own mind is the largest source of error

Models describe how the world works. They are no help if your brain misreads the situation before you reach for them. So Munger built a second list: 25 standard ways human judgment goes wrong. He ran through it the way a pilot runs a pre-flight checklist.

Incentives come first on the list. His favourite case was a parcel company whose night shift kept finishing late. Appeals and threats did nothing. Then someone noticed the crew was paid by the hour. Pay them by the shift and let them leave when the planes were loaded, and the delays vanished.

From this he took a two-pass routine for any decision. First pass: what are the real factors, weighed rationally? Second pass: which of these 25 pulls is working on me, or on the person advising me?

When forces stack

Extreme outcomes come from several forces pushing one way

Number 25 is different from the other 24. It says the tendencies do not politely add up. When three or four point in the same direction they feed each other, and the result is out of all proportion. Munger borrowed a word for it: a lollapalooza.

His standard example was an open auction, a setting that could have been designed to make people overpay. Switch the forces on and compare simple addition with what stacking does.

The effect runs both ways. The best businesses and the best habits are lollapaloozas too: several advantages reinforcing each other. Munger looked for those on purpose, and looked just as hard for the stacked pressures working on his own judgment.

What it looks like

In practice, it is mostly waiting

Put the pieces together and the behaviour that results is unusual. If you stay inside your circle, demand a margin of safety and refuse anything you cannot explain, very few ideas pass. So you do almost nothing for long stretches, and act in size when something clears every test.

In investing

  • Pay for quality. In 1972 he pushed Buffett to buy See's Candies for $25 million, a full price by their old standards. It turned Berkshire from buying mediocre businesses cheaply to buying excellent ones at fair prices.
  • Make few bets. Good ideas are rare. When one arrives, a small position wastes it.
  • Leave compounding alone. Selling, switching and paying tax each time all interrupt it.
  • Keep a margin of safety. Buy only when you would still be fine if your estimate turned out badly wrong.

In living

  • Deserve what you want. The reliable way to get a good job, partner or reputation is to be the person who merits it.
  • Be reliable. Do what you said you would. He ranked it above talent.
  • Go to bed a little wiser. He read for hours every day and treated learning as a duty that never finishes.
  • Drop envy, resentment and self-pity. He called them the sure routes to misery, and noted that envy is the one sin with no pleasure in it.
  • Kill a favourite idea. A year in which you did not discard one of your best-loved beliefs was, to him, a wasted year.

Where it creaks

The method has limits, and he showed some of them himself

Naming a model is different from owning it. A list of eighty labels gives a feeling of mastery without the substance. Munger's own models came from decades of reading the source material.

Knowing the biases does not switch them off. He said plainly that he still fell for them. The checklist lowers the error rate. It does not make anyone immune.

The circle's edge is hard to see from inside. Late in life he bought Alibaba shares and afterwards called it a mistake. His design for a mostly windowless university dormitory was widely criticised and never built.
