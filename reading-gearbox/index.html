Where the speed limit lives

The limit is in your head, not your eyes.

≈ 270

Eyes and ears feed one language system, and it has one top speed.

Speed up recorded speech and it stays clear to about 270 words a minute, then understanding falls steeply by 315. People read the same texts silently at about 269. Language skill, not eye speed, sets the pace.

The voice in your head

Your inner voice is a tool. Keep it.

Speed-reading courses call it a bad habit. The research says it helps you hold a sentence together.

Screens widen the leak

What actually makes you faster

Knowing more makes you read faster.

Vocabulary and background knowledge are the engine. Grow them and every gear gets faster.

Playbook

Ten habits for faster, deeper reading

Before you read

Pick the gear

Ask what you need: a fact, the gist, full understanding, or something to keep.

Map it first

Spend two minutes on the title, headings, first lines, figures and ending.

Put hard text on paper

Print it or use a distraction-free reader, and give it unhurried time.

While you read

Let the inner voice talk

Don’t try to silence it. It speeds up on easy text by itself.

Reverse on purpose

Go back the moment a sentence stops making sense, and fix it right then.

Catch the drift

When you notice your mind has left, go back to the last line you remember.

After you read

Close it and recall

Write the main point, the evidence and why it matters from memory. Then check.

Space it, skip the highlighter

Recall again a few days later. Re-reading and highlighting teach little.

The long game

Grow the engine

Read widely, and read an overview before a hard book. Knowledge speeds up every gear.

How bees give directions

A honeybee that finds a rich patch of flowers has a problem. It cannot point, and the hive is dark. Yet soon after it returns, dozens of its sisters arrive at the same flowers, sometimes kilometres away.

The answer is a dance. On the vertical wall of the comb, the scout runs a short straight line while shaking its body from side to side. Then it loops back and repeats the run, again and again. Researchers call this the waggle dance.

The straight run carries two pieces of information. Its angle gives the direction. If the bee runs straight up the comb, the food lies toward the sun. If it runs at an angle to the left of straight up, the flowers lie that many degrees to the left of the sun. Inside the hive, straight up stands in for the sun.

The length of the waggle gives the distance. A longer waggle run means a longer flight. Followers crowd close and take in the dance through touch and vibration, since there is too little light to watch it.

The Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch spent decades decoding this language, using marked bees and dishes of sugar water set at known distances. The work earned him a share of the Nobel Prize in 1973.

The dance is not perfect. Each run points in a slightly different direction, so recruits spread out around the target. Some researchers think this scatter helps, because a cloud of searching bees is more likely to cover a whole patch than a single tight line.
