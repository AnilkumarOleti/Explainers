Federer and Nadal 40 meetings · 2004 to 2019 · 42 Grand Slam titles between them

Federer took away your time. Nadal took away your space.

They played on the same courts and seemed to play different sports. Roger Federer stood on the baseline, hit the ball early and made opponents rush. Rafael Nadal stood well behind it, hit with more spin than anyone had measured before and made opponents retreat. This page explains how each game worked, where it came from, and what happened when the two met.

Two ways to make a court feel small

Federer: time

Federer’s game was built on taking the ball early. He stood on or inside the baseline, met the ball while it was still rising and sent it back flat. The ball spent less time in the air and came back before the opponent had recovered.

Points were short. A serve to a corner, a forehand to the other side, and it was over.

Nadal: space

Nadal’s game was built on weight. He stood two or three metres behind the baseline, swung up the back of the ball and sent it high over the net with heavy topspin. The ball dipped late, landed deep and jumped above the shoulder.

Opponents were pushed back and wide, hitting from places with no angle. Points were long, and each one cost the other player something.

Everything else follows from that difference: how they were coached, how they hit each shot, how they thought about a match, and why each owned a different surface.

How they were made

Neither style was an accident. Each came out of a particular childhood and a particular coach.

Federer

Basel: talent first, discipline later

Federer grew up in Münchenstein, a suburb of Basel, in a middle-class home. His father Robert is Swiss and his mother Lynette is South African, and both worked for the pharmaceutical company Ciba-Geigy. He played football and tennis until about twelve, worked as a ball boy at the Swiss Indoors in Basel, and had parents who supported him without pushing.

As a boy he was known for his temper as much as his talent. He threw rackets, shouted and cried after defeats. At fourteen he left home for the national training centre at Ecublens, in French-speaking Switzerland, where he barely spoke the language and was homesick for months.

The Australian coach Peter Carter shaped his technique at the Old Boys club in Basel, including the one-handed backhand and the all-court game. Carter died in a car accident in August 2002, when Federer was twenty. Federer has said the loss made him determined not to waste his ability. He won Wimbledon eleven months later. The calm that people later called natural was something he built in his early twenties.

Nadal

Manacor: discipline first, then everything else

Nadal grew up in Manacor, on Mallorca, in a family that shared one apartment building: grandparents, parents, uncles and aunts on different floors. His father Sebastián ran a glass business. One uncle, Miguel Ángel Nadal, played football for Barcelona and Spain, so elite sport was ordinary in the house and nobody was impressed by it.

Another uncle, Toni, put a racket in his hand when he was three or four and coached him until 2017. Toni’s method was hard on purpose. Sessions ran long, on poor courts with worn balls, and excuses were not allowed. Throwing a racket was out of the question, and Nadal went through his professional career without smashing one. In his autobiography he recalls winning a national under-12 title and Toni reading him the list of past champions, most of whom had never been heard of again.

Nadal is right-handed away from the court. As a small boy he hit with two hands on both sides, and when the time came to choose he settled on a left-handed forehand. At fourteen the Spanish federation offered to move him to Barcelona. The family said no, and he stayed at home with the same coach, the same friends and the same courts.

Federer learned control after he had the shots. Nadal was taught endurance before he had them. One left home at fourteen and worked with several coaches. The other never left and worked with his uncle for more than 25 years.

Spin is what let Nadal swing at full speed and still clear the net by a wide margin. The ball climbs, dips sharply into the court and jumps after the bounce. Federer’s lower figure was a choice as well. A flatter ball travels through the court faster, which was the point of his game.

Where the styles met

Nadal leads the head-to-head 24 to 16. Most of that margin comes from one pattern, repeated for a decade, and from the surface it was played on.

Federer made three changes late in his career. He moved to a bigger racket head in 2014, which gave him more margin on the backhand. He hired Ivan Ljubičić for 2016, who pressed him to drive the backhand instead of slicing it. And he came back from six months away in 2017 fresh enough to play that way for five sets. He beat Nadal in the Australian Open final from 3–1 down in the fifth, and won six of their last seven meetings.

Two kinds of stubbornness

Both were relentless. They showed it in different ways.

Federer

Every week, for years

Federer’s persistence shows up as streaks. He reached 23 Grand Slam semi-finals in a row and 36 quarter-finals in a row. He was No. 1 for 237 consecutive weeks. He played 65 majors without missing one.

He played 1,526 singles matches and never retired from one. Efficient movement and short points kept him healthy into his mid-thirties.

When his knee did fail in 2016 he took six months away, then returned at 35 to win three of the next five majors. He was No. 1 again at 36, at the time the oldest man to hold the ranking.

Nadal

Every point, whatever the score

Nadal played the first point of a match and a break point in the fifth set with the same intensity. He chased balls that were already past him, and opponents learned that a point against him was not over until the ball had bounced twice.

His body paid for it. He had chronic pain in his left foot from 2005, repeated knee injuries, wrist problems in 2014 and 2016 and a hip injury in 2023. After each long absence he came back and won majors: two in 2013 after seven months out, two in 2017 and two in 2022.

At the 2022 Australian Open he was 35, months removed from doubting whether his foot would let him play again, and two sets down in the final. He won in five sets after 5 hours 24 minutes.

What each believed about the game

Federer

  • Take the initiative.The player who decides where the ball goes is the one running less. Serve to a spot, hit the next ball to the other side, come forward.
  • Never the same ball twice.Slice, drive, drop shot, a change of height or pace. Variety kept opponents from finding a rhythm.
  • Protect the body.Short points and economical footwork were a long-term plan. He played his last match at 41.
  • Let the point go.A lost point is finished the moment it ends. The next one is the only one that can be played.

Nadal

  • Margin first.Hit high over the net with heavy spin and aim well inside the lines. If the ball keeps landing in with that much weight on it, the error comes from the other side.
  • Every point is the same point.The score does not change the effort. He prepared for a first round the way he prepared for a final and spoke of every opponent as a threat.
  • Expect it to be hard.Toni taught him that difficulty is normal and complaining is useless. Nadal has said doubt helps him because it keeps him working.
  • Fix the problem in front of you.Written off early as a clay specialist, he flattened his shots to win Wimbledon, rebuilt his serve to win the US Open, and in his thirties stood closer to the baseline to shorten points.

Federer gave these figures for his own career in a 2024 commencement speech at Dartmouth, where he also told graduates that “effortless is a myth”. The best player of his era lost nearly half the points he played. Winning came from how he handled the ones he lost.

They had more in common than the contrast suggests. Both kept changing their games into their mid-thirties. Both refused to blame injuries, schedules or luck in public. And each has described the other as the reason he improved.

How well they played

On the largest numbers they are close to level. Nadal has two more majors and the head-to-head. Federer has more titles, 101 more weeks at No. 1 and six season-ending championships to Nadal’s none.

What each one leaves

Federer showed that the game can be played with economy: early, varied, moving forward, and for a very long time. Nadal showed how far effort and one overwhelming shot can go when they are applied to every point for twenty years.

They also made each other. Nadal had to flatten his forehand and sharpen his serve to beat Federer on grass. Federer had to rebuild his backhand at 35 to beat Nadal on a hard court. In September 2022 Federer chose to play his last match as Nadal’s doubles partner at the Laver Cup in London. They lost, and sat side by side in tears afterwards.
