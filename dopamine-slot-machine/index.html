The reels

A reward you can’t predict is the hardest one to quit

In B. F. Skinner’s labs in the 1950s, animals rewarded on unpredictable schedules responded fastest and kept going longest after the rewards stopped. Be the pigeon.

Each line climbs one step per response; ticks mark rewards; steeper means faster. Idealised shapes after Ferster & Skinner (1957).

The lever in your thumb

Loren Brichter invented pull-to-refresh in 2009 for an app called Tweetie. He later told the Guardian: “Pull-to-refresh is addictive.”

The near miss

Players rate near misses as worse than plain losses, yet they raise the urge to play, and they light up the same striatum and insula circuits as wins (Clark et al., 2009). Feed version: the teaser, the cliffhanger, the blurred preview.

Lights for nothing

Slot machines celebrate “wins” smaller than the bet. Novices’ sweat response to these matched real wins (Dixon et al., 2010). Feed version: the ping that turns out to be an ad.

The spark

Dopamine isn’t pleasure. It’s “better than expected”

In the 1990s Wolfram Schultz recorded single dopamine neurons in monkeys. They fired for surprise rewards, not for rewards they saw coming. Run his experiment, with a buzz for the cue and a like for the juice.

A teaching model of Schultz’s recordings: a burst for “better than expected”, a dip for “worse”. Rows are trials; the newest is at the bottom.

Now make it a maybe

When a buzz only sometimes brings a like, the neurons hum between the buzz and the outcome. The hum is biggest at 50/50 (Fiorillo, Tobler & Schultz, 2003). What it means is still argued. A notification is a buzz that means maybe.

    Wanting vs liking

    You can keep wanting what you’ve stopped liking

    Kent Berridge’s lab showed that wanting and liking run on different wiring. Dopamine drives the want. The liking hotspots are tiny.

    Your enjoyment, post by post

    Almost no dopamine

    Rats with up to 99% of their dopamine depleted still make “liking” faces at sugar. They just won’t work to get it (Berridge & Robinson, 1998).

    Extra dopamine

    Mice with 70% more dopamine run harder for a sweet reward, but don’t like it any more (Peciña et al., 2003).

    Tiny pleasure, big want

    Each liking hotspot is about a cubic millimetre in rats, perhaps a cubic centimetre in you. The wanting system is far larger (Berridge lab).

    What’s proven, what’s argued, what’s inferred

    The mechanisms on this page are well established. How much harm they do, and to whom, is still argued. Here is where each claim stands.

    Solid

    • Unpredictable rewards make behaviour fast and stubborn. Ferster & Skinner, 1957
    • Dopamine neurons burst for better than expected and dip for worse. Schultz, Dayan & Montague, 1997
    • Near misses and celebrated losses act like wins on slot players. Clark et al., 2009; Dixon et al., 2010
    • Wanting and liking can come apart. Berridge & Robinson, 1998
    • A short pause with an easy way out cuts app opening: 57% fewer opens over six weeks. Grüning et al., 2023

    Argued

    • Whether heavy use is “addiction”. The WHO recognises gaming disorder, but no diagnostic manual recognises a social media disorder, and Instagram’s head has testified that he separates “problematic use” from clinical addiction.
    • How much harm. Across three big surveys of adolescents (n = 355,358), technology use explained at most 0.4% of the differences in well-being. Orben & Przybylski, 2019
    • Yet cutting back helps some people: four weeks off Facebook raised well-being, and a 30-minute daily cap cut loneliness and depression over three weeks. Allcott et al., 2020; Hunt et al., 2018

    Inferred

    • Nobody has recorded your dopamine neurons while you scroll. The phone story is assembled from animal experiments, gambling studies and brain scans like Sherman’s.
    • The simulations here are teaching models, not measurements.
    • Courts and regulators are now judging design itself, not just content. Those are legal findings, not scientific ones.

      Friction works

      In a six-week field study of 280 people, the one sec app’s pause cut app opening by 57%. Its strongest ingredient was an easy way to back out; the delay helped too; a reflective message on its own did not (Grüning et al., PNAS, 2023).
