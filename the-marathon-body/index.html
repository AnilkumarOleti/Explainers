Start line

You start with two fuel tanks

Sugar is a small can with a wide pipe. It gives fast energy, then runs out. Fat is a huge tank with a thin pipe. It almost never runs out, but it flows slowly. The faster you run, the more you drink from the can.

1,550 kcalusable sugar, stored in your leg muscles and liver
≈27 marathonsof energy stored as fat, even in a lean runner
KM0.3

The first minutes

The start always feels hardest

Your legs ask for energy at once. Heart and lungs need a minute or two to catch up. Until they do, muscles borrow from quick stores and burn sugar without oxygen. Then the run settles.

KM1

The pump

Your heart quadruples the flow

Blood carries the oxygen and fuel. Almost all the extra flow goes to working muscles and to the skin, which sheds heat.

70 → 160+heartbeats a minute, rest to hard running

KM3

The air

You breathe ten times more air

Each dot is one litre of air a minute. Your runner needs about 62 litres a minute at race pace.

KM5

Every stride

Every step is a landing

Each footfall pushes 2 to 3 times your body weight up through one leg. Tendons stretch like springs as you land and recoil as you push off, handing back some energy for free.

≈175 kgthrough one leg at each landing, for a 70 kg runner
≈165steps a minute for a typical recreational runner
22,100landings per leg in one marathon
KM10

The heat

Running is mostly heat

Muscles turn only a quarter of their fuel into motion. The rest becomes heat. Sweat is your radiator. Every litre that evaporates carries off about 580 kcal of heat.

20–25%of fuel actually moves you
≈1.3 Lsweat an hour, the average for endurance athletes
≈580 kcalof heat carried off by each litre of sweat that evaporates
KM15

The brain

Your brain joins the run

Running raises endocannabinoids, the body's own cannabis-like chemicals. They are the leading explanation for the runner's high: better mood, less anxiety. The brain also rations effort and keeps a reserve, which is why tired runners can often still sprint the last stretch.

KM30

The wall

When the sugar can runs dry

Only the thin fat pipe is left, and fat can't flow fast enough for race pace. Your body forces you down to a shuffle and your legs go heavy. Runners call it hitting the wall.

Try to avoid it · 70 kg, 4:30 target

Pacing

Sugar runs out at km 33.5. Pace drops by about 40%, and the last 8.7 km cost 22 extra minutes.

The legs pay in tiny tears

On every landing your thigh and calf muscles brake while being stretched. Tens of thousands of these braking contractions tear tiny muscle fibres.

What running does to you

Your body rebuilds itself

Each run is a small stress. Recovery rebuilds a little stronger. Months of it add up to a different engine.

The fine print

What about knees and injuries?

Recreational runners get less hip and knee arthritis than people who don't run.

Injuries are the real cost. Between 19% and 79% of runners get hurt, depending on the study, and the knee is the most common spot. Big weekly mileage and old injuries are the clearest warning signs. The life-extending payoff levels off at around 4.5 hours of running a week.

The whole run in three lines

  • Running turns food and oxygen into motion, and mostly into heat.
  • A marathon is long enough to drain the sugar can.
  • Training builds a bigger engine. Pacing and fuel get you home.
Sources, and how the numbers on this page are worked out

The model behind the bib

  • Energy: about 1 kcal per kg of body weight per km, at any pace (≈2,950 kcal for 70 kg).
  • Usable sugar: leg muscle is ≈21% of body weight and holds ≈80 kcal per kg, plus ≈350 kcal in the liver. Carb-loaded: ≈120 kcal per kg plus ≈500 kcal.
  • Share of energy drawn from sugar: 66% at even pace, 76% when starting too fast. These two figures are assumptions.
  • Gels: ≈60 g of carbs an hour, about 240 kcal.
  • After the wall: pace about 40% slower, as in the large race study below.
  • Steps: cadence ≈145 + 2 × speed in km/h, which gives ≈165 a minute at 4:30 pace.
  • Average heart rate is assumed: 172, 160, 152 and 140 beats a minute for the four finish times; 70 on the sofa.
  • Sweat ≈1.25 ml per kcal on a mild day. Oxygen: ≈4.9 kcal per litre. Air: ≈25 litres per litre of oxygen. Heart output: 5 litres a minute plus 5 per litre of oxygen used.
  • Fat tank: 15% body fat at 7,700 kcal per kg. One banana: 105 kcal.
  • Bodies vary widely. Treat every number here as a rough average.
