How an LLM works

The title, cut the way a model reads it: five tokens, each with an ID number.

A large language model is a forecaster. It reads the text so far, gives every possible next token a probability, draws one, adds it to the text and goes again.

Below, the whole machine is opened up: how text becomes numbers, how those numbers talk to each other, and how training set the billions of dials that make the forecast good.

It does one thing: forecast the next token

Answering, coding, translating, summarising: all of it is one move, repeated. Look at the text so far, and give every token in the vocabulary a probability of coming next.

The job sounds shallow. It isn't. To forecast well across books, code, forums and maths, a model has to absorb grammar, facts, arithmetic, programming and reasoning, because each of those decides what comes next in real text. Pick a text and see what it takes.

Your phone keyboard does a tiny version of this with three suggestions. An LLM does it over its whole vocabulary, after reading everything before the cursor.

Under the hood

The model computes one conditional distribution:

P( xₜ | x₁, x₂, …, xₜ₋₁ )

Its raw output is one score per vocabulary token, called a logit: 50,257 of them for GPT-2, 128,256 for Llama 3. A softmax turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 1. Generating text means sampling a token from that distribution, appending it, and running the model again on the longer text.

First, the text is cut into tokens

A model can't take in letters or words directly. A tokenizer cuts text into chunks it has seen often, and every chunk has an ID number. Common words are one token, usually with their leading space attached. Rare words are built from pieces.

In English a token averages about three-quarters of a word. Text the tokenizer saw less of, like the Hindi here, is cut much finer and costs more tokens for the same meaning.

This is why models fumble “how many r's in strawberry?”. They see str aw berry: three ID numbers, never the letters.

Under the hood: how the pieces are chosen

Most tokenizers use byte-pair encoding. Start from single characters (or the 256 possible bytes), count every adjacent pair across a huge corpus, merge the most frequent pair into a new token, and repeat until the vocabulary reaches its target size. Step through it on a four-word corpus:

Each token becomes a point on a map of meaning

The ID picks a row from a giant table: a list of a few thousand numbers called an embedding. Read it as coordinates. Training has arranged the table so that tokens used in similar ways sit close together.

Neighbours on the map behave alike in text: animals with animals, capitals with capitals, feelings with feelings. That is why a model copes with sentences it has never seen: “the kitten slept” lands near “the puppy slept”.

Directions mean something too. The step from man to king is roughly the step from woman to queen, and the step from a country to its capital is about the same everywhere. Concepts become directions in this space.

Under the hood

The embedding matrix has one row per token and one column per dimension: 128,256 × 4,096 ≈ 525 million numbers in Llama 3 8B. Token ID i simply selects row i. Nobody designs the rows: they start random, and training moves them until the useful structure appears.

cos θ = a·b / ( |a| |b| )

The vector arithmetic was first shown clearly with word2vec in 2013. In an LLM these input vectors are only the starting point; the layers above rewrite them using context.

Each token is stamped with its position

“Dog bit man” and “man bit dog” contain the same tokens. Without some sense of order, the model would see the same bag of words.

Modern models turn parts of each token's vector like the hands of a clock, by an angle that grows with its position. Some hands spin fast and track nearby word order; slow ones track long distances.

When two tokens are compared, what matters is the angle between their hands. Slide the sentence further down the document: every hand turns, but the angle between any two tokens stays put. The model senses how far apart words are, wherever they sit.

Under the hood

This is rotary position embedding (RoPE). The query and key vectors are split into pairs of numbers; pair i at position m is rotated by the angle m·θᵢ, with

θᵢ = base^( −2i / d )

Because rotations compose, the score between positions m and n depends only on m − n. The base is 10,000 in the original paper; Llama 3 raised it to 500,000 to reach longer contexts. GPT-2 used a simpler scheme: a learned vector for each of 1,024 positions, added to the embedding.

Attention: every token looks back for what it needs

Now the tokens talk. In every layer, each token asks a question of all the tokens before it and pulls in a blend of their information, weighted by how well each one answers.

Switch the last word between tired and wide. At it, the model can't know yet what “it” means, so attention is split between the animal and the street. By the last word it has settled: tired things are animals, wide things are streets.

One question isn't enough, so each layer runs many attention heads side by side. One tracks the previous word, one finds who did what, one rests on the first token when there's nothing worth fetching.

Under the hood

A token can only look backwards: the empty triangle in the grid. That rule, the causal mask, lets training check the forecast at every position of a document at once without the model peeking at the answer.

GPT-3 runs 96 heads in each of its 96 layers. Interpretability research has named some of them: previous-token heads, induction heads that spot a repeated pattern and continue it, and attention sinks that park on the first token.

The maths of a glance: queries, keys and values

Each token makes three small vectors from its own. A query: what am I looking for? A key: what do I offer? A value: what I hand over if chosen.

The query is compared with every earlier key by multiplying them number by number and adding up: a dot product. A high score means a good match. A softmax turns the scores into weights that sum to 1, and the token receives the weighted mix of everyone's values.

Drag the slider to change what the query wants. Every number follows.

Under the hood

Q = XW_Q K = XW_K V = XW_V
A = softmax( QKᵀ / √dₖ + M )
output = A · V

The W matrices are learned. M adds −∞ to future positions, which the softmax turns into zero weight. Dividing by √dₖ stops scores from growing with the vector length and saturating the softmax.

Llama 3 8B splits its 4,096 dimensions into 32 heads of 128, runs them in parallel, then concatenates the results and mixes them with a fourth matrix, W_O. Every token is compared with every earlier token, so cost grows with the square of the context length. To save memory, several query heads share one key/value head (8 shared heads in Llama 3), a trick called grouped-query attention.

Then each token thinks alone: the MLP is where knowledge lives

Attention moves information between tokens. Then every token's vector passes, on its own, through a wide two-layer network: the MLP.

Think of it as thousands of detectors. Each fires when the vector matches a pattern it learned (“this is about the Eiffel Tower”) and, when it fires, adds its own nudge to the vector (“Paris”, “France”). Much of what a model knows is stored here, and it holds most of the weights.

Switch to mixture of experts to see how many newer models save effort: a router sends each token to only a few blocks of detectors.

Under the hood

h = SiLU(W_gate x) ⊙ W_up x
MLP(x) = W_down · h

That is the SwiGLU form used by Llama: 4,096 numbers widen to 14,336 detectors, then shrink back to 4,096. Facts such as “Eiffel Tower → Paris” have been located in mid-layer MLPs and even edited there (the ROME method, 2022).

Real neurons are messier than these labels: a model packs in more concepts than it has neurons by overlapping them, which is called superposition. Mixture-of-experts models store many MLPs and use a few per token: DeepSeek-V3 holds 671B weights but uses 37B for each token.

Stack the layers, and meaning sharpens on the way up

One layer is attention, then the MLP. A model stacks dozens of them: 32 in Llama 3 8B, 126 in its 405-billion-weight sibling.

A layer doesn't replace a token's vector. It adds edits to it. Picture a running draft of “what this token means here”, revised by every layer in turn. That draft is called the residual stream.

Follow bank in two sentences. At the bottom both drafts are the same row of the embedding table. Layer by layer, the river and the money pull them apart.

Under the hood

x ← x + Attention( Norm(x) )
x ← x + MLP( Norm(x) )

Those plus signs are the residual connections. They keep the signal and the training gradients flowing through 100+ layers. Norm is usually RMSNorm, which rescales each vector to a steady size.

Probing studies find a rough division of labour: early layers handle spelling and local grammar, middle layers meaning and facts, late layers what to say next. Only the last token's final vector is used for the forecast.

At the top, the last vector becomes the forecast

After the last layer, only the final token's vector is used. It's compared with every token in the vocabulary, one dot product each, giving a score per token: about 128,000 scores.

A softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and one token is drawn at random by those odds, like throwing a weighted die.

Temperature reshapes the die before the throw: near 0 the favourite always wins; above 1 the odds flatten and odd choices creep in. Top-p throws away the long tail of unlikely tokens.

Under the hood

z = W_U · h
pᵢ = exp(zᵢ / T) / Σⱼ exp(zⱼ / T)

W_U is the unembedding matrix, 128,256 × 4,096 in Llama 3 8B; small models often reuse the embedding table here. T → 0 is greedy decoding. Top-p (nucleus) sampling keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities reach p, renormalises them, and samples from that set. The dice are why the same prompt can give different answers.

Then it loops, one full trip per token, with a cache

Your prompt is read in one parallel sweep. After that the answer comes one token at a time, and every new token needs a full trip through all the layers.

The keys and values a layer computes for a token never change, so they are saved: the KV cache. A new token computes only its own, and reads everyone else's from the cache. Switch the cache off to see the work pile up when every step starts from scratch.

That's also why a long prompt takes a moment before the first word appears (the parallel read, called prefill), and why words then stream out one by one (decode).

Under the hood

Decode speed is set by memory, not maths: each new token needs every weight read once. Llama 3 8B at 16-bit is 16 GB; an H100 reads about 3.35 TB/s, so one conversation tops out near 200 tokens a second. Serving many conversations at once shares each read.

KV per token = 2 × 32 layers
× 8 heads × 128 numbers
× 2 bytes = 128 KB

So a 128,000-token context needs about 16 GB of cache for this model, as much as the weights themselves. Long contexts are expensive in memory as well as compute.

Where the numbers come from: trillions of guesses, each one graded

A new model's weights are random numbers, so its forecasts are noise. Training feeds it real text and, at every position, compares the forecast with the token that actually came next.

The less probability it gave the right token, the bigger its surprise. After each batch, every weight is nudged a tiny amount in the direction that would have made the surprise smaller.

Slide training forward. Surprise drops first on easy tokens like “on the” and lingers on the genuinely unpredictable ones.

Under the hood

L = −(1/N) Σₜ log₂ pₜ
pₜ = p( xₜ | x₁ … xₜ₋₁ )

This is cross-entropy loss: average surprise, here in bits. Guessing uniformly over 128,000 tokens scores log₂ 128,000 ≈ 17 bits. Good models reach roughly 2 to 3 bits per token on ordinary web text.

Every position is a lesson, so one 2,000-token document gives 2,000 graded guesses. Llama 3 was trained on about 15 trillion tokens.

Learning is rolling downhill on a map of error

Picture every possible setting of the weights as a place on a map, with the height of the ground equal to the loss. Training starts somewhere random and keeps stepping downhill.

Backpropagation works out, for every weight at once, which way is downhill: the gradient. The learning rate sets how big a step to take. Too small and training crawls; too big and it overshoots the valley, bouncing from wall to wall or flying off.

Real training repeats this step millions of times, each time on a fresh batch of text, with billions of weights moving together.

Under the hood

w ← w − η · ∇L(w)

Backpropagation is the chain rule applied layer by layer, backwards; it costs about twice the forward pass. Real runs use the AdamW optimiser (momentum plus a step size per weight), batches of millions of tokens, and a learning rate that warms up then decays.

compute ≈ 6 × weights × tokens

Llama 3.1 405B: 6 × 405B × 15.6T ≈ 3.8 × 10²⁵ operations, run on up to 16,000 H100 GPUs.

A trained model only continues documents; fine-tuning makes it an assistant

Pre-training produces a model that continues any text the way the internet would. Ask it a question and it may reply with more questions, because quiz pages look like that.

Two more stages make an assistant. Instruction tuning trains it on thousands of example conversations. Feedback training lets it write several answers, scores them, and makes the better ones more likely.

Underneath, a chat is still one document. The app wraps each message in special tokens, and the model writes the assistant's part until it forecasts the end-of-turn token.

Under the hood

Instruction tuning uses the same next-token loss, applied only to the assistant's replies. RLHF (InstructGPT, 2022): people rank answers, a reward model learns their taste, and reinforcement learning raises the probability of high-reward answers. DPO gets a similar result straight from preference pairs. Where answers can be checked, as in maths and code, rewards are automatic; training on long worked solutions produced reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 (2025).

The demo runs the exact policy-gradient step for a choice among four answers:

θᵢ ← θᵢ + η · pᵢ · ( rᵢ − r̄ )

Real chat markers differ by model; Llama 3 writes <|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|> and ends each turn with <|eot_id|>.

Where the billions of weights live

Every weight is one learned number. Count them: the embedding table, then in every layer the attention matrices and the much bigger MLP. Pick a published model, or drag the sliders to build your own.

Two numbers set most of the size: how many numbers each token's vector holds, and how many layers there are. Weights grow with the square of the vector size, so doubling it quadruples each layer.

Bigger models trained on more data have improved along strikingly smooth curves, the scaling laws. That predictability is why labs kept building bigger.

Under the hood

attention: 2d² + 2d·d_kv
MLP: 3·d·d_ff

Those are per layer; d is the vector size. Generating one token costs about 2 operations per weight. At 16-bit each weight takes 2 bytes; 4-bit quantisation stores it in about half a byte with a small loss of quality. The Chinchilla study (2022) found compute is best spent on roughly 20 training tokens per weight; recent models train far longer than that, because a smaller model is cheaper to run. The largest labs no longer publish their sizes, so the open models here are the best documented.
