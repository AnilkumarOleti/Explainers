Range

range (n.) 1. a line of mountains. 2. how widely you have wandered before you climb.

Two famous athletes. Same summit. Very different trails. Press play.

Legend: Tiger   Roger

Tiger Woods · golf

  1. Before he can walk: a putter in his hands
  2. Age 2: putting on national TV
  3. Childhood: drilled on golf, and golf only
  4. Age 21: best golfer in the world

Roger Federer · tennis

  1. Skiing, wrestling, swimming
  2. Skateboarding, squash, basketball
  3. Handball, table tennis, badminton, football
  4. Mum is a tennis coach. She declines to coach him.
  5. Teens: finally narrows to tennis
  6. One of the greatest players ever

Tiger's story is the one we tell kids. Roger's is the one that usually happens.

When researchers study elite athletes, most had a sampling period: years of many sports and light structure, then a late focus. The rest of the book asks where else that pattern holds.

Waypoint 02 · the master key

Two kinds of terrain

Whether early focus wins depends on the world you are learning in.

Kind worlds reward early, narrow drilling. That is also exactly where computers now beat us. Most of real life is wicked.

1900s

The tongue doctor

A New York doctor became famous for spotting typhoid early by feeling patients' tongues with his bare hands. His predictions kept coming true. He was right because his hands were carrying the disease from patient to patient.

That is wicked feedback: each "success" taught him the wrong lesson more firmly.

Waypoint 03 · match quality

Sample first, then focus

A head start feels like winning. A good fit is what wins over a career.

Waypoint 04 · be a scientist of yourself

Don't plan who you are. Test it.

You find your path by trying things, not by thinking harder about it. Small experiments beat a 10-year plan.

Pick a curiosity

Compare yourself with yourself last year, not with a younger person who started earlier.

This idea comes from Herminia Ibarra's research on career changers, which Epstein leans on: people discover who they are by doing, then reflecting. Introspection alone rarely gets there.

Practice that feels worse works better

Easy practice gives quick wins that fade. Messy, mixed practice feels slow and sticks.

Why?

Mixed practice forces you to ask "what kind of problem is this?" every time. Blocked practice answers that for you, so you never learn it.

At the US Air Force Academy, professors whose students did best in Calculus I, and who got the best ratings, had students who did worse in Calculus II. Hand-holding helped today and hurt later.

Waypoint 06 · far transfer

Borrow solutions from far away

Try the most famous puzzle in this field. Only about 1 in 10 people solve it cold.

The puzzle

A patient has a tumour deep inside. A ray strong enough to destroy it also destroys the healthy tissue it passes through. A weak ray is safe, but does nothing to the tumour. What do you do?

The fortress

A general must take a fortress. Every road is mined: a big army would set the mines off. So he splits his army into small groups, one per road. Each is light enough to pass safely. They all arrive at once.

With the fortress story, far more people solve it. The two problems look nothing alike on the surface. Underneath, they share one structure: split the force, converge on the target.

Kepler's habit

To explain why planets move, Johannes Kepler kept reaching for analogies: light, smells, boats in a river, magnets. Most were wrong. Collectively they led him to the idea of a force from the Sun.

Practical move: when stuck, ask "where else does a problem shaped like this show up?"

Waypoint 07 · outsider advantage

The answer often comes from next door

Companies post problems their own experts couldn't crack. Strangers from other fields solve a surprising share.

The case · Alaska, after the 1989 Exxon Valdez spill

Spilled oil mixed with icy water and turned to a thick sludge. It froze inside the recovery barges and couldn't be pumped out. Oil experts were stuck for years. Who cracked it?

A chemist from the concrete industry. He knew that concrete stays liquid as long as you keep vibrating it. Put a vibrating tool in the sludge and it flows. It worked.

He wasn't smarter than the oil experts. He carried a tool from a different world.

Waypoint 08 · lateral thinking with withered technology

Old parts, new uses

A Nintendo engineer with no elite training had a rule: take cheap, well-understood tech and use it in a way no one has. Guess which handheld sold more.

Handheld A

Old, cheap screen. Green-and-black, no backlight.

Up to ~30 hours on 4 AA batteries. Cheap. Survives being dropped.

Handheld B

Newest tech. Full-colour, backlit screen.

About 3–5 hours on 6 AA batteries. Pricier.

A was the Nintendo Game Boy (1989). B was the Sega Game Gear. Gunpei Yokoi's "withered" tech beat flashier rivals and became one of the best-selling consoles ever. Kids cared about playing all trip long, not pixels.

Same idea elsewhere: Nintendo's Wii had weaker graphics than its rivals but controls anyone's grandparent could use. M-Pesa in Kenya ran mobile money over plain SMS on basic phones.

Waypoint 09 · drop your familiar tools

Know when to drop your tools

The habits that made you good can trap you when the situation changes.

1949

Mann Gulch, Montana

A wildfire raced uphill at a crew of smokejumpers. Their foreman shouted to drop their heavy tools and run. Some kept carrying them. Thirteen men died.

Researcher Karl Weick's reading: for a firefighter, the tools are part of who you are. Letting go felt like no longer being a firefighter.

1986

The Challenger launch

Engineers feared the cold would weaken the rocket's seals. They had a strong hunch but no clean chart. NASA's culture accepted only hard numbers, so the hunch lost. Numbers were the tool no one could put down.

Your pack. Tap a tool to drop it.

Pack weight

Each tool is useful. Each becomes dead weight when the ground changes.

Waypoint 10 · foxes and hedgehogs

The fox beats the hedgehog

Psychologist Philip Tetlock tracked tens of thousands of expert predictions over about 20 years. On average, the experts barely beat chance.

◎ bullseye = what actually happened · ✕ = average of the darts

A fox is an ensemble model. Many weak, different views, averaged, beat one confident view.

Later, Tetlock's forecasting tournament found ordinary people with fox-like habits who outperformed professional intelligence analysts.

Waypoint 11 · summit check

Five questions before the summit

Pack list

  1. Don't feel behind. A late, well-fitted start often overtakes an early, poor fit.
  2. Check the terrain. Kind world? Drill. Wicked world? Widen.
  3. Run small experiments. Try, reflect, adjust. Skip the 10-year plan.
  4. Make practice harder. Mix it, space it, guess before you look.
  5. Collect analogies. Read and wander outside your field on purpose.
  6. Hold tools loosely. Your methods are not your identity.
Where the book is careful

Epstein does not say specialists are useless. In kind worlds like chess, golf or classical performance, early deep practice works. His claim is that most of life is wicked, and there range is the better default. Critics add a fair warning: breadth without depth anywhere can slide into dabbling.
