Urban trees · what the felling permit leaves out

A sixty-year-old tree comes down in an afternoon.

The saw takes a few hours. What goes with the tree is harder to see: shade on a bus stop, rain held back from a storm drain, dust caught before it reached someone's lungs. This page follows those losses downstream.

The replanting promise

Why ten saplings don't equal one old tree

Most felling permits come with a condition to plant replacements, often several saplings per tree cut. In 2024 the Supreme Court ordered the Delhi Development Authority to plant 100 trees for every one felled on the Delhi Ridge. Ratios like these sound generous. The accounting has three problems.

Survival. Under India's Green India Mission, survival of planted trees has been estimated at around 50–60%. Plantations are usually counted by saplings planted, rarely by trees alive five years later.

Location. Compensatory planting often happens far from where trees were cut. A forest patch on the city's edge does nothing for the heat on the street that lost its shade.

Time. Cooling, rain interception and pollution capture scale with leaf area. A sapling has a tiny fraction of a mature crown, and the gap takes decades to close. The benefits are lost immediately. The replacement benefits arrive slowly and uncertainly.

Who pays

The costs don't land evenly

People with air-conditioned homes and cars feel the loss of a street tree least. The people who spend the day outdoors, or sleep in rooms that never cool down, feel it most.

Outdoor workers

Delivery riders, vendors, traffic police and construction crews lose the shade they rest in during the hottest hours.

The elderly and the very young

Their bodies regulate heat less well, so a few degrees of extra heat raises hospital admissions in these groups first.

Dense, low-income areas

These areas usually had fewer trees to begin with, more roofs that trap heat, and less money to spend on cooling.

Pedestrians and cyclists

A treeless road in April is hard to walk. People who can afford to switch to cars do, which adds traffic and exhaust.

Closer to home

Two Indian cities, two warnings

Bengaluru

From garden city to heat island

IISc researchers led by T.V. Ramachandra estimate the city has lost about 88% of its tree cover over five decades while built-up area grew by more than 1,000%. The losses have come alongside hotter summers, flooding after modest rain, and falling groundwater. Road widening and metro projects continue to require thousands of trees to be felled.

Hyderabad

Kancha Gachibowli, 2025

Bulldozers cleared part of a 400-acre wooded parcel next to the University of Hyderabad over a long weekend, ahead of a planned IT development. The Supreme Court stepped in on 3 April 2025, stopped all felling, called the damage to about 100 acres "alarming", and directed the state to restore the green cover and protect wildlife on the site.

A fair counterpoint

Some trees do need to come down. Dead or diseased trees can fall on people during storms, and a metro line can take far more cars off the road than a stretch of trees would offset. The problem isn't that cities ever cut trees. The problem is that the decision usually counts the cost of the road and leaves out the cost of the heat, the floods and the hospital visits.

What better looks like

Keeping the canopy while the city grows

  1. Design around mature trees first

    Shift an alignment, narrow a carriageway, or bend a footpath before approving a felling. Mature trees are the hardest asset to replace.

  2. Price the loss in the approval

    Put cooling, stormwater and health value into the project's cost-benefit, the same way land and materials are costed.

  3. Replant in the same ward

    Replacement trees should shade the same streets that lost them, not a plantation on the city's edge.

  4. Count survivors, not saplings

    Audit replacement trees at 3 and 5 years, publish the results, and fund the watering and guarding that keeps them alive.

  5. Set a canopy target by neighbourhood

    Cities including Barcelona, Bristol and Seattle have adopted a 30% canopy goal. Tracking it by ward shows where the gaps and inequities are.

  6. Make felling decisions public

    Notify tree counts, species and girth before cutting, and give residents time to object. Transparency deters the long-weekend clearance.
