Look closer
Made by hand firstJohn Conway worked out Life in the late 1960s, testing rules by hand with stones on a Go board. Martin Gardner's column in Scientific American (October 1970) made it famous.
The $50 betConway guessed no pattern could grow forever and offered $50 for a counterexample. Bill Gosper's group at MIT won it within weeks with the glider gun on this board.
A whole computerGliders can act as signals and guns as clocks. Life can run any computer program, so it is a universal computer.
Unpredictable on purposeBecause Life can compute anything, there is no shortcut for questions like "will this pattern ever die?" The only general method is to run it and watch.

No square moves. Only the pattern does.

Square 47 stays square 47 forever. It just switches on and off. Yet every 4 ticks the same shape reappears, one square further along.

5of its 5 lit squares were lit at the start.
Tick 0

By tick 8 it shares none of its original squares. So what is the glider? Not the squares. It is the arrangement, handed along like a rumour.

Keep this in mind. A thing can be a pattern rather than a piece of stuff. Every specimen in this lab has its own glider. At the end we ask whether you do too.

The edge of chaos

Line the outcomes up from most orderly to most chaotic. Interesting universes live in a thin band between frozen and boiling. Life sits right there.

← More orderThe edgeMore chaos →
DiedNothing survives. Rules too strict.
FrozeSettles into still or blinking shapes.
InterestingLasting shapes that move, collide and build.
ExplodedBoiling static. Nothing lasts long enough to matter.

The glider here: gliders only exist near the edge. Too orderly and nothing moves. Too chaotic and any shape is torn apart before it can travel.

Look closer
Four classesStephen Wolfram sorted simple universes into four classes in 1984: dies out, settles, chaos, and complex. The verdicts here follow the same idea.
Langton's dialChris Langton measured how generous a rule is with life, a number he called lambda. Complex behaviour clustered at middle values. For Life, lambda is about 0.27.
How the judge worksIt runs 360 ticks on a 66 × 66 wrap-around board and measures how much is alive, how much changes, and whether the board starts repeating. It can be fooled; classifying behaviour automatically is a famously hard problem.
Why the soup is boxedStarting from a small square of noise lets you see whether a universe spreads (explodes), shrinks (dies) or keeps its shape.

Highway!

Press Run to the highway. For thousands of steps the ant scribbles a mess with no sign of order. Then, from nowhere, it starts building a road and never stops.

ProvenThe ant can never stay trapped. Whatever squares you paint, its path always leaves every box (the Cohen–Kong theorem).
Not provenThat it always ends up building a highway. Every start anyone has tried does, but nobody can prove it must.
So how do we know?We run it. Two rules, and mathematics still cannot see the ending in advance.

The glider here: the highway. A loop of 104 moves that carries itself two squares diagonally, over and over, built from squares that never move.

Look closer
Where it came fromChris Langton, a founder of artificial life, introduced the ant in 1986.
The exact momentFrom a blank grid the highway begins at step 9,977, and each lap of 104 steps moves the ant two squares diagonally.
More coloursGive the ant more colours, each with its own turn, and you get turmites: some fill squares, some make triangles, some build braided highways. Try them in the Rule menu.
ObstaclesPaint filled squares before or during a run. The ant usually still finds its way to a highway, sometimes after a much longer struggle.

Nobody leads. Each bird reacts only to the few birds near it, the ones inside its circle. No bird knows the flock's shape. The flock is a pattern none of them can see.

The glider here: the flock itself. Birds drift in and out of it, yet it keeps its shape and heading, like a glider made of moving parts.

Look closer
1986Craig Reynolds built boids to animate flocks without scripting every bird, and presented them at SIGGRAPH in 1987. Films later used the idea for bat swarms and penguin armies.
Real starlingsField studies of starling flocks in Rome found each bird tracks about 6 or 7 nearest neighbours, however far apart they are.
The first meterFor each bird, it checks how closely its heading matches the birds it can see, then averages. Random headings score about 0.3 or less; a tight flock scores close to 1. No single bird has this property; only the group does.
Break itSwitch off separation and the flock collapses into a clump. Switch off alignment and it becomes a buzzing swarm. Switch off cohesion and it frays into loose streams.

The mind

Is a mind just neurons, the way a flock is just birds?

Weak emergence

Surprising, but it follows from the rules. Run them and it appears every time.

Gliders, highways and flocks are all this kind.

Strong emergence

Something genuinely new that the rules could never produce, even in principle.

Physics has no clear example. The leading candidate is consciousness.

The gap

Science explains each level from the one below. Tap a rung. Then look at the last step.

The easy problems

How does the brain see a face, store a memory, decide to speak? Hard work, but we know what an answer looks like: a mechanism.

The hard problem

Why does any of it feel like something from the inside? Philosopher David Chalmers named this in 1995. A mechanism explains what the brain does. It is not obvious it explains why there is something it is like to be you.

A theory that puts a number on it

Integrated Information Theory, proposed by neuroscientist Giulio Tononi in 2004, says consciousness is how much a system is more than the sum of its parts. It calls that quantity Φ (phi). Build a tiny network and measure a toy version.

How it works: try every way of cutting the network in two, with signals allowed to flow only one way across the cut. Count how much the network's next move becomes guesswork. Φ is the loss at the cheapest cut, the network's weakest seam. The real Φ is far harder to compute.

What IIT predictsAnything with feedback loops has some Φ. A one-way chain has none, even if it behaves cleverly. A whole brain might have a lot.
Why it's controversialIt implies some simple circuits are a little conscious. In 2023, 124 researchers signed a letter calling IIT pseudoscience; others defended it.
The 2025 testA head-to-head experiment against a rival, Global Workspace Theory, was published in Nature. The results challenged key predictions of both.
Can we compute yours?No. Four nodes need 14 one-way cuts. A brain has about 86 billion neurons; the number of cuts is beyond astronomical.

Your call

Is consciousness weak emergence, strong emergence, or neither? Pick one to see what you are signing up for.

Is a self like a glider?

The proteins in your neurons are rebuilt again and again; molecules come and go. The pattern carries on and still answers to your name.

~500 BCEBuddhist teaching of anattā: there is no fixed self, only an ongoing process.
1739David Hume looked inward for a self and found only a bundle of passing perceptions.
1984Derek Parfit asked: if a machine copied your pattern perfectly, which one would be you?
2007Douglas Hofstadter: an "I" is a strange loop, a pattern that models itself.

A glider is real, and it is not a thing. It is a pattern that keeps itself going.

Maybe you are one too. Or maybe a pattern can explain everything about you except one thing: what it is like to be you. That is where the lab runs out of rules.
