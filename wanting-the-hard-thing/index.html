1Two half-hours

It is 9:40 on a weeknight. You have thirty minutes before you should be asleep, and two ways to spend them.

The first is a feed. A dog misjudges a jump. A chef does something improbable with a blowtorch. A stranger says something you half agree with. Each clip lasts fifteen or twenty seconds, so half an hour is something like ninety of them, and each one is a small lottery ticket: most are duds, a few are genuinely funny, and you never know which is next. At 10:10 you surface, faintly tired. If someone asked you to name five of the clips, you would struggle.

The second is a hard problem: a model that won’t converge, a chapter you’ve been avoiding, a proof that refuses to close. The first ten minutes are unpleasant in a precise, physical way, a steady pull toward anything else. At minute fourteen you notice the bug is in the data, not the model. At minute twenty-six something clicks. At 10:10 you are also tired. But tomorrow, what you worked out is still there, and it makes the next problem slightly easier.

Both half-hours ran on the same machinery: dopamine neurons, predictions and a running tally of costs and benefits. The difference is not that one was “dopamine” and the other wasn’t. The difference is when the brain gets paid, how surprised it is, what the effort costs, and what is left behind.

A feed and a hard problem compared
DimensionThirty minutes of a feedThirty minutes on a hard problem

A feed is a slot machine that pays out in small change. A hard problem is a savings account that charges a fee to open.

What follows takes both apart: the reward system in brief, why the feed wins the moment, why effort wins the decade, and how to shift your brain’s preferences toward the second.

So what? The useful question isn’t whether something releases dopamine (almost everything does) but which reward schedule you are training your brain to expect.

2The reward system in five minutes

Four pieces of machinery explain most of what follows. None of them is “the pleasure chemical”.

Dopamine is a teacher, not a treat

In the 1980s and ’90s, Wolfram Schultz recorded single dopamine neurons in monkeys that were given drops of juice. When juice arrived unexpectedly, the neurons fired a burst. Once a light reliably predicted the juice, the burst moved to the light, and the juice itself produced almost nothing. And when the light came on but the juice failed to arrive, the neurons dipped below their baseline at the exact moment the juice should have come (Schultz, Dayan & Montague, 1997).

That pattern is a reward prediction error: the reward you got minus the reward you expected. It is positive when things go better than predicted, zero when they go exactly as predicted, and negative when they disappoint. If you work with machine learning you have met it before: it is the error term in temporal-difference learning, and the fit between that equation and real neurons is one of computational neuroscience’s great successes. Later experiments showed the signal does causal work: stimulating dopamine neurons at the moment an expected reward arrives is enough to drive new learning in rats (Steinberg et al., 2013). Strongcausal in animals, consistent human imaging

Dopamine isn’t the paycheque. It’s the memo saying the paycheque differed from what you expected, and by how much.

Go deeper: the equation behind the spike

In temporal-difference (TD) learning, an agent keeps a value estimate V for each situation. After each step it computes δ = r + γ·V(next) − V(now), where r is the reward just received and γ discounts the future, and then nudges its estimate: V(now) ← V(now) + α·δ. Phasic dopamine behaves remarkably like δ (Montague, Dayan & Sejnowski, 1996; Sutton & Barto, 2018).

Two consequences matter for this piece. Fully predicted rewards generate no δ, so repetition quietly drains the surprise out of anything. And the signal migrates backwards to the earliest reliable predictor of reward, which is why a notification sound, an app icon or the first flick of your thumb can become exciting in their own right.

Wanting is not liking

If dopamine were pleasure, removing it should remove pleasure. It doesn’t. Kent Berridge and colleagues depleted dopamine in rats and found that the animals still made the characteristic “liking” reactions to sugar on the tongue (the rodent equivalent of licking your lips) yet would not walk across a cage to get it. Mice engineered to have extra dopamine wanted sweet rewards more, and worked harder for them, without liking them any more (Berridge & Robinson, 1998; Peciña et al., 2003).

Berridge’s distinction is between wanting (incentive salience: the magnetic pull a cue exerts) and liking (the hedonic glow itself). Wanting leans heavily on dopamine. Liking depends on small “hotspots” in the nucleus accumbens and ventral pallidum that use opioid and endocannabinoid signalling (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015). The two usually travel together, which is why they are easy to confuse. When they separate, you get the odd experience of wanting something you no longer enjoy. Strongcausal, animal Moderatefor a clean split in humans

In people the line is blurrier. Raising dopamine with levodopa slightly increased how much participants enjoyed music, and blocking dopamine reduced it (Ferreri et al., 2019), while blocking opioids with naltrexone also dulled musical pleasure (Mallik, Chanda & Levitin, 2017). The fair summary: dopamine is mostly about motivation and learning, and at most a modulator of pleasure.

Wanting is the magnet; liking is the taste. A feed can keep the magnet strong long after the taste has gone flat.

Go deeper: how do you measure “liking” in a rat?

Through taste reactivity. Sweet tastes elicit rhythmic tongue protrusions and lip licking; bitter tastes elicit gapes and head shakes. Similar reactions appear in human newborns and other primates, so they are a reasonable readout of hedonic impact rather than motivation. Crucially, they can be measured when a taste is placed directly in the mouth, with no wanting or effort required. That is what lets researchers pull the two apart.

Two tempos: phasic and tonic

Dopamine works on two timescales. Phasic signals are bursts and dips lasting a fraction of a second: the prediction errors above. Tonic dopamine is the slower background level. An influential theory holds that tonic dopamine tracks the average rate of reward in your environment, and therefore the opportunity cost of time: when rewards are plentiful, idling is expensive and vigour pays; when they are scarce, conserving energy is rational (Niv et al., 2007). Newer recordings complicate the tidy split. Dopamine release in the striatum can ramp up as a reward approaches and track how worthwhile the work is, partly independently of how fast the neurons fire (Hamid et al., 2016; Mohebi et al., 2019). Moderatemodels plus animal data

One implication follows from the opportunity-cost model, though nobody has tested it with phones: if your recent hours are dense with cheap rewards, your brain’s sense of what an hour “should” pay goes up, and a slow, uncertain task looks worse by comparison. Speculative

The cost ledger

The last piece is accounting. Before acting, the brain weighs expected benefits against costs: how long you’ll wait, how hard it will be, how uncertain the outcome is. The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), on the inner surface of the frontal lobe, sits at the centre of the effort side of this ledger. Rats with ACC damage stop climbing a barrier for a larger reward and settle for the smaller one on the floor (Walton et al., 2003). In people, the dorsal ACC and striatum encode rewards net of the effort needed to get them (Croxson et al., 2009).

Dopamine tips the balance. Depleting it in the nucleus accumbens makes rats abandon effortful options for easy food they like less, even though they still eat normally (Salamone & Correa, 2012). In people, higher dopamine synthesis capacity, or drugs that raise dopamine, shifts choices toward cognitively demanding, better-paid work (Westbrook et al., 2020). Strongcausal, animal Moderatehuman pharmacology

So what? Your brain pays for surprise, not pleasure, and it charges for effort and delay. Change what it predicts and what it charges, and you change what it wants.

3Why scrolling wins the moment

Put the ledger to work on a single moment: a spare minute, two options. The feed wins almost every one of these contests, and not because you lack character. It wins on every line of the ledger at once.

Effort is a cost, and it feels like one

Start with the uncomfortable answer to the first question. Mental effort isn’t neutral work that happens to be tiring; the brain treats it as a cost. Offered two tasks with the same payoff, people drift toward the less demanding one (Kool et al., 2010), and they will give up real money to avoid harder versions of a working-memory task, more so as they get older (Westbrook, Kester & Braver, 2013). A 2024 meta-analysis of 170 studies from 29 countries found that the more mental effort people reported on a task, the more frustration, irritation and stress they reported too, across professions, ages and task types (David, Vassena & Bijleveld, 2024). Strongbehavioural, replicated

Two caveats are worth knowing. Critics point out that the effort measure in most of those studies captures how difficult a task was rather than how intensely people chose to think, and that self-initiated thinking may feel different (Grund, Roelle & Schmid, 2026). And the link was weaker in studies from Asian countries, a hint that how aversive effort feels depends partly on learning history. That thread returns in section 5.

Why should effort feel bad at all? Not because thinking burns through fuel: the brain’s energy use barely changes between hard concentration and daydreaming, because task-related increases are small next to its large baseline budget (Raichle & Gusnard, 2002). The leading accounts treat the feeling of effort as an opportunity-cost signal. Attention and cognitive control are limited, and the ache of concentrating is your brain reporting that those resources could be doing something else (Kurzban et al., 2013; Shenhav et al., 2017). Moderatetheory with indirect support One study adds a physiological twist: after a day of demanding cognitive work, glutamate had built up in the lateral prefrontal cortex and people shifted toward low-effort, immediate options (Wiehler et al., 2022). Emergingsingle study

That is the core of the answer to “why does hard work feel unpleasant?” The bill for effort arrives immediately, and it arrives as a feeling. The benefits arrive later, and they arrive as abstractions.

The discount on “later”

Delay is the other big cost. People and animals discount future rewards hyperbolically: value falls steeply over the first stretch of waiting and then flattens (Ainslie, 1975; Mazur, 1987). The steep front of the curve is why a small reward now can beat a much larger one later, and why the same person who prefers the larger, later option when both are distant will flip to the smaller, sooner one as it comes within reach. Strongreplicated across species

V = A / (1 + kD)The felt value V of a reward of size A at delay D, where k is your impatience.

Two things in that figure matter for the rest of this piece. A project whose payoff is a year away is, to your ledger, worth a small fraction of its real value, often less than the next clip. And k is not fixed. Vividly imagining specific future events, a technique called episodic future thinking, reduces discounting in experiments (Peters & Büchel, 2010; Daniel, Stanton & Epstein, 2013), with a moderate average effect across studies (Rung & Madden, 2018). Moderatecausal, mostly lab tasks

Steep discounting is also linked with addictive behaviour (MacKillop et al., 2011) and, in correlational studies, with heavier smartphone use (Wilmer & Chein, 2016). Whether phones steepen discounting, or impatient people simply use their phones more, is unknown. Emergingcorrelational

A slot machine in your pocket

Now the reward side. Behaviourists found decades ago that the most persistent behaviour comes not from reliable rewards but from unpredictable ones. On a variable-ratio schedule, where a reward arrives after an unpredictable number of responses, animals respond at high, steady rates and keep going long after the rewards stop (Ferster & Skinner, 1957). Slot machines run on this schedule. So does a feed: the funny clip might be one swipe away, or twenty.

Dopamine neurons have a particular taste for this uncertainty. When a cue predicted juice with 50% probability, monkeys’ dopamine neurons showed a sustained ramp of activity during the wait, largest exactly when the outcome was least predictable (Fiorillo, Tobler & Schultz, 2003). And because each hit is unpredictable, the prediction error never fully learns itself away: something that pays out at random keeps surprising you. Strongfor the mechanisms Speculativeas a description of feeds: nobody has recorded dopamine neurons in a person scrolling

Go deeper: why would uncertainty itself be rewarding?

From a learning perspective, an uncertain option carries information: sampling it tells you something you didn’t know. Many reinforcement-learning agents are given an “exploration bonus” for exactly this reason, and novelty and uncertainty signals in the midbrain look like a biological version of it. The design problem is that a feed offers endless uncertainty with very little to learn. It is all exploration and no exploitation.

Novelty on tap, effort near zero

Newness is a signal in its own right: the human substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area (VTA), the midbrain’s dopamine hubs, respond to novel images as such (Bunzeck & Düzel, 2006). Moderate A recommender system is, among other things, a machine for delivering calibrated novelty: new enough to catch interest, familiar enough to be understood instantly. In one fMRI study of young adults, personalised short videos drew more activity in the VTA and in the default mode network than generic ones did (Su et al., 2021). Emergingsingle fMRI study

Meanwhile the cost side of the ledger is nearly empty. A clip needs no set-up, no goal held in working memory, no tolerance for confusion and no wait. In ledger terms it is a small reward at near-zero delay and near-zero effort, and almost anything beats nothing.

No bottom, no stopping cue

Most pleasures come in units that suggest when to stop: the end of an episode, an empty plate. People lean on those units more than they realise, and eat more when the same food simply comes in bigger portions or units (Geier, Rozin & Doros, 2006). Moderate Infinite scroll and autoplay remove the unit, and with it the moment of decision. You never decide to watch ninety clips. You fail to decide to stop, ninety times.

A large field experiment put a number on the resulting self-control gap. Given tools to limit their own screen time, many participants used them, and the authors estimated that self-control problems account for roughly 31% of social media use (Allcott, Gentzkow & Song, 2022). Moderatecausal field experiment, model-based estimate

Habituation, and the “tolerance” question

Two things are well established. Prediction learning turns yesterday’s surprise into today’s baseline. And hedonic habituation is nearly universal: repeat the same kind of stimulus and it lands softer. Together they mean a feed’s best moments lose their punch, which is why the feed must keep finding new ones. Strong

Whether heavy scrolling causes something like drug tolerance is a different and much bigger claim. In people who have used methamphetamine heavily, dopamine transporters are reduced and take many months of abstinence to recover (Volkow et al., 2001). There is no comparable evidence in ordinary phone users. Speculative A more modest mechanism has better support: contrast. Rats switched from a large reward to a small one run more slowly than rats that only ever received the small one (Crespi, 1942). Rewards are judged against recent experience. After twenty minutes of engineered surprise a spreadsheet doesn’t feel neutral; it feels like a downgrade. Moderatestrong in animals Speculativeapplied to work after scrolling

So what? Scrolling wins because it zeroes out every cost your brain charges (effort, delay and even the decision to continue) while paying out on the schedule dopamine responds to most. That’s design, not weakness, which means the remedy is design too.

4Why effort wins the decade

If short rewards win the minute, what does sustained effort buy that a feed can’t? Mostly things that accumulate: stronger synapses, new myelin, better mental chunks, a sharper attention system and, eventually, a different relationship with effort itself.

You become what you repeatedly do

Every skill is a physical change. At the level of synapses, repeated co-activation strengthens connections, a phenomenon called long-term potentiation (first reported by Bliss & Lømo, 1973). Strong At the level of brain regions, the changes show up in scans. Three months of juggling practice increased grey matter in a motion-processing area, which shrank back after people stopped (Draganski et al., 2004). Six weeks of juggling altered white-matter structure (Scholz et al., 2009). And trainee London taxi drivers who passed the notoriously hard “Knowledge” exam showed growth in the posterior hippocampus that trainees who failed did not (Woollett & Maguire, 2011). ModerateMRI measures are indirect, samples small

Myelin, the insulation that speeds and synchronises neural signals, turns out to be activity-dependent too. In pianists, white-matter structure tracks how much they practised in childhood (Bengtsson et al., 2005), and mice that cannot make new myelin-forming cells fail to learn a new motor skill (McKenzie et al., 2014). Strongcausal, animal

Go deeper: what a “grey matter increase” does and doesn’t mean

Structural MRI measures signal differences, not cells. A local increase could reflect more synapses, dendritic branching, glial cells, blood supply or even water content. The defensible claim is that practice changes brain tissue in task-relevant places, measurably and sometimes reversibly. Claims about exactly what grew, or that more is always better, go beyond the data.

One honest limit: what you practise is what improves. Commercial “brain-training” games make people better at the games and rarely at anything else (Simons et al., 2016; Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme, 2016). Strong Long, hard work pays off in the domain you work on, plus a few portable meta-skills: tolerating confusion, sustaining attention and knowing how to get unstuck.

Sustained attention is a skill, and it’s trainable

Attention isn’t one thing. The classic model separates networks for alerting, orienting and executive control (Posner & Petersen, 1990), and sustained attention fluctuates from moment to moment between “in the zone” and “out of the zone” states that are visible in both behaviour and brain activity (Esterman et al., 2013). It can be trained. Three months of intensive meditation training improved sustained attention on a demanding vigilance task (MacLean et al., 2010), and a two-week mindfulness course reduced mind-wandering and raised scores on the reading section of the GRE (Mrazek et al., 2013). Moderatecausal, modest samples

The most direct evidence about phones comes from a randomised trial in which participants blocked mobile internet for two weeks while calls and texts still worked. Sustained attention on an objective task improved by about as much as it typically declines over ten years of ageing, alongside gains in wellbeing and mental health, even though only about a quarter of participants complied fully (Castelo et al., 2025). Emergingone unblinded trial; volunteers may have expected benefits

Experts don’t have bigger working memories. They have better chunks.

Chess masters shown a real game position for five seconds can reconstruct it almost perfectly; shown pieces scattered at random, they do little better than novices (Chase & Simon, 1973). Expertise packs knowledge into larger meaningful units and builds retrieval structures in long-term memory that behave like an extension of working memory within the domain (Ericsson & Kintsch, 1995). Strong This is the engine of compounding: each chunk makes the next idea cheaper to hold, which makes the next chunk easier to build.

Difficulty that pays

The learning literature has a counterintuitive finding with direct bearing on enjoying effort: the conditions that feel most productive often aren’t. Testing yourself beats rereading for long-term retention (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Spacing practice out beats cramming (Cepeda et al., 2006). Mixing problem types beats practising one type at a time (Kornell & Bjork, 2008), and generating an answer beats reading it (Slamecka & Graf, 1978). Robert and Elizabeth Bjork call these desirable difficulties (Bjork & Bjork, 2011). Stronglarge, replicated literature

The twist is that learners reliably misjudge them. In Kornell and Bjork’s study, most participants judged massed practice at least as effective as spaced practice, even though spacing had produced better results. Fluency feels like learning and struggle feels like failure, when often it is the other way round (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

The night shift

Part of what you build during the day is finished at night. Sleep supports the consolidation of new memories (Diekelmann & Born, 2010). Strong In one elegant study, people who slept after practising a number task were more than twice as likely as those who stayed awake to discover a hidden shortcut in it (Wagner et al., 2004). Moderate The synaptic homeostasis hypothesis proposes that sleep also scales back the day’s synaptic strengthening, restoring the capacity to learn again (Tononi & Cirelli, 2014). Some famous claims have softened: overnight gains in motor skills look smaller than early studies suggested (Pan & Rickard, 2015).

A late-night scroll is therefore doubly expensive. It spends the hour, and it can shave the sleep that would have finished the day’s learning.

Flow: effort’s dividend

Flow, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi’s name for absorbed and effortless-feeling concentration, appears when a task’s challenge matches your skill and it offers clear goals and quick feedback (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). Experiments that manipulate challenge relative to skill find the balanced condition the most absorbing and enjoyable (Keller & Bless, 2008). Moderate In experience-sampling studies, people reported flow far more often at work than in leisure, while still saying they would rather be doing something else (Csikszentmihalyi & LeFevre, 1989).

The neuroscience of flow is young. One proposal is that parts of the prefrontal cortex quiet down (Dietrich, 2004), and imaging of experimentally induced flow found reduced activity in medial prefrontal regions (Ulrich et al., 2014). Emerging But the behavioural point is solid, and it is the hopeful half of the answer to the first question: many of the most enjoyable experiences people have are hard tasks at which they have become good. Enjoying effort is often the consequence of competence, not a precondition for it.

Compounding, honestly

Skill usually follows a power law: rapid early gains, then smaller gains per hour (Newell & Rosenbloom, 1981). Strong So where does compounding come from? From what the skill lets you do next. Children who read well read more and pull further ahead, which Keith Stanovich called a Matthew effect after the sociologists’ term (Stanovich, 1986). Knowledge makes new knowledge cheaper to acquire. Practice matters, but not without limit: a meta-analysis found deliberate practice explained about 26% of the differences in performance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions (Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2014). Strongwith debate over how practice is defined

So what? Short rewards pay you in the moment and leave little behind. Hard work charges up front and pays interest in structure, in speed and eventually in enjoyment. The catch is that the interest is back-loaded, exactly the kind of reward your discount curve makes invisible.

5How to actually learn to enjoy hard work

The encouraging finding in this literature is that the price your brain charges for effort isn’t fixed. It is a learned estimate, and estimates can be updated. First the evidence, then the protocol.

Effort can become its own reward

Psychologists call the puzzle the effort paradox: effort is costly, yet people seek it out (climbing mountains, running marathons, solving cryptic crosswords) and value things more because they were hard (Inzlicht, Shenhav & Olivola, 2018). People value furniture they assembled themselves above identical pre-built pieces, the so-called IKEA effect (Norton, Mochon & Ariely, 2012). Rats and many other animals will often work for food when identical free food is available, a behaviour called contrafreeloading (Jensen, 1963; Inglis, Forkman & Lazarus, 1997). Moderate

Robert Eisenberger’s theory of learned industriousness explains part of this. When high effort is repeatedly followed by reward, the sensation of effort itself acquires secondary reward value, and the habit of working hard generalises to new tasks (Eisenberger, 1992). A 2022 study tested the idea directly in people. Participants rewarded specifically for the effort they put into a working-memory task later preferred harder maths problems, even though they knew no further reward was on offer (Clay et al., 2022). Moderatecausal, replicated online; long-term durability unknown

Go deeper: what the 2022 study actually did

In the lab experiment (121 people), the researchers measured how hard each participant was working in real time using cardiovascular signals of sympathetic arousal, and made the reward depend on that effort rather than on performance alone. Comparison groups were rewarded in ways not tied to effort. In a transfer phase with a different task, and no rewards at all, the effort-rewarded group chose more demanding maths problems. Five online experiments with 1,457 people replicated the pattern. The transfer was measured shortly after training, so we don’t yet know how long it lasts or how far it travels.

There may even be a hub for this. The anterior midcingulate cortex (aMCC) integrates effort, bodily state and goals, and has been proposed as central to tenacity (Touroutoglou et al., 2020). In two patients undergoing brain mapping before epilepsy surgery, electrical stimulation of this region produced a vivid sense of an approaching challenge together with the determination to push through it (Parvizi et al., 2013). Emerging The popular claim that doing things you don’t want to do “grows” the aMCC, though, rests on correlations, such as thicker cortex here in unusually sharp older adults, rather than on training studies. Speculative

One idea to discard is willpower as a fuel tank that drains with use. The “ego depletion” effect largely failed a preregistered replication run across 23 labs (Hagger et al., 2016). Strongfor the failed replication The more useful model is the cost–benefit one: effort feels expensive when the brain predicts a poor return. Change the predicted return, and the felt price changes with it.

You can’t enlarge a fuel tank that doesn’t exist. You can change the price your brain charges for effort.

The protocol

Eight practices, each tied to a mechanism above. Treat them as experiments rather than commandments: run one or two for a fortnight and keep what works.

  1. Reward the process, not only the outcome

    Do. Decide what counts as a unit of real effort: a 50-minute focused block, ten attempted problems, one page drafted. Pay yourself for every unit, whether or not it went well. A mark on a visible chain, a good coffee, and the feed too, once the block is done.

    Why it works. This is learned industriousness in practice. Pairing effort itself with reward teaches your brain that effort predicts payoff, which lowers its felt price (Eisenberger, 1992; Clay et al., 2022). Moderate

  2. Shrink the start

    Do. Make the first step almost comically small (“open the notebook and write the next line”) and attach it to a cue with an if–then plan: “After I make my 9 a.m. coffee, I’ll open the notebook and write one line.”

    Why it works. The effort estimate is at its most pessimistic before you begin. If–then plans had a medium-to-large effect on follow-through across 94 independent tests (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). Strong The idea that starting itself lowers the felt cost is plausible but less tested. Speculative

  3. Raise the friction on scrolling

    Do. Log out, move the apps off your home screen or delete them, install a delay app, and keep the phone in another room during deep work.

    Why it works. Habits run on cues and ease. A self-installed app that imposed a short wait and a prompt before chosen apps opened cut actual openings by 57% over six weeks (Grüning, Riedel & Lorenz-Spreen, 2023), and self-set limits reduced social media use in a large field experiment (Allcott, Gentzkow & Song, 2022). Moderate Evidence that a phone’s mere presence drains mental capacity is mixed (Ward et al., 2017; a replication failed: Ruiz Pardo & Minda, 2022), but a phone out of reach is a habit out of reach.

  4. Effort first, then the treat

    Do. Put the pleasant thing after the demanding thing, never before: a work block, then ten minutes of whatever you like. Bundle a pleasure you only allow yourself during a chore, such as a podcast you only play at the gym.

    Why it works. A preferred activity reinforces a less preferred one that comes before it (the Premack principle; Premack, 1959), and this kind of “temptation bundling” raised gym attendance in a field experiment (Milkman, Minson & Volpp, 2014). Moderate Contrast effects suggest the reverse order, a feed before work, makes the work feel worse. Speculative

  5. Train boredom tolerance

    Do. Spend a few minutes a day with no input: a walk without headphones, a queue without your phone. When the urge to reach for the phone arrives, name it and watch it rise and fall without acting on it, a technique called urge surfing.

    Why it works. Boredom is a signal that your attention wants a better use (Westgate & Wilson, 2018), and many people find doing nothing surprisingly hard: in one study, 67% of men and 25% of women gave themselves an electric shock rather than sit alone with their thoughts for fifteen minutes (Wilson et al., 2014). Mindfulness training improves sustained attention (MacLean et al., 2010; Mrazek et al., 2013), and a brief urge-surfing intervention reduced smoking (Bowen & Marlatt, 2009). Moderate

  6. Engineer flow

    Do. Give every session one clear, finishable goal and a source of fast feedback, such as tests that run or answers you can check. Tune the difficulty so you succeed most of the time but not always. If you never fail, level up; if you fail most attempts, scale down.

    Why it works. Matching challenge to skill predicts absorption and enjoyment (Keller & Bless, 2008). Moderate A machine-learning analysis found that learning is fastest at an error rate of about 15% (Wilson et al., 2019); that figure was derived for artificial learners and is only suggestive for people. Emerging

  7. Make progress visible

    Do. Break a distant payoff into milestones you can reach in days rather than months, and log progress somewhere you’ll see it: a chart, a checklist, a paper calendar.

    Why it works. Monitoring progress improves goal attainment, especially when progress is recorded or reported (Harkin et al., 2016). Strongmeta-analysis of experiments Visible progress creates frequent positive prediction errors and pulls part of a delayed reward into the steep front of your discount curve; try the milestone switch in the discounting figure. Motivation also rises as a goal gets closer (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006). Moderate

  8. Protect sleep, and rest on purpose

    Do. No feed in bed, and charge the phone outside the bedroom. Between deep-work blocks, take real breaks (a walk, a stretch, a glass of water) rather than a scroll.

    Why it works. Sleep consolidates what effort builds (Diekelmann & Born, 2010), and bedtime phone use is associated with worse sleep (Exelmans & Van den Bulck, 2016). Moderatecorrelational for phones Brief breaks can stop attention from sagging over long tasks (Ariga & Lleras, 2011), and breaks spent on a phone appear less restorative than other kinds (Kang & Kurtzberg, 2019). Emerging

A four-week ramp

A suggested way to combine the steps. It hasn’t been tested as a package.

  • Week 1, clear the ground. Add friction to your two most-used apps and move the phone out of the bedroom (steps 3 and 8).
  • Week 2, pay for effort. Define your effort unit, shrink the start and put treats after work blocks (steps 1, 2 and 4).
  • Week 3, tune the task. Adjust difficulty toward flow and add daily boredom repetitions (steps 5 and 6).
  • Week 4, stretch the horizon. Choose a longer project and break it into visible milestones (step 7).

When it isn’t a willpower problem. If effort feels impossible most of the time, and nothing seems worth doing, including things you used to enjoy, that can be a symptom rather than a trait. Altered effort-based decision-making is documented in depression (Treadway et al., 2012), and trouble starting tasks is common in ADHD. A doctor or psychologist can help tell these apart, and both are treatable.

So what? You can’t install a love of effort by force of will. You can train it the way the brain trains anything: make effort reliably predict reward, then let competence do the rest.

6The verdict

What are the best tasks for the brain in the long run? Long, sustained-attention tasks, structured so the brain can follow them: long arcs, short loops.

Neither extreme is ideal. A diet made mostly of short, rapid rewards builds little that lasts, rehearses switching rather than sustaining, and quietly displaces sleep and deep work. But a task whose only reward sits at the end of a very long, very hard stretch isn’t optimal either. From where you stand, hyperbolic discounting shrinks that distant reward to almost nothing while the effort bill arrives daily, and motivation predictably collapses. The best long-run diet is dominated by sustained effort on something hard and meaningful, with the delayed payoff broken into frequent, honest feedback: milestones, visible progress, problems that resolve within a single session.

What the long kind concretely gives you

  • Structure that compounds. Chunks, skill and myelin make each later hour of work cheaper and more productive. Strong
  • Better versions of the attention it uses. Sustained attention and tolerance for confusion are trainable, and long tasks are the practice. Moderate
  • A lower price for effort. When effort reliably predicts reward, its felt cost falls, and the habit generalises (Clay et al., 2022). Moderate
  • Flow. Absorbed, effortless-feeling concentration mostly happens at things you have become good at. Moderate
  • The things you actually want. Most goals worth having are delayed-payoff goals: a craft, a body of work, a career, a relationship.

  • Displacement. Every hour spent is an hour not spent building. It is the largest and least controversial cost. Strongarithmetic
  • Sleep. Late-night feeds push bedtimes back and go with worse sleep. Moderatemostly correlational

Go deeper: reading the short-form video meta-analysis like a data scientist

The 2025 meta-analysis pooled 71 studies with 98,299 participants. Heavier short-form video use correlated with poorer cognition overall (r = −.34), most strongly with attention and inhibitory control, and more weakly with poorer mental health (r = −.21). Three reasons to hold the numbers loosely. First, nearly all the included studies are cross-sectional and rely on self-reported use and self-reported symptoms, which share method variance and can inflate correlations. Second, the cognitive estimates rest on a much smaller subset of studies than the headline total, and critics note that most of that subset came from China. Third, the causal direction is open: people with attention difficulties may be drawn to short content, and confounders such as lost sleep could drive both. What it does establish is a consistent association worth taking seriously, and a clear case for more randomised trials.

When short content is fine

Short-form video isn’t poison. In large datasets, the average association between adolescents’ digital technology use and their wellbeing is tiny, about the size of the association with eating potatoes (Orben & Przybylski, 2019), and effects vary from person to person, with most people showing little or no effect (Beyens et al., 2020). Moderate Short content is fine, even good, when it is:

  • chosen, not defaulted to, because you went looking for something;
  • bounded, with a unit set in advance, such as ten minutes or one topic;
  • after effort rather than instead of it;
  • social, like a laugh shared with someone;
  • a door rather than a room, like a one-minute explainer that sends you somewhere deeper.

A workable rule: give deep work your best hours, give short content your leftover minutes, and keep both out of the hour before sleep. And watch for one tell. When you catch yourself scrolling past things you don’t even enjoy, wanting has outlived liking. That’s the cue to stop.

So what? Aim for long arcs with short loops: hard, meaningful work, broken into pieces that pay out often enough for your brain to follow. Treat the feed as dessert.

7Myths, and what we don’t know yet

Myths worth retiring

Each verdict is on the right; open a myth to see the evidence behind it.

  • “Dopamine is the pleasure chemical.”Mostly false

    Dopamine mainly signals prediction errors, powers wanting and energises effort. Pleasure itself leans heavily on opioid and endocannabinoid hotspots. In humans dopamine can nudge enjoyment up or down, which is why the verdict is “mostly” rather than “entirely” false (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015; Ferreri et al., 2019).

  • “A dopamine detox resets your dopamine.”False as stated

    A day without your phone doesn’t reset dopamine levels or receptors. There’s no evidence that ordinary pleasures deplete dopamine, and even in heavy drug users, transporter changes take many months of abstinence to recover, not a weekend (Volkow et al., 2001). Cameron Sepah, the psychologist who popularised “dopamine fasting”, has said the name was never meant literally: the method is stimulus control borrowed from cognitive behavioural therapy (Sepah, 2019). That part is sound. Fewer cues, more friction and less contrast genuinely change behaviour.

  • “Our attention span is now eight seconds, shorter than a goldfish’s.”False

    The figure comes from a 2015 Microsoft Canada marketing report that cited a statistics website; nobody has been able to trace it to actual research (Microsoft Canada, 2015). There is no single attention span. How long you can sustain attention depends on the task, your motivation and your state (Bradbury, 2016), and people routinely watch three-hour films or play games for hours. Goldfish, for the record, can remember trained tasks for months (Brown, 2015). What has plausibly changed is behaviour: in Gloria Mark’s observational studies of people at work, the average time spent on one screen before switching fell from about two and a half minutes in 2004 to roughly 47 seconds in recent years (Mark, 2023). That is a habit shaped by an environment, not a shrunken capacity.

  • “Willpower is a battery that runs down.”Largely unsupported

    The ego-depletion effect behind this idea shrank to near zero in a 23-lab preregistered replication (Hagger et al., 2016). Fatigue is real, but the better model is a running cost–benefit estimate that motivation, meaning and reward can shift (Kurzban et al., 2013).

  • “Ten thousand hours makes anyone an expert.”Oversimplified

    This is a popularisation of Anders Ericsson’s research that Ericsson himself disputed. Deliberate practice matters a great deal in some domains and much less in others (Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, 1993; Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2014), and there is nothing magic about the number.

  • “Scrolling permanently rewires your brain.”Unsupported

    Every experience changes the brain a little; that’s what learning is. But there’s no evidence that short-form video causes lasting structural damage, and most documented effects are correlational or short-term. The mobile-internet trial suggests sustained attention can improve within two weeks of cutting back (Castelo et al., 2025), a hopeful sign that whatever changes do occur are reversible.

  • “Brain-training games make you smarter.”Mostly false

    You get better at the trained games and closely related tasks. Transfer to general intelligence or everyday cognition is weak to absent (Simons et al., 2016; Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme, 2016).

What we don’t know yet

  • Causality. Nearly all research linking short-form video with attention problems is cross-sectional and relies on self-reported use. People with attention difficulties may gravitate to short content rather than the reverse. Randomised trials are few, short and unblinded.
  • Content versus format. Studies rarely separate what people watch from how it is delivered. A feed of physics explainers and a feed of pranks may do quite different things.
  • Dopamine in scrolling brains. Nobody has measured dopamine release or receptor changes in ordinary users while they scroll. Claims about “dopamine tolerance” extrapolate from drug and animal research.
  • How long learned industriousness lasts. Lab studies show effort can become rewarding within a single session. Whether that persists for months, and how far it transfers, is open.
  • Why effort hurts. Is it an opportunity-cost signal, a metabolic by-product such as glutamate build-up (Wiehler et al., 2022), or both? The answer matters for how best to relieve it.
  • Who is vulnerable. Adolescents, people with ADHD or depression, and very heavy users may respond differently, and averages hide wide individual variation (Beyens et al., 2020).
  • Dose. No validated threshold says how many minutes of short-form video a day is too much.

So what? Be suspicious of any claim simpler than the evidence. Most of what’s true here is probabilistic, modest in size and still being worked out, which is a reason to run careful experiments on yourself rather than to panic.

8The one-page version

  • Dopamine teaches; it doesn’t reward. It mostly signals prediction errors and drives wanting. It pays out for surprise, not pleasure.
  • Effort hurts because it’s billed as a cost, most likely an opportunity cost, and the bill arrives immediately while the benefits arrive later.
  • Later is discounted steeply. A payoff a year away can feel smaller than the next clip.
  • Feeds win the moment by zeroing effort and delay, paying out unpredictably, supplying novelty and removing stopping cues.
  • The clearest costs of heavy short-form use are lost time, lost sleep and constant switching. Effects on attention itself are plausible but mostly correlational.
  • Long, hard work compounds into chunks, skill, myelin, trainable attention and a lower felt price for effort.
  • Effort can become rewarding if you reward the effort itself, not only the results.
  • Make long goals legible to a short-sighted brain with milestones and visible progress.
  • Enjoyment often follows competence. Flow is effort’s dividend.
  • Short content is dessert, not poison: chosen, bounded, after effort, never in bed.

Your brain will always feel the pull of the next surprise. You get to decide what it learns to be surprised by.
