The gut microbiome, explained from zero

You rent out your gut. The tenants pay in molecules.

Trillions of bacteria live in your large intestine. You give them a warm, dark home and food you can't digest. In return they do chemistry your own genes can't.

The deal

A lease, not an infection

Almost all of these microbes are tenants with a contract. Each side gives the other something it can't make itself.

At 1×

You can't break down most plant fibre. They can. Almost everything on this page grows out of that one fact.

At 1000×

  • The host holds the leash. Mutualism here is enforced, not friendly. You control tenants with mucus, secretory IgA, antimicrobial peptides (defensins, RegIIIγ) and by keeping the wall surface oxygen-poor, which filters for strict anaerobes that can't invade tissue well.
  • Gene arithmetic. Your genome has ~20,000 protein-coding genes. The first gut gene catalogue found 3.3 million non-redundant microbial genes Qin 2010; later catalogues reach ~10 million. That's the "extended genome" you borrow.
  • Germ-free animals prove the deal. Mice born and raised in sterile isolators survive, but they have enlarged caeca full of undigested fibre, immature gut immunity, altered stress hormones and lower body fat on the same diet Bäckhed 2004.
  • Ratio reality. The old "10 microbes per human cell" figure was wrong. The best estimate is ~1.3 : 1, and one bowel movement can shift it noticeably Sender 2016.
Where they live

Almost all of them crowd into the last 1.5 metres

Acid, bile and fast flow keep the upper gut thinly populated. The colon is slow, dark and oxygen-poor, so a billion times more microbes live there.

At 1×

Each gridline is a thousand times more microbes. Your colon is one of the densest microbial habitats ever measured.

At 1000×

  • Stomach. pH 1.5–3.5 kills most arrivals. Helicobacter pylori is a specialist that survives by using urease to make a cloud of ammonia around itself.
  • Small intestine. Bile acids (detergents), pancreatic enzymes, Paneth-cell antimicrobial peptides and a 3–5 hour transit keep numbers low. Residents are fast-growing, oxygen-tolerant types: Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Veillonella, Enterobacteriaceae. When counts climb here, that's the disease called SIBO.
  • Colon. Transit slows to roughly 20–40 hours. Luminal oxygen is close to zero, so strict anaerobes dominate. Bacteria make up about half of stool solids.
  • pH dips in the caecum. Fermentation acids drop it to ~5.7, then it rises toward ~6.7 in the distal colon as those acids are absorbed. That mild acidity itself suppresses some pathogens.
  • A radial oxygen gradient. Oxygen diffuses from blood through the wall, so the zone right next to the mucus holds a little O₂ and the centre holds none. Who sits where depends on oxygen tolerance. Payment No. 2 is about controlling that gradient.
Who lives there

A few hundred species per person, from two big families

Colours here follow the Gram stain microbiologists use: violet for thick-walled bacteria, pink for those wrapped in an outer membrane.

At 1×

Two healthy people can share few species. What their microbes do is far more similar than who they are.

At 1000×

  • Functional redundancy. The Human Microbiome Project found taxonomic make-up varied enormously across healthy people, while the abundance of metabolic pathways stayed remarkably stable HMP 2012. Many species can fill the same job.
  • Strain level is where biology lives. Two E. coli strains can differ by hundreds of genes. One is a quiet resident; another (O157:H7) carries Shiga toxin. Species names hide this.
  • Not all LPS is equal. Gram-negatives carry lipopolysaccharide, a strong TLR4 trigger. But Bacteroides lipid A is penta-acylated and immunologically quiet, while E. coli's hexa-acylated form is potent. Infants dominated by Bacteroides LPS showed weaker immune education Vatanen 2016.
  • Beyond bacteria. Archaea (methanogens), fungi (well under 1% of microbial DNA), protists, and a large virome, mostly bacteriophages that infect the bacteria and trade genes between them.
  • Renamed in 2021. Firmicutes → Bacillota, Bacteroidetes → Bacteroidota, Actinobacteria → Actinomycetota, Proteobacteria → Pseudomonadota. Older papers use the old names.
1Payment · Energy

They turn fibre you can't digest into fuel you can

Fibre passes through you untouched. In the colon, bacteria ferment it into three small fatty acids: acetate, propionate and butyrate.

At 1×

Butyrate is the colon wall's favourite food. Propionate goes to the liver. Acetate travels everywhere, even to the brain.

At 1000×

  • The enzyme gap. Your genome encodes about 17 enzymes for digesting dietary carbohydrates. B. thetaiotaomicron alone carries roughly 260 glycoside hydrolases and polysaccharide lyases Cantarel 2012, organised into PULs (polysaccharide utilisation loci): gene clusters that bind a specific fibre with SusC/SusD-like proteins, cut it at the cell surface and import the pieces.
  • Three routes. Acetate comes from pyruvate via acetyl-CoA, and from acetogens through the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway. Propionate mostly via the succinate pathway (Bacteroidota), plus the acrylate and propanediol routes. Butyrate mostly via butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase in Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, E. rectale and Anaerostipes; a minor route uses butyrate kinase.
  • Cross-feeding. That CoA-transferase step consumes acetate. So Bifidobacterium makes acetate and lactate, and butyrate producers eat them. One species' waste is another's input. Fibre feeds a food web, not a single microbe.
  • Energy budget. SCFAs supply roughly 5–10% of a person's daily energy. Colonocytes get about 60–70% of their energy from butyrate Roediger 1980, burning it by β-oxidation.
  • They are signals too. SCFAs bind FFAR2 (GPR43), FFAR3 (GPR41) and butyrate binds HCAR2 (GPR109A) on gut, immune and fat cells. Butyrate also inhibits histone deacetylases (HDACs), changing which genes are switched on. See Payment No. 4.
  • The butyrate paradox. Healthy colon cells burn butyrate. Cancerous colon cells run on glucose (the Warburg effect), so butyrate piles up inside them, where HDAC inhibition slows their growth Donohoe 2012.
  • Gas must go. Fermentation stalls if hydrogen builds up. Methanogens, sulfate reducers (making H₂S) and acetogens act as the hydrogen sink.
2Payment · The wall

Well-fed tenants keep the walls sealed

The colon wall is one cell thick. Mucus and oxygen chemistry keep bacteria at a safe distance. Switch between states to see what happens when the rent stops.

At 1×

Fibre in, butyrate out. Wall cells burn it and use up the oxygen. With no oxygen at the surface, only friendly anaerobes thrive. Break that chain and oxygen-loving troublemakers move in.

At 1000×

  • Two mucus layers. Goblet cells secrete MUC2 mucin. The inner layer (~50–200 µm) is dense and nearly bacteria-free. Host proteases loosen it into an outer layer that bacteria live in and graze Johansson 2008.
  • No fibre, they eat the house. In mice colonised with a defined 14-strain human community, a fibre-free diet pushed bacteria to switch to mucin glycans. Mucus-degrading enzymes rose, the layer thinned, and mice became vulnerable to the pathogen Citrobacter rodentium Desai 2016.
  • The oxygen switch. Butyrate is burned by β-oxidation in mitochondria, which consumes O₂ and keeps the epithelial surface hypoxic. That stabilises HIF-1α, which supports barrier genes. Butyrate also activates PPAR-γ, which keeps this metabolism on and suppresses iNOS (less nitrate). Antibiotics remove butyrate makers → cells switch to glycolysis → O₂ and nitrate leak into the lumen → E. coli and Salmonella respire them and bloom Byndloss 2017.
  • Tight junctions. Claudins, occludin and ZO-1 seal the gaps between cells. Butyrate strengthens their assembly through AMPK signalling.
  • Antibody coat. The gut secretes about 3 g of secretory IgA a day, more than all other antibody classes combined. It coats bacteria and keeps them in the lumen without triggering inflammation.
  • Chemical fence. RegIIIγ, a lectin induced by microbial signals and IL-22, kills Gram-positive bacteria that approach the surface, enforcing a ~50 µm no-go zone in the mouse small intestine Vaishnava 2011.
  • Akkermansia. A mucus specialist whose abundance tracks with leaner, insulin-sensitive mice and people. Its outer protein Amuc_1100 signals via TLR2; pasteurised cells improved some insulin markers in a small pilot trial Depommier 2019. Mucus grazing is healthy when fibre keeps the gel replenished.
3Payment · Security

A full house leaves no room for invaders

Pathogens need food, space and a foothold. Residents take all three, and they make chemical weapons too. The clearest example runs through bile.

At 1×

Antibiotics empty the building. That's when C. difficile, a hospital superbug, moves in. Putting a healthy community back cures most cases.

At 1000×

  • Nutrient niches. A pathogen must find a nutrient no resident is already using. Commensal E. coli strains that use the same sugars as pathogenic E. coli can block their colonisation in mice. Diversity covers more niches.
  • Oxygen and acid. The hypoxic wall (Payment No. 2) starves aerobic respirers. Undissociated SCFAs at low pH cross bacterial membranes and acidify the inside of susceptible pathogens like Salmonella.
  • Weapons. Residents secrete bacteriocins and microcins, and Bacteroides fragilis uses a type VI secretion system to inject toxins into neighbours on contact.
  • Bile chemistry. BSH deconjugation is widespread (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, Clostridia). 7α-dehydroxylation via the bai operon belongs to a tiny minority. C. difficile spores germinate on taurocholate plus glycine as co-germinant. Losing that minority tips the balance Buffie 2015.
  • The loop. The ~3 g bile acid pool cycles between liver and gut several times a day. About 0.5 g a day escapes to the colon and is replaced by new synthesis, which is why microbial bile chemistry also controls cholesterol turnover.
  • From stool to medicine. The U.S. FDA has approved two microbiome products for preventing recurrent C. difficile: Rebyota (2022, rectal) and Vowst (2023, oral capsules of purified Bacillota spores).
4Payment · Training

They teach your immune system calm versus attack

The gut holds the largest collection of immune cells in your body. Specific microbes send specific signals that push it toward tolerance or toward defence.

At 1×

No microbes, no training. Mice raised sterile have stunted gut immunity, and some parts of it overreact for life.

At 1000×

  • Tregs from fermentation. A consortium of 17 human Clostridia strains induced colonic Foxp3⁺ Tregs in mice Atarashi 2013. Butyrate raises histone acetylation at the Foxp3 CNS1 enhancer Furusawa 2013; Arpaia 2013, and SCFAs acting through FFAR2 expand colonic Tregs Smith 2013.
  • A symbiosis factor. B. fragilis polysaccharide A signals through TLR2 on CD4⁺ T cells to drive IL-10 and protect against experimental colitis Mazmanian 2008.
  • One microbe, one cell type. SFB alone were enough to induce intestinal Th17 cells Ivanov 2009. Useful against pathogens, but the same Th17 push worsened autoimmune arthritis in a susceptible mouse strain.
  • Tryptophan as a language. Bacterial indoles activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, driving IL-22 from ILC3s and resistance to Candida Zelante 2013.
  • Reach beyond the gut. Peptidoglycan fragments in blood, sensed by NOD1, prime neutrophils in bone marrow Clarke 2010. The gut sets the systemic baseline.
  • An early-life window. Germ-free mice accumulate iNKT cells in colon and lung and develop worse colitis and allergic airway disease. Colonising them as newborns prevents this; colonising adults doesn't Olszak 2012.
  • The human echo. C-section birth, early antibiotics and urban living are repeatedly associated with more allergy and autoimmunity. The "old friends" idea explains this as missed training. The associations are consistent; causation in humans is still being tested.
5Payment · Chemistry

They run a chemistry lab you were born without

Vitamins, plant compounds and even your medicines pass through microbial hands. Same molecule in, different molecule out.

Vitamins
Nothing→Vitamin K2
Bacteroides, E. coli · menaquinones

Bacteria also make folate, biotin, riboflavin and B12. Their share of your total needs is uncertain, and colonic B12 is made below where you absorb it, so you still need it from food.

Plant compounds
Ellagitannins→Urolithin A
Gordonibacter, Ellagibacter

From pomegranate, walnuts and berries. Only some people carry the right microbes, so the same food gives different people different molecules.

Plant compounds
Soy daidzein→Equol
Slackia, Adlercreutzia

Roughly 30–50% of people are "equol producers". Producer status may explain mixed results in soy studies.

Heart drug
Digoxin→Inactive form
Eggerthella lenta · cgr genes

Some strains reduce digoxin and lower its effect. Dietary protein suppresses the enzyme in mice Haiser 2013.

Parkinson's drug
L-dopa→Dopamine (wrong place)
Enterococcus faecalis · TyrDC

Converted in the gut before reaching the brain. The standard add-on, carbidopa, doesn't block the bacterial enzyme Maini Rekdal 2019.

Cancer drug
Inactive SN-38G→Toxic SN-38
bacterial β-glucuronidases

Your liver neutralises irinotecan's active form; gut bacteria undo it, causing severe diarrhoea. Blocking the bacterial enzyme protected mice Wallace 2010.

At 1×

Two people swallow the same pill or the same pomegranate. Their microbes decide what actually arrives.

At 1000×

  • Scale of drug metabolism. When 76 gut strains were tested against 271 oral drugs, 176 drugs were significantly metabolised by at least one strain Zimmermann 2019. Gene-level mapping found the responsible enzymes, enabling prediction from a metagenome.
  • Reactions microbes favour. Reductions (azo and nitro groups, double bonds), hydrolysis (glucuronides, amides, esters), deconjugation, dehydroxylation and decarboxylation. The colon is anaerobic, so reductive chemistry dominates, the opposite of your liver's oxidative CYP450 system.
  • Hormone-like bile signals. Microbially modified bile acids act on FXR in the ileum, which releases FGF19 and tells the liver to slow bile synthesis via CYP7A1. They also act on TGR5 on L-cells, raising GLP-1. Microbes are effectively editing an endocrine signal.
  • Metabotypes. For urolithins, people fall into producer groups (A, B, or none), and the group shifts with age and health. The idea of a metabotype is a fair way to think about personal response to plant foods.
  • Practical consequence. Long antibiotic courses can reduce vitamin K status, and drugs like digoxin show unexplained variation partly due to microbial genes. Precision dosing using microbiome data is an active research area, not routine care.
6Payment · Messages

They send signals to your brain, appetite and fat

Microbial molecules trigger gut hormones and nerves. The effects travel well beyond the gut.

At 1×

In mice, the evidence is strong and causal. In people, we mostly see correlations so far. Keep both in mind when you read headlines.

At 1000×

  • Appetite. SCFAs on FFAR2/3 in enteroendocrine L-cells release GLP-1 and PYY (satiety, insulin secretion). Colonic acetate reached the hypothalamus and reduced appetite in mice Frost 2014. Delivering propionate directly to the human colon reduced weight gain over 24 weeks Chambers 2015.
  • Serotonin. About 90% of the body's serotonin is made by enterochromaffin cells. Spore-forming bacteria raise their TPH1 expression through SCFAs and secondary bile acids Yano 2015. Gut serotonin doesn't cross the blood–brain barrier; it acts on motility, platelets and bone.
  • Vagus nerve. Lactobacillus rhamnosus JB-1 changed brain GABA receptor expression and anxiety-like behaviour in mice. Cutting the vagus nerve abolished the effect Bravo 2011.
  • Stress axis. Germ-free mice show an exaggerated cortisol-equivalent response to restraint stress, reversed by early colonisation with Bifidobacterium infantis Sudo 2004. Microglia, the brain's immune cells, stay immature without microbial SCFAs Erny 2015.
  • Human mood. Coprococcus and Dialister were depleted in people with depression across two large cohorts, even after adjusting for antidepressants Valles-Colomer 2019. Association, not proof.
  • Fat storage. Germ-free mice are leaner and resist Western-diet obesity. Microbes suppress gut ANGPTL4 (FIAF), raising lipoprotein lipase activity and fat storage Bäckhed 2004, 2007. Microbiota from the obese twin of a discordant pair made germ-free mice fatter; lean-twin microbes invaded and protected, but only on a high-fibre diet Ridaura 2013.
  • Blood sugar. Gut microbiome features helped predict individual post-meal glucose spikes to identical foods Zeevi 2015.
The fine print

The same tenants can pay in poison

Microbes make what their food and surroundings allow. Change the conditions and the payments change.

Heart
Choline, carnitine→TMA→TMAO
cutC/D lyases · liver FMO3

From red meat and eggs. Higher TMAO predicted heart attack and stroke risk Tang 2013 and made platelets hyper-reactive in mice Zhu 2016.

Gut lining
Saturated fat → taurine bile→H₂S
Bilophila wadsworthia

Milk-fat diets bloomed this sulfite reducer and triggered colitis in genetically susceptible mice Devkota 2012.

DNA
pks⁺ E. coli→Colibactin
genotoxin · DNA cross-links

Its distinct mutation fingerprint appears in human colorectal cancer genomes Pleguezuelos-Manzano 2020.

Liver & colon
Excess bile→Deoxycholic acid
7α-dehydroxylating Clostridia

Protective against C. difficile, but in excess it damages DNA. In obese mice it promoted liver cancer Yoshimoto 2013.

Metabolism
Leaky barrier→LPS in blood
TLR4 · "metabolic endotoxemia"

Low-grade LPS infusion alone caused weight gain and insulin resistance in mice, mimicking a high-fat diet Cani 2007.

Tumours
Oral microbe→Colon tumours
Fusobacterium nucleatum · FadA

Enriched inside colorectal tumours, and it travels with them into liver metastases Bullman 2017.

At 1×

"Good bacteria" and "bad bacteria" is too simple. Context decides. Fibre-fed, the same community pays in butyrate. Fed mostly meat and fat, it pays in TMA, sulfide and harsh bile acids.

At 1000×

  • Saccharolytic vs proteolytic fermentation. When carbohydrates run out in the distal colon, bacteria ferment protein instead, producing ammonia, phenols, p-cresol, indoxyl sulfate, branched-chain fatty acids and H₂S. Indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate are uremic toxins that accumulate in kidney disease.
  • Pathobionts. Organisms like pks⁺ E. coli or Bilophila live quietly in many healthy people. Disease depends on host genetics, diet, inflammation and community context.
  • TMAO caveat. Fish contains preformed TMAO and fish-rich diets are linked to good heart health, and kidney function strongly drives TMAO levels. Its role as a cause rather than a marker is still debated.
  • Inflammation feeds the wrong side. Gut inflammation generates nitrate and tetrathionate, which oxygen-tolerant Enterobacteriaceae and Salmonella use for respiration. Inflammation and dysbiosis can lock each other in.
The lease over a lifetime

Signed at birth, renegotiated for life

You start out almost sterile. The first three years decide much of who moves in.

At 1×

Human milk carries hundreds of sugar shapes the baby can't use. Evolution spends that energy to feed the right tenants first.

At 1000×

  • Birth mode. In ~600 UK babies, C-section births showed disrupted transmission of maternal strains and more opportunistic hospital pathogens such as Enterococcus and Klebsiella; differences largely faded by weaning Shao 2019.
  • Human milk oligosaccharides. Over 150 structures; the third-largest solid component of milk after lactose and fat. B. longum subsp. infantis carries a ~43 kb gene cluster to import and digest them Sela 2008. The result is an acidic, acetate-rich infant gut that resists pathogens.
  • Maturation. Large cohorts (TEDDY, ~900 children) show stepwise development, with cessation of breastfeeding the biggest single shift toward an adult-like, Bacillota-rich community by ~2–3 years Stewart 2018.
  • Adult stability. About 60% of strains in an adult gut were still present after five years, with Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria especially stable Faith 2013.
  • Antibiotic recovery. After a 4-day, three-drug course in healthy men, most species returned within ~1.5 months, but several common species were still undetectable at 6 months Palleja 2018.
  • Generational loss. Mice on low-fibre diets lost taxa that did not return when fibre was restored, and the loss compounded across generations Sonnenburg 2016. Industrialised populations carry fewer taxa than hunter-gatherers such as the Hadza, whose microbiome also cycles with the seasons Smits 2017.
For the data people

How we know, and why it's hard

A stool sample is a snapshot of a moving river, reported as percentages. That creates statistical traps.

Keeping good tenants

What actually helps, ranked by evidence

The strongest moves are boring. Feed the fibre-eaters and avoid wiping them out.

STRONG

Skip antibiotics you don't need

Each course knocks out taxa, and some stay missing for months. When antibiotics are needed, take them; the point is avoiding the unnecessary ones.

STRONG · ONE USE

Donor microbes for recurrent C. difficile

Cures most recurrent cases. For obesity, IBD, autism and other conditions it remains experimental.

MODERATE

Eat many kinds of plant fibre

Different fibres feed different specialists. People eating 30+ plant types a week had more diverse microbiomes than those eating 10 or fewer (observational) McDonald 2018. Most guidelines suggest about 25–38 g of fibre a day; most people eat less.

EARLY

Fermented foods

In a 10-week trial of 36 adults, yogurt, kefir, kimchi and similar foods raised diversity and lowered 19 inflammatory proteins including IL-6. The high-fibre arm changed enzyme capacity but not diversity in that window Wastyk 2021.

MIXED

Probiotic supplements

Strain-specific. Useful for some uses, such as preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhoea. After antibiotics, one probiotic delayed the native community's return compared with doing nothing Suez 2018.

EARLY

Regular exercise

Six weeks of training raised butyrate-producing taxa in sedentary lean adults; the change reversed when training stopped Allen 2018.

The whole thing in one sentence
You rent out the dark end of your gut. Feed the tenants fibre and they pay in butyrate, sealed walls and a calm immune system. Starve or bomb them and squatters move in.
