KM 0 · The one idea

Training makes you tired. Rest makes you fit.

Every run does two things at once. It adds a little fitness, which builds slowly and fades slowly over weeks. It also adds fatigue, which builds fast and clears within days. How you feel on any morning is roughly fitness minus fatigue.

A good plan keeps fatigue high enough to force adaptation but never so high that it outruns fitness for weeks. Then, in the last fortnight, it lets fatigue drain away while fitness stays. That's the taper, and it's why you arrive at the start line feeling better than you have in months.

The common mistake is running every run at "medium". Too hard to build the base cheaply, too easy to sharpen speed, and tiring enough to wreck the next day. If your easy runs and your hard runs are within 30 seconds per km of each other, your easy runs are too fast.

Use the talk test before the watch. Heart-rate zones need a real maximum, and the age formula (220 minus age) can be off by 10–15 beats for any individual. In heat and humidity, heart rate drifts up at the same pace; slow down and let it.

KM 10 · The arc

Build for three weeks, back off for one

Bars are weekly km; the dots and the bottom row are each week's long run in km. Weeks 4 and 8 are cutbacks. This is the same plan as "Steady build + taper" above. It assumes you can already run about 5 km comfortably and 15–20 km a week.

Ramp about 10% a week

Tendons and bones adapt more slowly than heart and lungs. Most running injuries are a load that grew faster than the tissue could.

Cut back every 3rd or 4th week

Drop volume by 20–30%. The recovery week is when the previous three weeks turn into fitness.

Peak long run 16–19 km

You don't need to run 21 km in training. Race-day adrenaline and the taper cover the last few km.

Training paces are estimates built from goal pace. Treat them as ceilings: if the talk test says slower, go slower. For goals above about 2:45, a planned run-walk (for example 4 min running, 1 min walking from the start) often finishes faster than running until you're forced to walk.

KM 15 · Fuel

Anything over 90 minutes needs food on the move

Carbs during the race

Your stored glycogen covers roughly 90 minutes of steady running. Beyond that, take 30–60 g of carbohydrate an hour: one gel every 30–45 minutes, starting around the 45-minute mark. A 3-hour finish means about 4 gels.

Train the gut

Stomachs adapt to eating while running, just like legs. Take gels on every long run over 75 minutes, in the brand the race will hand out or the one you'll carry.

Heat and humidity

Drink to thirst and sip at most aid stations. In a humid race, add electrolytes: sweat loses sodium, and plain water alone can leave you flat. Weigh yourself before and after a long run; losing more than 2% of body weight means drink more next time.

The day before

Eat a normal, carb-heavy dinner early. No need for a giant pasta binge before a half. Skip anything spicy, fried or new.

KM 18 · Race week

Nothing new on race day

T − 7 days

Last long-ish run

10–12 km easy, with 2–3 km at goal pace. Volume this week is roughly half of peak.

T − 3 days

Short sharpener

20–30 min easy with 4 × 1 min at goal pace. You'll feel restless. That's the taper working.

T − 1 day

Lay out everything

Bib pinned, shoes you've run 50+ km in, gels, chafe balm. 15 min shakeout jog or rest. In bed early; the night before the night before matters more for sleep.

T − 2.5 hrs

Familiar breakfast

Something you've eaten before long runs: toast, banana, idli, a little coffee if that's your habit. Then small sips of water.

T − 45 min

At the start

Toilet queue, then 5–10 min very easy jogging. Get into the corral that matches your goal, not your hopes.

KM 21 · Guardrails

The best plan is the one you finish uninjured

Check readiness each morning

If your overnight HRV sits clearly below your normal range, or resting heart rate is up 5+ beats for a couple of days, swap the hard session for an easy one. One missed workout costs nothing. Training through illness can cost weeks.

Strength twice a week

Runners who lift get injured less. Focus on single-leg work and calves: split squats, step-ups, calf raises (straight and bent knee), glute bridges, planks. 25–30 minutes is enough.

Sleep is training

Most tissue repair and hormonal recovery happens in deep sleep. A week of 6-hour nights shows up as a bad long run.

Stop and get it checked if pain is…

  • Sharp, or in one spot you can press with a finger
  • Getting worse as the run goes on
  • Making you change your stride
  • Still there the next morning, or in a bone rather than a muscle
