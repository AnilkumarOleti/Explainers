The code got cheap.
The judgment didn't.

LLMs now write the SQL, build the dashboard and draft the readout faster than any analyst. That removes the slowest part of analytics and exposes the part that was always the real job: choosing the question, checking the answer, and owning the decision.

This manual covers how to split that work between people and models, and why the split falls where it does.

The one picture to keep

Autopilot flies the cruise

Autopilot didn't remove pilots from the cockpit. It moved them. The automation holds altitude and heading through the long, predictable middle of the flight. Pilots own takeoff and landing, watch the automation during cruise, and take the controls back the moment something abnormal happens.

An analysis has the same shape. Framing the question is the takeoff. Querying, coding and charting is the cruise. Interpreting, deciding and explaining is the landing. And a surprising number is the abnormal event, where you take the controls back.

The model is fastest exactly where the work is most predictable. The human's time concentrates at the two ends, and on watching the middle.

This has happened before. When VisiCalc arrived in 1979, recalculating a financial model went from days of hand work to seconds. Bookkeeping clerk jobs shrank. Jobs for accountants and financial analysts grew, because cheap calculation raised demand for people who could decide what to model and whether the model made sense.

What changed

The middle of the week collapses

Most analysts spent most of their week in the middle: finding tables, writing and debugging SQL, formatting charts. That middle is now minutes. The ends don't shrink. They grow, because every cheap answer still needs a question in front of it and a check behind it.

Expect more analysis, not less. When coal engines got more efficient, total coal use went up (economists call this the Jevons paradox). Cheap analysis works the same way: product managers and leaders will ask ten times as many questions, most of them directly to a model. What becomes scarce is attention, trust in the numbers, and people who can tell a real finding from a plausible one.

Why not hand over everything

Four things that don't get cheaper

01 · PLAUSIBLE IS NOT CORRECT

Wrong answers now look finished

A bad query used to fail loudly. Now it returns a clean chart with a confident title. This one runs fine and overstates GMV:

SELECT o.category, SUM(o.order_value) AS gmv
FROM orders o
JOIN shipments s ON s.order_id = o.order_id
WHERE o.order_date >= '2026-07-01'
GROUP BY o.category;
-- one order, three parcels → counted three times
-- cancelled orders and test accounts included

Someone who knows the grain of the shipments table spots this in seconds. A model spots it only if the grain is written down somewhere it can read. Analysts have an old rule for this, Twyman's law: any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong.

02 · CHEAP CUTS MANUFACTURE FINDINGS

Slice enough and noise looks like signal

A model can slice a metric forty ways before you finish your coffee. At the usual 5% significance level, some of those slices will look "significant" by chance alone.

Assumes independent tests at p < 0.05. The fix is procedural: state the hypothesis first, count the cuts, and confirm on a holdout period.

03 · CONTEXT LIVES IN PEOPLE

The table doesn't know what happened

The facts that decide whether a number means anything are rarely in the warehouse:

  • The 14 Aug spike was a logging change, not demand.
  • The Germany dip is a public holiday.
  • "Active seller" excludes accounts under fraud review.
  • Categories were remapped in March, so year-over-year is broken.

Until someone writes these down where the model can read them, the model will confidently explain a logging bug as a change in user behaviour.

04 · ACCOUNTABILITY DOESN'T TRANSFER

Someone has to own the number

When a number drives a fee change, a launch or a headcount plan, someone must be able to say: "I checked this, here is how, and I'll answer for it next quarter." A model can produce an explanation. It can't carry the consequence.

Organisations run on that ownership. Take it away and you get numbers nobody fully trusts and decisions nobody fully owns.

The control map

Hand over control in proportion to how cheaply you can check

How much control to give the model depends on two things: how cheaply a wrong output gets caught, and how much a wrong output costs.

Checkability can be built. The map isn't fixed. Certified metric definitions, reconciliation tests and a set of questions with known answers each make a class of work cheaper to verify, which moves it to the right. Much of an analytics team's work now is to move tasks rightward on purpose, so more of the work can safely run on autopilot. The top-left corner never empties, and it shouldn't.

What the team owns now

Dashboards become disposable. Definitions become durable.

If a model can build any dashboard in two minutes, the dashboard stops being the product. What lasts is the layer the model stands on. In the incident above, the analyst's most valuable output wasn't the readout. It was the known-issue note and the data test, which make next Monday's answer right without anyone involved.

Decisionshuman-owned

Framing, trade-offs, the call, and a name on it.

The model as analystqueries · charts · drafts

Fast, tireless, disposable output. Only as good as what sits below it.

Semantic layerteam owns

Certified metric definitions: what counts as an active seller, GMV net of cancellations, the grain of each table, the right join paths. The model queries this, never raw tables, for anything that leaves the team.

Context storeteam owns

Known data issues, launches, promotions, holidays, a glossary of house terms. It turns tacit knowledge into text the model can read. Every investigation should add to it.

Golden questionsteam owns

A hundred or so business questions with verified answers. Rerun them on every model, prompt or schema change. These are unit tests for your AI analyst.

Decision logteam owns

What was decided, on which numbers, and what happened afterwards. It lets you judge whether the analysis was any good, which dashboards never could.

Raw datawarehouse

Events, tables, logs.

How the analyst's job changes

From answering questions to making answers safe

Your stakeholders will ask the model directly. That's a good thing: self-serve analytics finally works for people who know what to ask. The analytics team's job shifts from answering those questions to making sure the model they ask stands on certified definitions and says so when it's unsure.

Writes the queries→Writes the question, and names the decision it serves
Builds dashboards→Owns the definitions every dashboard and model stands on
Works the ad-hoc request queue→Designs the system that answers requests safely
Reviews a junior's SQL→Audits machine output: reconciliation, golden questions, spot checks
Delivers the report→Sits in the decision, argues the trade-offs, says when evidence is enough
Measured by dashboards shipped→Measured by decisions changed and errors caught before they travelled

Hiring follows. Fluency in SQL matters less. Domain knowledge, statistical and causal reasoning, scepticism, and the ability to hold a room during a disagreement matter more.

The automation paradox

The better the autopilot, the rustier the pilot

Aviation learned this the hard way. Automation hands control back at the worst moments, in unusual conditions with little time, and crews who rarely fly by hand are least ready. After Air France 447 was lost in 2009, investigators pointed to how the crew handled a stall after the autopilot disconnected. Regulators now push airlines to give pilots regular manual-flying practice. Analytics teams face the same trap, along with a few of their own.

Standing orders

The checklist

Pilots don't rely on memory for the things that matter; they run a challenge-and-response checklist. Here is one for any analysis that will inform a decision.

Decision-grade analysisChallenge · Response
  1. Decision at stakeStated before any query

    If no decision would change, it's curiosity. That's fine, but label it that way.

  2. MetricsCertified definitions only

    For anything that leaves the team.

  3. Every numberCarries its receipt

    Query, metric version, filters, date range, one click away.

  4. Cuts triedCounted and reported

    "We looked at 26 slices; one stood out" reads very differently from "Android is down."

  5. Surprising resultTreated as a bug until proven real

    Check logging, grain and joins before you check the world.

  6. Headline numberReconciled or derived twice

    Against the system of record, or by a second independent path.

  7. Causal claimExperiment or named confounders

    Say what else could explain it and why you ruled it out.

  8. OwnerOne named human

    Per decision, written into the decision log.

  9. LearningsWritten to the context store

    So the next question, from anyone, starts smarter.

  10. Hand-flyingScheduled for every analyst

    Keep the skill you'll need when the automation hands back control.

Last word

Let the model fly the cruise. Own the takeoff, the landing and anything abnormal.

When answers get cheap, the value moves to the question, the check and the call. Teams that treat LLMs as faster dashboard builders will drown in plausible charts. Teams that build the layer underneath (definitions, context, tests) and keep people at the decision will make better calls, more often.
