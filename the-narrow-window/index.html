Antibiotic resistance · an interactive explainer

The Narrow Window

Antibiotic resistance is not something bacteria acquire when you attack them. It is something you cultivate when you attack them badly.

Between the drug concentration that kills an ordinary bacterial population and the concentration that kills its rarest mutants lies a band in which the antibiotic stops being a weapon and becomes a selective medium. This page is about that band: what puts a patient inside it, where in the body it opens, and how the genes it selects move between bacteria.

00

A hip replacement in 1935

Before the mechanism, the stakes: what routine medicine looks like when antibiotics cannot be relied on.

Take an ordinary operating list at a district hospital: two hip replacements, a caesarean section, a child starting induction chemotherapy for leukaemia, and a man with burns over a fifth of his body. Each depends on a drug most patients never notice.

The hip operation puts a large foreign body into bone. Bacteria colonise that surface easily and the immune system struggles to clear them, and a prophylactic antibiotic given in the hour before the incision is one reason prosthetic joint infection is now uncommon. The caesarean opens the uterus, and a single prophylactic dose substantially reduces wound infection and endometritis well-established. Induction chemotherapy removes the child's neutrophils for a week or more, and any fever in that week is treated as bacterial sepsis until proven otherwise. A burns unit is, microbiologically, a large open wound colonised by hospital flora.

Before antibiotics these were different procedures. In 1941, physicians at Boston City Hospital reported that 82% of 122 patients with Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia died (Skinner & Keefer 1941). Puerperal sepsis, infection after childbirth, was among the leading killers of mothers until sulfonamides arrived in the late 1930s. Joint replacement against a bacteraemia mortality of four in five would not be an elective operation.

A modelling study of the ten most common surgical procedures and cancer chemotherapy in the United States put numbers on a partial version of that world. If prophylactic antibiotics lost 30% of their efficacy, it estimated about 120,000 extra infections and 6,300 extra deaths a year; at a 70% loss, about 280,000 infections and 15,000 deaths (Teillant et al. 2015). The same study found that 39–51% of the bacteria causing surgical-site infections were already resistant to the standard prophylactic drug.

10

Alexander Fleming saw the problem before most of the world had seen the drug. In his Nobel lecture of December 1945 he warned that penicillin would be bought over the counter, that people would take too little of it, and that by exposing bacteria to non-lethal doses they would teach them to resist it. Eighty years on, that is still a fair one-paragraph summary of this page.

The modern count

The best current estimates come from the GRAM Project's analysis in The Lancet (GRAM / Naghavi et al. 2024). Two measures matter. Attributable deaths are those that would not have happened if the infection had been drug-susceptible. Associated deaths are those in which a resistant infection was involved at all. In 2021, bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR) caused 1.14 million attributable deaths and was associated with 4.71 million. On current trends, those figures reach 1.91 million and 8.22 million in 2050, rises of about 67% and 75%. Between 2025 and 2050 that adds up to more than 39 million attributable and 169 million associated deaths.

The fastest-growing category was carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, from about 127,000 deaths in 1990 to 216,000 in 2021. The burden is also changing age. Between 1990 and 2021, AMR deaths among children under five fell by about half while deaths among people over seventy rose by more than 80%, and both trends are projected to continue. AMR is becoming a disease of ageing populations: of hip fractures, urinary catheters, ICU stays and immune systems weakened by age and chemotherapy.

The same modelling carries a less-quoted result. Better access to care and better treatment of severe infection could avert up to about 92 million deaths by 2050; a sustained pipeline of new drugs against Gram-negative bacteria, about 11 million. Much of the avoidable death in this story still comes from people who never receive an effective antibiotic at all, a point section 10 returns to.

You may have seen the claim that AMR will kill 10 million people a year by 2050. It comes from the 2016 O'Neill Review, which extrapolated from thin data and was criticised for it contested. The GRAM forecasts are better grounded, and they are serious enough without inflation.

01

What an antibiotic actually does

Resistance only makes sense against the thing it resists. Five targets, at the molecular level.

Clinically useful antibiotics hit a small number of processes that bacteria need and human cells either lack or build differently. Each drug's mode of action already implies the ways a bacterium can escape it.

The cell wall

The bacterial wall is a mesh of glycan strands cross-linked by short peptides. Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) make the cross-links: their transpeptidase domain uses an active-site serine to attack the D-Ala-D-Ala end of one pentapeptide and stitch it to a neighbour. A β-lactam is a structural mimic of that D-Ala-D-Ala end. The serine attacks the strained β-lactam ring instead and forms a covalent acyl-enzyme that hydrolyses so slowly the PBP is effectively dead. Cross-linking stops while the cell's own autolysins keep cutting, and the wall fails under osmotic pressure.

Vancomycin works differently. It binds the substrate rather than the enzyme: five hydrogen bonds clamp it onto the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of the wall precursor, hiding it from every PBP at once. The distinction decides how resistance evolves. A drug that binds an enzyme can be escaped by a point mutation in that enzyme. A drug that binds a substrate can only be escaped by changing the substrate, which means rebuilding a biosynthetic pathway. Vancomycin resistance needed a whole operon of cooperating genes, and it took about thirty years to reach the clinic (plate ⑧).

The ribosome

Bacterial 70S ribosomes differ enough from human 80S ribosomes to be hit selectively. On the small 30S subunit, aminoglycosides bind the decoding site in 16S rRNA and make the ribosome accept the wrong tRNAs. The mistranslated proteins that result damage the membrane and let in more drug, which is part of why aminoglycosides kill rather than merely inhibit. Tetracyclines bind nearby and block aminoacyl-tRNA from docking at the A site. On the large 50S subunit, macrolides such as azithromycin bind in the exit tunnel through which the growing peptide leaves; oxazolidinones (linezolid) and chloramphenicol bind the peptidyl transferase centre, where peptide bonds are made.

DNA and RNA

Fluoroquinolones target DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, enzymes that cut both strands of DNA, pass another duplex through the gap and reseal it. Ciprofloxacin wedges into the cut and traps the enzyme in its cleaved state. That is a gain of toxic function rather than simple inhibition: the cell is left with enzyme-bridged double-strand breaks along its chromosome, which is far more lethal than having less topoisomerase, and which switches on the SOS response (section 04). Rifamycins bind the β-subunit of RNA polymerase in the channel the new RNA must exit through, so transcription stalls after two or three nucleotides.

Folate

Bacteria make their own folate; humans take it from food. Sulfonamides compete with para-aminobenzoic acid at dihydropteroate synthase (DHPS), and trimethoprim inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) two steps later. Co-trimoxazole combines them as a sequential blockade of one pathway.

Membranes

Polymyxins, including colistin, are cationic peptides that bind the phosphate groups of lipid A, the anchor of lipopolysaccharide (LPS). They displace the Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ ions that bridge neighbouring LPS molecules and disrupt the outer membrane. Daptomycin, active only against Gram-positive bacteria, needs calcium to insert into the cytoplasmic membrane, where it oligomerises and depolarises the cell.

The Gram-negative problem

The Gram-negative envelope is the main reason antibiotic discovery stalled. These bacteria wrap their thin peptidoglycan in a second, outer membrane whose outer leaflet is LPS, packed tightly and cross-bridged by divalent cations. Hydrophobic molecules cross it slowly. Hydrophilic molecules must pass through porins, water-filled channels such as OmpF and OmpC that admit molecules up to roughly 600 daltons (approximate). Whatever gets through then meets RND efflux pumps such as AcrAB-TolC, which span both membranes and push a wide range of compounds back outside. The two barriers multiply: slow entry and fast exit keep the internal concentration low even when the outside concentration is high. Many compounds that kill Gram-positive bacteria never reach their targets in Gram-negatives.

When Richter, Hergenrother and colleagues measured which compounds actually accumulate inside Escherichia coli, they found rules the field had lacked. Molecules were more likely to accumulate if they carried an ionisable nitrogen, ideally a primary amine, had few rotatable bonds, and had low globularity, meaning flat or elongated rather than spherical (Richter et al. 2017). They called these the eNTRy rules and used them to convert a Gram-positive-only compound into one active against Gram-negatives. It is one of very few general principles available for this problem probable.

02

Resistance is older than medicine

We did not invent resistance. We invented the selection pressure that concentrated and mobilised it.

The familiar story, that we invented antibiotics and bacteria then invented resistance, is wrong in its first step. Most antibiotics are natural products of soil microbes, chiefly Streptomyces and fungi, refined by chemists. Their producers needed to protect themselves, and their neighbours had hundreds of millions of years to evolve countermeasures.

In 2011, D'Costa and colleagues sequenced DNA from Beringian permafrost dated to about 30,000 years ago. They found genes for β-lactamases, tetracycline resistance and a complete vanA-type vancomycin resistance cluster whose protein structure matched modern versions (D'Costa et al. 2011). A year later, Bhullar and colleagues cultured bacteria from Lechuguilla Cave in New Mexico, a cave system isolated from the surface for more than four million years. Most strains resisted several antibiotics, and some resisted most of the drugs they were tested against (Bhullar et al. 2012).

What antibiotics do in soil is still debated. At the low concentrations found there, many may act less as weapons than as signals that change gene expression in neighbouring cells probable. Either way, the resistome, the full set of resistance genes in a microbial community, is vast and ancient.

What changed after 1940 was selection. Human medicine and agriculture now release antibiotics on a scale soil never produced, and every exposure that sits between the concentration that stops the majority and the concentration that stops the rare resistant cell enriches that cell. That pressure concentrated rare genes into human pathogens and moved them onto plasmids that cross species lines.

KEY

We did not create resistance. We created the selection pressure that concentrated and mobilised it. The genes were already in the soil. The selection window, introduced in section 05, is the mechanism by which a prescription turns a rare gene into a common one.

Plate ⑧ shows the consequence. Each drug's introduction is plotted against the first documented resistance. For most, the wait was a few years or less, because the genes already existed somewhere. Vancomycin's thirty-year gap is the outlier, and section 01 explains why: you cannot mutate your way around a drug that binds your substrate.

03

The six mechanisms

There are six broad ways bacteria survive a drug. Each has named genes, and most reached the clinic within a decade of the drug they defeat.

These mechanisms are what gets selected. Section 05 explains why selection happens and when. Plate ② animates each strategy; the notes below name the genes that microbiologists use to identify them in the clinic.

1

Destroy the drug

The commonest answer to the β-lactams is an enzyme that breaks the β-lactam ring before it reaches its target. Microbiologists sort these β-lactamases by Ambler class: A, C and D are serine enzymes, class B are metallo-enzymes that use zinc. The clinically important names to recognise are the extended-spectrum enzymes, with blaCTX-M-15 the dominant global lineage, and the carbapenemases KPC, OXA-48, and the metallo-enzymes NDM-1, VIM and IMP. Aminoglycosides meet a different family of enzymes that chemically tag the drug; their names (AAC, ANT, APH) encode the reaction, and section 09 returns to NDM-1 in particular.

2

Change the target

If the drug binds a bacterial molecule, altering that molecule can lower the drug's grip. MRSA carries mecA, which encodes an extra penicillin-binding protein, PBP2a, that keeps building wall while β-lactams block the normal PBPs. Vancomycin resistance (the vanA/vanB operons) swaps the drug's binding target so that one of five hydrogen bonds is lost, dropping affinity roughly a thousandfold. Point changes in gyrA and parC blunt fluoroquinolones; erm genes methylate the ribosome against macrolides; rpoB changes underlie rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis, and katG/inhA isoniazid resistance. Methyltransferases such as armA and rmtB can knock out almost all aminoglycosides at once. The one that worried microbiologists most is mcr-1, which modifies lipid A to resist colistin and, unlike the chromosomal colistin mechanisms before it, sits on a plasmid.

3

Pump it out

Efflux pumps export drugs faster than they enter. They fall into families (RND, MFS, SMR, ABC, MATE), but the ones that matter most in Gram-negatives are the RND pumps such as AcrAB-TolC and MexAB-OprM. These are tripartite, span both membranes, and move a wide range of unrelated drugs, so turning one up gives multi-drug resistance in a single regulatory step. This is why efflux is so dangerous: one change, many drugs lost.

4

Lock the door

Reducing the drug that gets in. Gram-negatives downregulate porins (OmpF, OmpC); losing the OprD porin in Pseudomonas aeruginosa blocks carbapenem entry with no enzyme at all. The key point is synergy: modestly raised efflux plus modestly reduced entry together can cross the clinical breakpoint where neither alone would. Resistance is often additive in exactly this quiet way.

5

Bypass the pathway

Supply a replacement part the drug cannot touch. Against the folate drugs, sul1/sul2 provide a drug-insensitive DHPS and dfrA cassettes a drug-insensitive DHFR, so the pathway runs despite the block. Overproducing the original target achieves the same by swamping the drug.

6

Hide or protect

Shield the target instead of changing it. Ribosomal protection proteins (TetM, TetO) knock tetracycline off the ribosome; Qnr proteins shield the fluoroquinolone target; the biofilm matrix and intracellular niches hide whole populations from drug and immune system alike (sections 06 and 08).

Read across the six and a pattern appears. Only mechanism 2, and only sometimes, needs a brand-new mutation in the pathogen you are treating. The rest are genes that already exist somewhere and can be acquired intact. That is why resistance usually arrives faster than new mutation alone would predict, and it is the subject of the next section.

04

How the genes travel

Resistance genes are not inherited so much as traded. The gut is the exchange floor.

Vertical: the mutants are already there

A bacterium copies its genome with an error rate near 10⁻⁹–10⁻¹⁰ per base pair per generation. That sounds reassuring until you count the population. A modest infection holds 10⁸–10⁹ cells; a dense one far more. With a billion cells dividing, a mutation that occurs once in a billion base-copies is not a remote possibility: for a target like rpoB, where a single base change confers rifampicin resistance, a resistant cell is almost certainly already present before the first dose. You are not usually creating the mutant. You are deciding whether to select it.

Some strains make matters worse. Hypermutators have lost a mismatch-repair gene such as mutS or mutL and mutate ten to a thousand times faster. They are common in the chronic P. aeruginosa that colonises cystic-fibrosis lungs, where repeated antibiotic courses select for the very trait that accelerates further resistance.

The SOS response: the drug speeds up its own defeat

Bacteria carry a stress programme called the SOS response, held down by the LexA repressor and triggered when RecA detects DNA damage. Once triggered, it raises the mutation rate and activates the enzymes that shuffle gene cassettes (below). The uncomfortable part: fluoroquinolones, which cause DNA breaks by design, and some β-lactams are among the triggers probable. A sub-lethal exposure, exactly the selection-window condition of section 05, can therefore accelerate the evolution of resistance to the very drug causing it.

Horizontal: acquiring a finished gene

The faster route is to receive a resistance gene whole from another bacterium, even a different species. Three channels carry it. In conjugation, one cell builds a pilus to another and copies a plasmid across; plasmid families such as IncF, IncX and IncHI2 are the usual vehicles for resistance. In transformation, a cell takes up loose DNA from its surroundings, which is how Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria assembled mosaic PBP genes from several species. In transduction, a bacteriophage carries bacterial DNA between hosts.

The cargo is organised like a set of nesting dolls. The innermost unit is a gene cassette. An integron (class 1 integrons carry the intI1 integrase and an attI capture site) collects cassettes into an array and can rearrange them. The integron rides inside a transposon such as Tn21, which can jump between DNA molecules, and the transposon sits on a conjugative plasmid that crosses between cells. A gene can thus travel from a soil organism to a human pathogen without either ever being an ancestor of the other. Plate ⑥ animates the exchange and the nesting.

Why restricting a drug rarely reverses resistance

Resistance usually costs the bacterium something: a slower growth rate, an energy burden. The intuition is that if you stop using the drug, the cheaper susceptible strains should win back the population. They often do not. Bacteria acquire compensatory mutations that restore fitness while keeping the resistance gene, so the cost that would have driven reversal disappears probable. This is counterintuitive and it matters: stewardship slows new resistance far more reliably than it rolls back the resistance already present.

Co-selection: buy one, select for all

Resistance genes often cluster on a single plasmid, sometimes alongside genes for heavy-metal and disinfectant tolerance such as qacE and the mer operon. When they share a plasmid, using any one of the relevant drugs, or exposing the bacteria to those metals or biocides, selects for the whole package. It is a reason resistance to a rarely used drug can persist, and a reason pollution with metals or antiseptics can maintain antibiotic resistance with no antibiotic present. Section 10 returns to this where it matters most, in manufacturing effluent.

Watching it happen in days

All of this, de novo mutation and selection across a concentration gradient, is directly watchable. In 2016 Baym and colleagues built a giant agar plate, about the size of a dining table, with the drug laid down in bands that step up roughly tenfold at each boundary: no drug at the edges, then 1×, 10×, 100×, up to about 1000× the dose that stops the starting strain (Baym et al. 2016). They inoculated sensitive E. coli at both ends and filmed. The bacteria sweep across the drug-free band and halt at the first wall of drug. Then, at the frontier, a mutant appears that can grow a little way in, founds a wedge-shaped colony, and its descendants push to the next wall, where the process repeats. Within about eleven days, lineages were growing at a thousand times the starting lethal dose. The plate below recreates it.

05

The narrow window

The centrepiece. Where pharmacokinetics, the empty-stomach question, and selection meet.

Everything so far has been about what resistance is. This section is about why a given prescription does or does not select for it. The argument runs through three ideas in order: what the MIC actually measures, how a drug's concentration moves through a shape over time, and the band of concentrations in which selection happens.

MIC is a measurement, not a constant

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is the lowest drug concentration that stops visible growth in a standard test. In broth microdilution, a fixed inoculum of bacteria is added to a row of wells with the drug doubling in each (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4 mg/L and so on), incubated 16–20 hours, and read by eye. Two consequences follow. First, because the steps are doublings, the result is grainy: an MIC of 2 mg/L means somewhere between 1 and 4, and repeat tests of the same strain often differ by one dilution. Second, "susceptible" and "resistant" are not read off the MIC directly. A committee, EUCAST in Europe or CLSI in the United States, sets a breakpoint from the drug's pharmacokinetics, its clinical track record and the distribution of MICs in wild populations. The two committees sometimes choose different breakpoints, and they revise them. For meropenem against Enterobacterales, CLSI now calls an isolate susceptible at an MIC of 1 mg/L or below; it once drew that line at 4. Lowering it reclassified a swathe of isolates as resistant overnight, with no change in the bacteria at all.

(Throughout, MIC is in mg/L, which is identical to µg/mL.)

One housekeeping note used throughout: MIC is quoted in mg/L, which is numerically the same as µg/mL.

Three shapes of killing

Whether a drug works depends not only on reaching the MIC but on how its concentration relates to the MIC over the dosing interval. Three patterns, each with its own index:

  • Time above MIC (T>MIC) governs the β-lactams. They kill at a steady rate whenever the concentration is above the MIC and gain nothing from going higher, so what matters is the fraction of the interval spent above it. This is why dose frequency, and in hospitals extended or continuous infusion, matters more than dose size for these drugs.
  • Peak over MIC (Cmax/MIC) governs aminoglycosides and, with a contribution from the next index, fluoroquinolones. A high peak kills more, and a prolonged post-antibiotic effect suppresses regrowth after the drug falls. This is why a single large daily dose of gentamicin outperforms the same amount split through the day.
  • Exposure over MIC (AUC₀₋₂₄/MIC) governs vancomycin, linezolid and the tetracyclines: the total area under the concentration-time curve over a day, divided by the MIC. Vancomycin dosing has shifted from chasing a trough concentration to targeting an AUC of roughly 400–600 mg·h/L.
MSW

The Mutant Selection Window

Draw two lines. The lower is the MIC: the concentration that stops the ordinary population. The upper is the mutant prevention concentration (MPC): the concentration that stops even a single-step resistant mutant from growing. Above the MPC, resistant mutants die too, so none is selected. Below the MIC, nothing is inhibited, so there is no selective advantage to being resistant. Between the two, the susceptible population is suppressed while a resistant mutant grows freely. That band is the mutant selection window, described by Drlica and Zhao. Time spent inside it is time spent enriching resistance.

Honesty requires two caveats. The MPC is an in-vitro construct, and how well it predicts what happens in a patient is debated. And dosing strategies explicitly designed to stay above the window have not been broadly validated in clinical trials contested. The window is a way to think, not a dosing rule.

Then, and only then, the stomach

Now the question that sends people to the internet at night: does it matter whether you take the tablet with food? It matters because food changes the shape of the concentration curve, and the shape is what decides where the curve sits relative to the window. Four mechanisms:

  • Gastric pH. A fasting stomach runs around pH 1.5–3.5; a meal raises it to roughly 4–5. Drugs that need acid to dissolve, or to hydrolyse an ester prodrug, are affected. Acid-suppressing drugs (proton-pump inhibitors, H2-blockers) do the same thing for longer.
  • Gastric emptying. Food slows the stomach's delivery to the small intestine, where most absorption happens. For many drugs this delays and flattens the peak even when the total amount absorbed is unchanged. This is the crucial distinction: rate of absorption versus extent. A lower, later peak with the same area hurts a Cmax/MIC drug and barely touches an AUC/MIC drug.
  • Chelation. Tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones bind divalent and trivalent metal cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Fe²⁺, Zn²⁺) into insoluble complexes that are not absorbed. The offenders are milk and yoghurt, antacids, iron and zinc supplements, and some mineral waters. This is the single most consequential food interaction in outpatient practice, and the numbers are large: taken with milk, ciprofloxacin's peak fell 36% and with yoghurt 47%, and bioavailability dropped about a third (Neuvonen et al. 1991). An aluminium-magnesium antacid taken minutes before dropped ciprofloxacin's relative bioavailability to about 15% (Nix et al. 1989): most of the dose was simply lost.
  • Adherence as a PK variable. A missed dose is a planned trough, a guaranteed excursion into the window. Stopping when symptoms ease, splitting one course across two illnesses, and buying three days of a seven-day course, a real pattern where antibiotics are sold loose by the strip, are all ways of widening the time inside the window.

The rules go in opposite directions for different drugs, which is why "take with food" and "take on an empty stomach" both appear on real labels:

  • Fasting helps or is required: rifampicin (a meal cut its peak 36% with little change in total exposure, and rifampicin resistance is a single-step rpoB change, so the lost peak matters; Peloquin et al. 1999), isoniazid (peak cut about half with food), penicillin V, ampicillin, azithromycin capsules, tetracycline.
  • Food helps or is required: nitrofurantoin (food raises bioavailability by about 40%, per the label), cefuroxime axetil (needs gastric acid to hydrolyse its ester prodrug, bioavailability about 37% fasting rising to 52% with food), cefpodoxime proxetil, the lipophilic anthelmintics like albendazole.
  • Food is for tolerance, not pharmacokinetics: amoxicillin-clavulanate and metronidazole are taken with food chiefly to reduce nausea. That is an adherence argument, a patient who feels less sick finishes the course, not a claim that food improves absorption. The two reasons are worth keeping separate.

The "always finish the course" question

This deserves care, because the honest answer is neither the old slogan nor its glib reversal. The instruction to finish every course was never built on evidence for most infections, a point argued forcefully by Llewelyn and colleagues in the BMJ in 2017. For a growing list of conditions, community-acquired pneumonia, uncomplicated urinary infection, intra-abdominal infection after the source is controlled, cellulitis, shorter courses are non-inferior to longer ones and expose the body's bacteria to less drug for less time probable. Since most resistance is selected not at the infection site but in the gut flora (section 07), a shorter course is also a smaller selective event in the place that matters most.

But this is not universal, and saying otherwise is dangerous. Tuberculosis, infective endocarditis, osteomyelitis and S. aureus bloodstream infection genuinely need long courses; stopping early in these risks relapse and, in TB, actively breeds resistance. The defensible formulation is the one worth remembering: take the course your clinician prescribed. The real argument is about what length gets prescribed, which is a question for prescribers and trials, not for patients improvising mid-course. Shortening courses is a change to make through the people who write them.

06

Where the drug does and does not go

Plasma is a poor proxy for most sites of infection. The window can open in a sanctuary while the blood level looks fine.

A drug kills where it reaches an adequate concentration, and that is the concentration at the site of infection, not in the blood. For many sites the two differ by an order of magnitude or more. The body map below is the counterpart to the cell map of section 01: click a compartment to see how much drug gets there, which organisms live there, and whether the local concentration can plausibly sit inside the selection window while the plasma level reads therapeutic.

Seven compartments show the principle:

  • CSF and meninges. The blood-brain barrier keeps most drugs out, and inflammation changes everything: inflamed meninges leak, so ceftriaxone reaches treating concentrations in meningitis that it could not reach in a healthy brain. First-generation cephalosporins and aminoglycosides never get there in useful amounts, which is why they are not used for meningitis.
  • Bone. Perfusion is poor and dead bone has none, so osteomyelitis is treated for weeks, not days, and often needs surgery to remove the dead bone the drug cannot reach.
  • Prostate. A lipid membrane and a pH gradient trap weakly basic, lipophilic drugs inside. Fluoroquinolones and trimethoprim concentrate there; β-lactams largely do not, which shapes how prostatitis is treated.
  • Lung. What matters is the epithelial lining fluid. Macrolides and linezolid concentrate in it well above plasma; aminoglycosides penetrate it poorly, part of why inhaled formulations exist for some lung infections.
  • Urine. The inverse case. Nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin reach urinary concentrations orders of magnitude above plasma because that is where they are excreted. This is exactly why nitrofurantoin cures cystitis and is useless for pyelonephritis or bloodstream infection: it is barely present anywhere but the bladder.
  • Abscess. Low pH, low oxygen and a huge bacterial density. Many drugs lose potency in acid; aminoglycosides need oxygen to be taken up. And at high cell numbers a β-lactamase-producing population raises the local enzyme concentration enough to destroy the drug, the inoculum effect. This is why draining an abscess beats any antibiotic: source control removes the bacteria the drug cannot reach.
  • Biofilm and intracellular niches. In a biofilm, matrix slows diffusion and the deep cells are metabolically stalled, so measured MICs run 10 to 1000 times the free-floating value (approximate, organism-dependent). This is the world of prosthetic joints, catheters, the cystic-fibrosis lung and heart-valve vegetations. Inside human cells, Salmonella, Legionella and Mycobacterium hide from drugs that cannot cross the host membrane, which is why cell-wall agents fail against them.

Each of these is a place where local concentration can sit squarely inside the selection window while the blood level looks perfectly therapeutic. The curve in the simulator was plasma. The infection is somewhere the plasma curve does not describe.

07

The gut is the reactor

The least intuitive point on the page. The organism you treat is almost never the one in which resistance is selected.

Picture the scale. A urinary infection might hold 10⁸ bacteria. The colon holds on the order of 10¹¹ bacteria per gram of contents, across hundreds of species, which is several orders of magnitude more bacteria than any infection you are treating. It is the largest bacterial population in the body by far, and it is the one most antibiotics pass through.

They arrive there two ways. Oral drug that is not fully absorbed continues down the gut. And drug cleared into bile, a large fraction for some drugs, is delivered straight into the intestine. By the time it reaches the colon the concentration has usually fallen to the sub-inhibitory-to-inhibitory range: squarely window territory, applied to the densest and most diverse bacterial community in the body. So the uncomfortable conclusion:

The organism you are treating is almost never the organism in which resistance is selected.

You dose for a bladder infection. The selection happens in the colon, among bacteria that were never infecting you.

Colonisation resistance, and what a course costs

A healthy gut community resists invaders through what is called colonisation resistance: it consumes the nutrients a newcomer would need, transforms bile acids into forms that inhibit pathogens, produces short-chain fatty acids that lower pH and feed the gut lining, and maintains the mucus layer. A broad-spectrum antibiotic degrades all of this at once. Into the cleared ground, resistant and opportunistic organisms expand.

The clearest consequence is Clostridioides difficile. Clear enough space in the gut flora and C. difficile, intrinsically resistant to many antibiotics, blooms and releases toxins that inflame the colon. Clindamycin and fluoroquinolones are classic triggers. The treatment is itself an antibiotic (vancomycin or fidaxomicin), which is the paradox in miniature: an antibiotic-caused disease treated with more antibiotic. When that fails repeatedly, the fix is ecological rather than pharmacological, a faecal microbiota transplant to reinstall a functioning community.

Recovery is incomplete, and leaves a mark

The flora is resilient but not perfectly so. Palleja and colleagues gave twelve healthy men a four-day course of three last-resort drugs (meropenem, gentamicin, vancomycin) and followed their gut flora for six months (Palleja et al. 2018). The community recovered its broad composition in about a month and a half, but nine common species were still undetectable in most of the men at six months, and carriers of β-lactam resistance genes were positively selected during and after the course. The infection was long gone; the gut resistome, the reservoir of resistance genes it enriched, outlasted it by months. Every course leaves the gut a little more enriched for resistance than it found it, and that reservoir is what the next infection, or the next person, can draw on.

08

Tolerance, persistence, and the thing MIC cannot measure

A population can survive a drug without its MIC changing at all. And surviving is the first step to resisting.

The MIC captures one thing: the concentration needed to stop growth. It is blind to a second thing that matters just as much: how fast a drug kills. Three distinct phenomena hide in that blind spot.

  • Resistance is the ability to grow at a higher drug concentration. The MIC rises. This is everything in sections 03 and 04.
  • Tolerance is dying more slowly at a concentration that would normally kill. The MIC is unchanged; the killing kinetics change. A tolerant population is eventually killed, but it takes much longer.
  • Persistence is a small subpopulation entering a dormant, low-metabolism state in which the drug has almost nothing to act on. When the drug clears, the survivors wake and regrow. The MIC of those survivors is normal; they simply waited the drug out.

To measure what the MIC misses, Balaban and colleagues proposed MDK₉₉, the minimum duration of killing: the time a drug needs to kill 99% of a population at a given concentration. Two strains with identical MICs can have wildly different MDK₉₉ values, and the difference is invisible to a standard susceptibility test.

→

Tolerance is a stepping stone to resistance

This is the clinically important sequence, shown by Levin-Reisman and colleagues in Science in 2017. In repeated antibiotic pulses, bacteria first evolved tolerance, simply surviving longer, and only then, from that surviving population, did true resistance mutations appear and sweep. Tolerance comes first because it is easy, often a single change that slows growth, and it buys time: a population that survives longer under the drug has more opportunities to acquire or receive a resistance gene. Tolerance is cheap, and it is the on-ramp well-established.

There is a thread connecting this to the whole page. Bactericidal drugs mostly need their targets to be active: β-lactams need cells building wall, fluoroquinolones need replicating DNA, aminoglycosides need active translation. A dormant persister offers none of these, which is precisely why the slow-growing deep cells of a biofilm (section 06) and the starved cells of an abscess are so hard to kill, and why those sites give resistance the time it needs to evolve. The window is not only a band of concentration. It is also a span of time, and tolerance is how bacteria buy more of it.

09

The named enemies

A short deck of the organisms that concentrate the problem, and what each has left to treat it.

Two shorthand lists organise clinical worry. ESKAPE names six organisms that together cause much of hospital-acquired resistant infection: Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterobacter species. The acronym is a pun: these are the organisms that escape. The WHO's Bacterial Priority Pathogens List, updated in May 2024, is the formal version: 24 pathogens across 15 families, sorted into critical, high and medium tiers to steer research and investment.

The 2024 revision moved things. Carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa dropped from critical to high, reflecting reported global declines. Third-generation-cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales became a standalone critical entry. Fluoroquinolone-resistant Salmonella Typhi and Shigella sit in the high tier, and two newcomers, macrolide-resistant Group A streptococci and penicillin-resistant Group B streptococci, enter at medium. Filter the deck below by tier, Gram stain or mechanism.

Five case files

MRSA. Methicillin resistance arrived on a mobile element called SCCmec, carrying mecA and its low-affinity PBP2a. For decades MRSA was a hospital organism; then a distinct community lineage emerged (USA300, often carrying the Panton-Valentine leukocidin toxin) that spread among healthy people. There is a genuine success buried here: sustained infection control drove MRSA bloodstream infections down markedly in several countries. England's combined MRSA bacteraemia rate fell about 54% between 2009 and 2018 (UKHSA surveillance). Resistance is not always a one-way ratchet.

VRE. Vancomycin-resistant enterococci carry the vanA operon from section 03. In Europe the leading hypothesis for its emergence is agricultural: avoparcin, a glycopeptide close to vancomycin, was used as a growth promoter in livestock for years, plausibly selecting vanA in animal enterococci that then reached people probable.

CRE and NDM-1. Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales are the organisms the critical tier is built around. NDM-1 is a metallo-β-lactamase: it uses zinc to hydrolyse almost every β-lactam including the carbapenems, our usual last resort. Two features make it especially hard. It is plasmid-borne, so it spreads horizontally between species. And because it is a metallo-enzyme rather than a serine one, the new serine-β-lactamase inhibitors like avibactam do not touch it: avibactam is built to jam a serine active site, and NDM-1 has zinc and water instead. The chemistry of the enzyme decides which new drug can work, which is why the one agent with useful activity, cefiderocol, works by a completely different route (section 11). NDM-1 leaves monobactams such as aztreonam standing, which is the basis of the aztreonam-avibactam combination now used against it.

Drug-resistant tuberculosis. TB is its own universe. Resistance is chromosomal (rpoB for rifampicin, katG and inhA for isoniazid) and treatment historically meant 18–24 months of multiple drugs. The shift here is real and recent: the BPaLM regimen (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, moxifloxacin) has cut treatment of most drug-resistant TB to six months, now WHO-recommended. It is one of the few places the clock has run backwards well-established.

Drug-resistant gonorrhoea. Neisseria gonorrhoeae is a slow march through the formulary: sulfonamides, then penicillin, then tetracyclines, then fluoroquinolones, each abandoned as resistance rose, leaving injectable ceftriaxone as effectively the last reliable option, with ceftriaxone-resistant strains now reported. The one hopeful note is late: in December 2025 two new oral agents, zoliflodacin and gepotidacin, were approved for gonorrhoea, the first new oral options in nearly forty years (US FDA, Dec 2025).

10

The India chapter

Written from Hyderabad, with precision and without either defensiveness or sensationalism.

A gene named after a city

NDM stands for New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase. It was named after the city linked to the first patient it was characterised from, a 2008 case with a New Delhi hospital history, reported by Yong and colleagues in 2009 and brought to wide attention by a 2010 Lancet Infectious Diseases paper (Kumarasamy et al. 2010). The naming caused a diplomatic row, and the scientific objection behind the politics was sound: tagging a mobile resistance gene with a place implies an origin the biology does not support. These genes move across borders on plasmids; a place name is epidemiologically misleading and unfairly stigmatising. The convention has largely been abandoned since.

More people in India die from lack of access to effective antibiotics than from resistance to them.

Any stewardship message that forgets this is wrong for most of the country. Access first.

Access before excess

This is the central tension, and it must be stated plainly: the dominant antibiotic problem in much of India is too little effective treatment, not too much. A 2025 modelling study in The Lancet Infectious Diseases estimated that across eight low- and middle-income countries in 2019, of roughly 1.5 million carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative infections, only 6.9% were treated with an appropriate antibiotic (GARDP / Lancet Infect Dis 2025). India carried the largest share of those infections, on the order of a million, and procured about 80% of the eight-country total of appropriate courses, yet still treated only around 8% of its own resistant infections adequately. A patient who dies because the right drug never reached them is as dead as one who dies of resistance, and there are more of the former. Stewardship and access are not opposites here; both are failures of the same health system.

The excess, where it is real

  • Over-the-counter sales. Many antibiotics are dispensed without the prescription that Schedule H1 of the drug rules requires, often sold loose by the strip rather than as a full course. The Red Line campaign marked prescription-only medicines with a red stripe on the pack to curb this; awareness remains limited.
  • Irrational fixed-dose combinations. India's market carries an unusual number of antibiotic combination products never properly approved. One analysis found 118 antibiotic FDC formulations on sale, of which about two-thirds had no record of central approval (McGettigan et al. 2019). The government banned 344 FDCs in 2016 and a further 328 in 2018; court challenges and the sheer number of formulations have made enforcement slow.
  • Agriculture. Colistin, a last-resort human drug, was used as a growth promoter in poultry. India banned its manufacture, sale and use in food-producing animals in July 2019, a genuinely significant step. The plasmid-borne colistin-resistance gene mcr-1 itself was first reported from pig farming in China (Liu et al. 2016), the clearest single example of agricultural use producing a mobile human-relevant resistance gene.

The production side, on Hyderabad's doorstep

There is a selection pressure most global coverage ignores entirely, and it sits in the industrial belt northwest of this city. Patancheru-Bollaram hosts a large concentration of bulk-drug manufacturers whose waste once flowed to a common treatment plant. When Larsson and colleagues measured the treated effluent, they found the highest pharmaceutical concentrations ever recorded in any effluent: fluoroquinolones up to about 31 mg/L, dominated by ciprofloxacin, a level toxic to many bacteria and higher than the concentration in the blood of a treated patient (Larsson et al. 2007). A single treatment plant was discharging, by one estimate, the equivalent of a large country's daily ciprofloxacin use.

The consequences are documented downstream. Sediment below the discharge carried resistance genes at a scale not seen at control sites: the gene sul2 enriched roughly 66-fold, class 1 integron-integrase genes about 7-fold, and a background of mobile plasmids and transposases, the exact machinery of section 04 (Kristiansson et al. 2011). A 2017 survey found antimicrobials in every environmental sample tested, with one azole antifungal reaching an extraordinary 237 mg/L in an industrial sewer, and resistant Enterobacteriaceae carrying blaNDM, blaKPC and blaOXA-48 in more than 95% of samples (Lübbert et al. 2017). This is the selection window of section 05, opened not in a patient but in a river, at industrial scale, continuously. It is a reminder that a prescription is only one of the ways a selective concentration gets into the world.

What the surveillance shows

India's clearest picture comes from ICMR's AMR Surveillance Network. Its 2024 report, covering about 99,000 culture-positive isolates from tertiary-care hospitals, shows the trajectory. In Klebsiella pneumoniae, susceptibility to imipenem fell from 58.5% in 2017 to 31.2% in 2024; in E. coli, imipenem susceptibility fell from 81.4% to 57.6% over the same period (ICMR AMRSN 2024). In other words, for two of the commonest Gram-negative infections, a carbapenem, one of the drugs held in reserve, now fails in a third to a half of hospital isolates. Resistance to meropenem in Acinetobacter baumannii reached 91%. These are tertiary-hospital figures and should not be read as community rates, a caveat the report states itself, but the direction is unambiguous.

11

Why the pipeline is nearly empty

The science is hard and the economics are worse. A new antibiotic is a product designed to be used as little as possible.

The discovery void

The golden age ran from the 1940s to the early 1960s, when screening soil microbes against each other (the Waksman platform) turned up class after class: aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides, glycopeptides. Then it stopped. No structurally new class active against Gram-negative bacteria reached the clinic for decades. The reasons are specific. The easy soil targets were exhausted; keep screening the same way and you keep rediscovering the same molecules. When the industry pivoted in the 1990s to target-based screening, picking an essential gene and hunting for inhibitors against the purified protein, it largely failed, because a molecule that inhibits a protein in a test tube usually cannot cross the Gram-negative envelope of section 01 to reach that protein in a living cell. Medicinal chemistry's usual toolkit for improving a drug, making it larger and more lipophilic, pushes compounds in exactly the wrong direction for the eNTRy rules. The barrier that makes Gram-negatives dangerous is the same barrier that defeats discovery.

The economics are the deeper problem

Suppose you solve the science. You now own a drug that, if it is to stay useful, must be held in reserve and used as little as possible; that is given for days, not for life; and that is priced against generic competitors in its class. Every incentive is inverted. A drug for a chronic disease earns more each year a patient stays on it; a reserve antibiotic earns the least precisely when it is being best managed.

The canonical case is Achaogen. It won FDA approval for plazomicin, an aminoglycoside active against some resistant Gram-negatives, in mid-2018, and filed for bankruptcy about a year later, in 2019. A company can bring a genuinely useful new antibiotic to market and fail anyway, because the market rewards volume and a good steward sells little. Several small antibiotics companies have followed the same path. The net present value of an antibiotic asset simply does not compete with an oncology or chronic-disease asset, so the large companies that could absorb the risk have mostly left the field.

What is being tried

Push incentives fund the research directly, before a drug exists: CARB-X, BARDA and the industry-backed AMR Action Fund underwrite early development to share the risk. They help molecules get discovered; they do nothing about the empty market a successful molecule faces.

Pull incentives fix the reward. The idea that has moved furthest is the subscription or "Netflix" model: a health system pays a fixed annual fee for access to a drug, decoupled from how much is used, so the company is paid to have the drug available and to steward it, not to sell more. England's NHS has run exactly this since 2022, paying flat annual fees for cefiderocol and ceftazidime-avibactam, and has been expanding it toward a broader scheme worth up to £20m per product per year. In the United States the PASTEUR Act proposes a national version; it has been introduced repeatedly, most recently in February 2026, and as of this writing has not been enacted contested. The pull side is where the real experiment is, and the verdict is not yet in.

12

What works, and what is still unknown

Two honest lists. The second is not a token paragraph.

Filter the panel below to separate what the evidence supports from what it genuinely does not yet settle. The unresolved questions are not rhetorical hedges; they are places where serious people disagree and the data are thin.

Works, or clearly promising

Stewardship. The disciplines are unglamorous and they work: start narrow rather than broad, de-escalate to a targeted drug once cultures return, use the shortest evidence-supported duration, switch from intravenous to oral as soon as the patient can take it, and set an explicit stop date. Each shortens the time any population spends in the window.

Rapid diagnostics may be the real bottleneck. Conventional culture and susceptibility testing takes 48–72 hours. Everything prescribed in that window is a guess, and the safe guess is broad-spectrum, which is exactly the selective pressure to avoid. Faster methods, molecular panels, MALDI-TOF identification, direct-from-blood sequencing, let the drug be narrowed sooner. Shrinking the empiric window may do more than any single new drug.

Vaccines are an underrated resistance intervention. An infection prevented needs no antibiotic, and a pathogen that never grows cannot be selected. A WHO analysis in 2024 estimated that wider use of existing vaccines against pneumococcus, Hib and typhoid could avert roughly 106,000 resistance-associated deaths a year, and new vaccines against TB and Klebsiella a further half-million; vaccines could cut global antibiotic use by about 22%, some 2.5 billion daily doses a year (WHO 2024). The effect is concrete: when an extensively drug-resistant typhoid strain spread in Hyderabad, Pakistan from 2016, the typhoid conjugate vaccine proved about 95% effective against confirmed typhoid and 97% against the resistant strain specifically (Yousafzai et al., Lancet Glob Health 2021). Influenza vaccination even helps indirectly, by cutting the febrile illnesses that draw inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions.

Infection prevention, hand hygiene, water and sanitation. In high-burden settings these dominate everything else. A gut infection not transmitted is a course of antibiotics not given and a resistance gene not passed on. The England MRSA decline of section 09 was built on infection control, not a new drug.

New chemistry, buying time. The β-lactamase-inhibitor combinations extend old drugs against new enzymes: ceftazidime-avibactam, meropenem-vaborbactam, imipenem-relebactam. Their limits are instructive, avibactam does nothing against the metallo-enzymes like NDM-1, as section 09 explained. The most elegant recent agent is cefiderocol, a cephalosporin disguised as an iron-chelating siderophore: the bacterium's own iron-uptake machinery actively imports it, a Trojan horse through the very envelope that defeats other drugs. Newer-mechanism agents in development include gepotidacin and zoliflodacin, both now approved for gonorrhoea.

Beyond antibiotics. Phage therapy uses viruses that kill bacteria, with real obstacles: each phage hits a narrow host range, the regulatory framework fits poorly, and bacteria evolve phage resistance too. Monoclonal antibodies, anti-virulence agents that disarm rather than kill (removing the selective pressure to resist), CRISPR-based antimicrobials targeted at specific resistance sequences, and microbiome restoration are all in earlier development.

Honestly unresolved

These are not settled, and pretending otherwise would betray the whole point of the page.

  • Does reducing antibiotic use reverse resistance at the population level? Sometimes, partly, slowly, and sometimes not at all. Compensatory evolution (section 04) means the fitness cost that should drive reversal often disappears. Restriction reliably slows new resistance; whether it rolls back existing resistance is genuinely mixed contested.
  • Is the selection-window framework clinically actionable? The concept is well supported in the lab. Whether the MPC predicts patient outcomes, and whether dosing to stay above the window helps in practice, is not established contested.
  • Do antibiotic cycling or mixing strategies in ICUs work? Rotating drugs to deny resistance a stable target is appealing in theory; the trials have been largely disappointing contested.
  • How much do environmental and agricultural reservoirs actually contribute to human clinical resistance? The gene flow is documented (section 10). The quantitative attribution, what fraction of human resistance traces to a farm or a river, is contested and probably unknowable with current methods contested.
  • Can collateral sensitivity be exploited? Resistance to one drug sometimes creates vulnerability to another. Whether that can be turned into reliable drug-cycling therapy, or is too strain-specific to use, is open contested.
  • Will phage therapy scale? It works in compassionate-use case reports. Whether it becomes a routine, regulated therapy rather than a last resort is unknown contested.
END

The page began with a claim: resistance is something you cultivate when you attack bacteria badly. Every section since has been a way of attacking badly, dosing into the window, letting the gut do the selecting, treating a site the plasma level does not describe, releasing the drug into a river. The encouraging implication is the mirror image. Most of these are choices, at the level of the prescription, the health system and the factory. The genes are ancient and beyond recall. The selection pressure is ours to govern.
