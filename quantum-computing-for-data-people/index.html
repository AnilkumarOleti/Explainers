Explainer · for SQL, stats & ML people

Quantum computing is the art of making wrong answers cancel out.

The background is a ripple tank: two sources whose waves add up in some places and cancel in others. Keep that picture in mind. Nearly everything a quantum computer does, and everything it can't do, comes from it.

01 — THE BIG PICTURE · ~5 MIN

A computer that calculates with waves, not switches

A normal computer stores information in bits, tiny switches that are either 0 or 1. A quantum computer stores information in qubits (quantum bits). A qubit is still read out as 0 or 1 in the end, but while the computation runs it behaves more like a wave than a switch.

For a data person, the most useful way to think about it is this: a quantum computer is a machine for engineering probability distributions. You don't get an answer printed out. You set up a process so the distribution you sample from at the end puts most of its mass on the right answer. The four ideas below explain how.

Qubit

A bit is a coin lying on the table, heads or tails. A qubit is a coin still spinning. It carries information about both faces, and it settles to one face only when you look (measurement).

Superposition

A qubit can be in a blend of 0 and 1, the way a musical chord is a blend of notes. Each possibility carries an amplitude, a number like a probability except that it can be negative. That one difference is where everything below comes from.

Entanglement

Two qubits can share one joint state that can't be split into “qubit A's state” and “qubit B's state”. Think of a pair of gloves sent to two cities: open one box, and you know what's in the other. With n qubits, the joint state has 2n amplitudes. That's a table of 2n numbers that you can never read in full.

Interference

This is the one that matters most. Amplitudes add like waves. A +0.5 and a −0.5 for the same outcome cancel to zero, and two +0.5s reinforce. A quantum algorithm is a carefully choreographed set of steps that makes wrong answers cancel and right answers reinforce, so measurement lands on the right one with high probability.

The one mental model to keep Noise-cancelling headphones don't “listen harder”. They play an inverted wave so the noise disappears. A quantum computer doesn't try every answer and pick the best (that's the myth). It sets up waves so the bad answers erase each other. If a problem has no structure you can use to arrange that cancellation, quantum gives you nothing.

“Just a faster computer”? No. It's a different kind of instrument.

The common myth is that because n qubits hold 2n possibilities at once, a quantum computer “tries every answer in parallel”. Here's the catch: when you measure, you get one sample. One row out of a 2n-row distribution. All that hidden parallelism is useless unless interference has already concentrated the probability on the answer you want. Only some problems have the mathematical structure that allows this.

A better analogy than “faster CPU” is “GPU, but much narrower”. A GPU doesn't speed up your SQL query parser. It speeds up one shape of work, dense parallel linear algebra. A quantum processor (QPU) is a coprocessor for an even narrower shape: problems about quantum systems, plus a few mathematical problems with hidden periodic or algebraic structure.

02 — QUANTUM VS CLASSICAL DATA PROCESSING

Why your warehouse isn't going quantum

Here's the uncomfortable fact for analytics: quantum computers are good at heavy computation on small inputs, and analytics is mostly light computation on huge inputs. Those two shapes don't overlap much.

Bottleneck 1: getting data in

Before a quantum algorithm can process your 1-billion-row table, the data has to be encoded into qubit amplitudes. That's called state preparation or data loading. Writing N numbers into a quantum state takes about N operations on a generic device. Many textbook quantum ML speedups quietly assume a device called QRAM (quantum random-access memory) that would load data almost instantly. QRAM at scale has never been built, and it's an open question whether it ever will be.

If loading costs N steps, an algorithm that then runs in √N or log N steps doesn't matter: you've already paid N. It's like a supersonic jet that takes three days to board.

Bottleneck 2: no storage, and you can't read the answer out

Qubits are not storage. They hold their state for milliseconds to seconds and then decohere (more in Section 5). There's no “quantum disk”. Reading out is also lossy: each run gives one bitstring, so estimating a vector of N output values takes many repeated runs. A quantum computer can “hold” a 250-entry vector, but you can only ever learn a few summary numbers about it per run.

Rule of thumb Quantum wins when the input is small, the output is small, and the hard part in between grows exponentially for classical machines. Example: a molecule's description is a few kilobytes, the answer is one energy number, and the classical cost in between blows up. A GROUP BY over 4 TB is the exact opposite.

The algorithm families, and what each could plausibly speed up

Speedups come in two flavours. A quadratic speedup turns N steps into √N: a million becomes a thousand. An exponential speedup turns 2n into something like n3, which is the difference between “never” and “Tuesday”. The catch is that error correction makes every quantum step thousands of times slower than a classical step. In 2021 a Google team argued that quadratic speedups therefore probably won't produce real advantage on early fault-tolerant machines.

A data scientist knows the dequantization story already. In 2018 an undergraduate, Ewin Tang, showed that a celebrated “exponentially faster” quantum recommendation algorithm had a classical counterpart. The trick was that the quantum version assumed a data structure which, if you actually had it, also lets a classical computer do length-squared sampling: random sampling of rows in proportion to their size. Same idea as importance sampling. Much of quantum ML's apparent advantage on classical data disappeared in a similar way.

What a quantum-assisted analytics stack would actually look like

In any realistic design, the QPU is a remote accelerator called for one small, expensive subproblem, like calling a GPU kernel or an external solver API. Everything else stays classical. Data loading happens once, on a compressed problem description, never on the raw table.

That's the pattern in the HSBC–IBM bond experiment. Historical data was passed through a quantum feature map offline, and the live trading system used the enriched dataset with no quantum computer in the loop. For the foreseeable future, quantum shows up in analytics as one more column in your feature store, not as a new database.

Key takeaways · Section 2

  • The data-loading bottleneck and the lack of quantum storage rule out quantum for most scan/join/aggregate work. Proven in the sense that no workaround exists today.
  • Most quantum ML speedups on classical data were dequantized or assume QRAM, which hasn't been built.
  • Quadratic speedups (Grover, amplitude estimation) probably need much better error correction before they beat classical hardware.
  • The realistic stack is hybrid: the classical side compresses the problem, the QPU handles a tiny hard core, and the results come back as features or estimates with confidence intervals.
03 — SQL VS QUANTUM PROGRAMMING

Where quantum meets the database

SQL declares operations over stored relations, and quantum computers don't store relations (Section 2). So quantum work touches databases at three points that sit next to SQL, not inside it: search over computable spaces, optimization sub-steps such as query planning, and quantum programs written in Python SDKs.

1. Grover-style “database search”

Grover's algorithm is often described as “searching an unsorted database of N items in √N steps.” The word “database” is misleading. Grover searches an oracle, a circuit that can compute whether a candidate is the answer. If your “database” is a real table on disk, building that oracle means encoding the whole table into the circuit, which costs N operations. The √N advantage is gone before you start. And SQL already wins with indexes: a B-tree lookup is log N, far better than √N.

Grover is genuinely useful when the search space is implicit: “find the input x where f(x) is true”, with f cheap to compute and the space of x astronomically large (cryptographic keys, constraint satisfaction). That's a search over possibilities, not over records.

2. Quantum-accelerated sub-steps: joins, query planning, optimization

The most serious “quantum for databases” research doesn't run queries. It targets the query optimizer. Choosing a join order is an NP-hard combinatorial problem (the number of possible orders grows explosively with the number of tables), so it can be rewritten as a QUBO (quadratic unconstrained binary optimization), the input format for quantum annealers and QAOA. Researchers have mapped join ordering, index selection and multi-query optimization onto quantum hardware. One group even built a PostgreSQL plugin where a trained variational circuit suggests join orders, though experiments covered only four tables on a simulator. A 2025 review of the area says plainly that a clear quantum advantage for database problems is still an open question.

Note the shape again: the optimizer's search space is huge, but its input (table statistics, a query graph) is tiny. That's why this is the right place to look, even though nobody has found a win yet. No advantage shown

3. How developers write quantum programs today

Quantum code today is written at roughly the level of assembly language: you place individual gates on individual qubits, usually from Python.

Side by side: “find the one listing that matches”

The same intent written two ways. The quantum version is a toy: the “table” has 8 rows, and the matching row (101) is hard-coded into the oracle. That is exactly the point. You have to build the answer-checker into the circuit.

Quantum · Qiskit (Python)imperative, gate-level
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.circuit.library import grover_operator
from qiskit.primitives import StatevectorSampler

# Oracle: flip the sign of |101⟩, the "row" we want
oracle = QuantumCircuit(3)
oracle.x(1)                       # 101 → 111
oracle.h(2); oracle.ccx(0, 1, 2); oracle.h(2)
oracle.x(1)                       # undo

qc = QuantumCircuit(3)
qc.h([0, 1, 2])    # superpose all 8 rows
G = grover_operator(oracle)
qc.compose(G, inplace=True)   # 2 steps ≈ optimal
qc.compose(G, inplace=True)   # for N = 8
qc.measure_all()

job = StatevectorSampler().run([qc], shots=1000)
print(job.result()[0].data.meas.get_counts())
# ≈ {'101': 945, '000': 8, '110': 7, ...}

  • Grover searches computable spaces, not tables. Against a real table, a classical index beats it easily.

Mostly the same job, with one new skill

Quantum won't replace the analytics stack. The bigger change is that analysts may become consumers of quantum outputs (simulation results, features, risk estimates) and a few will become translators who can tell which business problem has a quantum-shaped core.

05 — WHY THIS IS SUCH A WILD TECHNOLOGY

Counterintuitive physics, brutal engineering

The physics that breaks your intuition

Negative probabilities, sort of. In ordinary statistics, probabilities only add up: two ways to reach an outcome always make it more likely. In quantum mechanics, amplitudes can cancel, so two paths to an outcome can make it impossible. Nothing in classical probability theory works like this, and it's the core resource.

Looking changes things. Measuring a qubit forces it to a definite 0 or 1 and destroys the superposition. So you can't “peek” at intermediate results to debug, and you can't copy a qubit's state either (the no-cloning theorem). No print statements, no backups.

Exponential bookkeeping. Describing 300 entangled qubits fully would take 2300 numbers, more than there are atoms in the observable universe. Nature tracks this for free. That's why simulating nature on classical machines is so hard, and why Richard Feynman proposed building computers out of quantum parts in 1981.

The engineering that makes it hard

Decoherence. Any stray interaction (heat, a vibration, a cosmic ray, a neighbouring qubit) leaks quantum information into the environment and turns waves back into ordinary coin flips. Superconducting qubits are kept at around 10–15 millikelvin, colder than deep space, inside the familiar gold “chandelier” dilution refrigerators. Trapped-ion and neutral-atom machines hold single atoms in place with lasers in ultra-high vacuum.

Error correction. Today's best physical operations fail roughly once per thousand. Useful algorithms need billions of operations. The fix is to spread one logical qubit (reliable) across many physical qubits (noisy), and keep checking parities to detect errors without measuring the data directly. It's like RAID or a checksum, except you can't copy the data. The key milestone is being below threshold: adding more physical qubits makes the logical qubit better rather than worse. Google showed this on its Willow chip in late 2024.

Scaling. Each qubit needs control wiring, calibration and isolation, and all of these get harder as you add qubits. Controls drift, so machines are constantly recalibrated. The roadmaps are about modularity: linking chips or traps together instead of building one giant chip.

The gap between “advantage” demos and useful machines

“Quantum advantage” has become a crowded term, so read each claim on three axes:

  • Is the task useful? Google's 2019 random-circuit-sampling result was a benchmark with no application. Quantum Echoes and IBM's 2026 papers simulate physics, which is closer to useful but still scientific, not commercial.
  • Is it verifiable? If no classical computer can reproduce the answer, how do you know it's right? The 2025–26 wave is mostly about building that trust: repeatable outputs, cross-checks on other quantum machines, self-certified fidelity bounds.
  • Will it survive? Classical simulation researchers have repeatedly narrowed or erased earlier advantage claims after publication. IBM's open tracker exists so that challenge happens in public.

Also check what “logical qubit” means in each announcement. IBM's July 2026 “70 logical qubits”, for instance, used error detection and post-selection, which is not the fully fault-tolerant kind assumed in resource estimates for breaking encryption. The honest summary: Proven quantum machines now do some scientific computations that classical computers can't easily reproduce. Plausible fault-tolerant machines with hundreds of logical qubits arrive around 2029–2031. Not yet shown no quantum computer has beaten classical on a commercially meaningful business task.

The one deadline that is real: cryptography

Estimates of what it takes to break RSA-2048 keep falling, mostly from better algorithms rather than better hardware: about 20 million physical qubits in 2019, under 1 million in a May 2025 Google paper (running for under a week), and a February 2026 preprint claiming under 100,000 with newer codes (not yet validated). Because encrypted data can be stolen now and decrypted later, migration to post-quantum cryptography is urgent today, even if the machine is years away.

Key takeaways · Section 5

  • The hard part is keeping waves coherent long enough, which needs error correction with large qubit overhead.
  • 2024–2026 brought real progress: below-threshold error correction, logical qubits in the dozens, verifiable advantage claims.
  • Commercial advantage is not yet shown. Read every claim for usefulness, verifiability and durability.
  • Crypto migration is the one place where acting now is clearly justified.
06 — IS IT THE NEXT BIG UNLOCK AFTER AI?

Probably a complement, not a successor

AI is a general-purpose technology: it improves almost any task that involves data or language. Quantum computing, on current evidence, is a special-purpose technology with a few potentially enormous wins. Both can be transformative, but in very different shapes.

The case for “yes”
  • Exponential speedups for simulating nature are mathematically grounded, and chemistry, materials and drug design underpin trillions in economic value.
  • Error-correction progress in 2024–26 went faster than many expected: 10× improvements within months.
  • Shor's algorithm alone would reshape global security.
  • Money and talent are pouring in: public listings, government programmes, and big-tech labs on several hardware approaches at once.
The case for “no”
  • The speedups that matter commercially are narrow; most software and data work gains nothing.
  • Data loading and readout limits rule out big-data and most AI workloads.
  • Quadratic speedups may never pay for error-correction overhead.
  • Classical algorithms and AI keep improving and keep eroding claimed advantages. AI models are getting good at approximating quantum chemistry too.
  • Fault-tolerant roadmaps have slipped before.
The likely reality
  • A specialised accelerator in the HPC/cloud stack, next to GPUs, from the early 2030s.
  • AI → quantum is already real: AI calibrates chips, decodes errors and designs circuits. Proven
  • Quantum → AI: quantum simulations generate high-quality training data for chemistry and materials models. Plausible
  • Quantum speeding up LLM training or inference: Speculation, blocked by data loading.

What would need to be true for the bold claims to hold

  1. Error correction at scale: thousands of logical qubits with error rates around one in a billion or better, running for hours, with real-time decoding.
  2. Affordable overhead: far fewer physical qubits per logical one, and faster logical gates. This is what would make quadratic speedups matter.
  3. Exponential-advantage algorithms for valuable problems that survive the best classical and AI methods, not just a physics benchmark.
  4. A way around data loading: practical QRAM, or a steady supply of problems whose inputs are tiny and outputs are summary statistics.
  5. Economics: cost per useful answer below the classical alternative, including queueing and the engineers needed.

Items 1 and 2 are on credible, if ambitious, roadmaps. Items 3–5 are where the real uncertainty is, and they're what to watch.

Key takeaways · Section 6

  • AI is general-purpose; quantum is special-purpose with a few huge potential wins.
  • The two already combine: AI helps build quantum computers, and quantum may later feed AI with simulation data.
  • Judge bold claims against five conditions: scale, overhead, surviving algorithms, data loading, economics.
TL;DR

Five lines

  1. Quantum computers use interference of amplitudes so wrong answers cancel. They sample answers rather than “try everything”.
  2. They suit small input, huge hidden computation, small output, which is mostly simulating molecules and materials, plus breaking RSA.
  3. Data loading and the lack of storage mean SQL, BI and most ML stay classical; quantum helps only with small sub-problems like query optimization.
  4. 2026 has real, contested scientific advantage demos; fault-tolerant machines are targeted for ~2029; no business-task advantage yet.
  5. Treat it as a future coprocessor and a present-day security deadline, a complement to AI rather than its successor.
