Bengaluru, 1973 to 2026 · a visual story

The city that needed no fan

For most of the last century, Bengaluru lived under a roof of leaves. In fifty years it has lost most of that roof. This is the story of the trees that held it up, the work they did every day, and the city that is left without them.

Chapter1

Under the rain trees

Picture an old Bengaluru road at five on a summer evening, fifty years ago.

The rain trees on either side have grown until their crowns meet overhead. Their leaflets are starting to fold. A rain tree closes its leaves at dusk and under cloud, and lets the rain fall straight through to the ground below. That habit is the usual explanation for its name. What light still reaches the road comes down in coins.

A flower seller has set her basket down under the widest crown. Down the road, a lake holds the last of the sky, and a pond heron waits at its edge. The air under the trees is cooler than at the open junction a block away. Nobody on this road is thinking about the weather, which was always the point of Bengaluru’s weather.

People called it the Garden City, and the Pensioners’ Paradise. Long-time residents still say their homes had no ceiling fans.2

A canopy planted on purpose

None of it was an accident. Kempegowda, who founded the city, planted shade trees and set water fountains at road junctions.3 Hyder Ali began Lalbagh in 1760, and Cubbon Park followed in 1864.4 In the early 1900s, the Mysore Maharaja’s German horticulturist, Gustav Krumbiegel, chose avenue trees so that some part of the city would be in flower in every season. He took the idea from Kalidasa’s poem of the seasons, the Ritusamhara.3 Many of the rain trees that still line the Cantonment are part of his legacy.5

Then and now

On the numbers. How much green is left depends on who is counting, and where the boundary sits. IISc’s summaries describe an 88% loss of vegetation since 1973, which matches its maps from the mid-2010s.7 Its 2020 table gives built-up land as 83%, though the same table’s hectare figures work out to 93% and its summary text cites 86.6%.1 A 2025 study in IMD’s journal Mausam, covering the wider 2,195 km² urban district, found vegetation fell from 50% to 24% between 1992 and 2022.8 Satellite pixels this coarse also miss many single street trees.

Every study agrees on the direction.

1 tree for every 7 people

IISc’s estimate is about 1.5 million trees for 9.5 million residents. By its reckoning that is not even enough to absorb the carbon dioxide those residents breathe out.1

A city of lakes, drained

Bengaluru sits on a plateau far from any big river. For centuries it lived on its kere, man-made lakes built in chains so that each one spilled into the next and the city could hold on to its monsoon.

In 1961 the city had 262 lakes. About 81 survive, and only around 34 of those are still living water bodies.9,10 Many of the rest became places you may have stood in: Kanteerava Stadium, the National Games Village, city bus stands.11

Chapter3

How it happened

Nobody decided to cut down the Garden City. It went one road, one layout and one lake bed at a time, usually for something people needed.

The city’s population grew fifty-fold in a century, from about 1.6 lakh people in 1901 to 85 lakh in 2011.13 Its mild weather and tree-lined streets were part of the draw. From the mid-1990s, software companies turned it into India’s technology capital, and the people who came needed homes, offices and roads.2

484 acresof 41 lakes, taken by the city’s own development authority

Lakes became layouts

As the city spread, lake beds were drained and built on, often by public agencies. A legislature committee found the Bangalore Development Authority itself had encroached on 41 lakes.12

Why it happened: a flood of people needed housing, and lake beds were public land that could be handed out quickly.

2011the year a court-appointed commission raised the alarm

Offices on the floodplain

The Outer Ring Road grew into the city’s IT corridor. The Patil Commission, set up on the High Court’s orders, warned that parts of it, including Agara, Ibbalur and Mahadevapura, had been built on lake land.14

Why it happened: the technology boom brought jobs, and it needed land fast.

245 treesmarked for felling on one road, Jayamahal Road, in 2017

Wider roads

Every road widening takes out a row of trees. On Jayamahal Road the corporation marked 245 trees and planned to fell 112 in the first phase.15

Why it happened: congestion. Widening was the quickest fix on offer.

6,868 treesaffected by Metro Phase 3 alone

The Metro

Phase 2 of Namma Metro removed 4,067 trees, and the Silk Board to KR Puram line sought to clear 2,115 more. Only about a quarter were marked to be moved rather than felled.16 Of the trees in Phase 3’s path, 65% are to be felled, 15% transplanted and 20% pruned.17

Why it happened: a metro line carries far more people than a road of the same width. The argument has mostly been about how many trees each line must take.

The flyover that was never built

In September 2016, Karnataka’s cabinet approved a 6.7 km steel flyover from Basaveshwara Circle to Hebbal, to speed traffic toward the airport. By the government’s own count it would cost ₹1,791 crore and 812 trees, among them mahogany, akasha mallige and pink tabebuia.18 The forest minister said it would “ease traffic on Ballari Road and help thousands of people every day.”19

Researchers from Azim Premji University and the citizen group Project Vruksha counted again. With a linked road widening, they put the loss at 2,244 trees.20 Citizens for Bengaluru argued that the flyover would serve about 50,000 people driving to the airport, while some 5 lakh commuters used buses.21

On 16 October 2016, more than 5,000 people held hands along the route in a human chain.21 Twelve days later the National Green Tribunal stayed the project, and in March 2017 the government scrapped it.22

Chapter4

What the weather did

A street tree does three jobs, all day and for free. It is a shade, a sponge and a sieve.

Shade

Leaves stop sunlight before it heats the road. They also sweat. Water pulled up from the soil evaporates from the leaves, and evaporation carries heat out of the air around them.

On Bengaluru roads, stretches under trees were up to 5.6°C cooler in the afternoon, and their asphalt up to 27.5°C cooler.38

Sponge

Rain hits the leaves first and reaches the ground slowly. Roots and fallen leaves keep the soil loose, so water sinks in and refills the ground. On concrete it runs straight to the drains.

More than 85% of the city’s surface is now paved.7

Sieve

Leaves catch dust on their surfaces and take in some gases through their pores. Rain then washes the dust down into the soil.

Tree-lined road stretches had up to 65% less sulphur dioxide. 80% of them kept dust within legal limits, while half the exposed roads came close to twice the limit.38

Hotter days, warmer nights

For 85 years, the city’s hottest day on record was 38.3°C, set on 30 April 1931. In April 2016, IMD’s observatory recorded 39.2°C.24 In 2024, after a March without a single rainy day, it reached 38.5°C on 28 April.33,34 The normal April maximum is 34.1°C.29

The long-term record does not show the city simply getting hotter every year, and the details matter. A comparison of 1960 to 1995 records, published in IMD’s journal Mausam, found that the Palace Road observatory, in the dense old centre, warmed faster than the HAL airport station on the eastern outskirts, most clearly in winter: 0.23°C a decade against 0.05°C.39 A 2025 study using IMD’s reanalysis data for 1980 to 2022 found the afternoons slightly cooler and the nights clearly warmer, while the city also grew wetter.8

Warm nights are what a heat island does. Concrete and asphalt soak up heat all day and give it back after dark. In the data, the cool nights are the part of Bengaluru’s climate that has changed most consistently.

From a satellite, the difference is starker still. In a 2017 image, parks and lakes had surface temperatures of 30–35°C. Airports, airfields and dried-up lake beds reached 44–51°C.40

Rain that comes all at once

Bengaluru now swings between extremes. 2022 was the wettest year in the city’s record, with 170.6 cm by mid-October, against a normal of about 108 cm for a whole year.31,29 May 2023 and May 2025 each set a record for the wettest May.41 In between, March 2024 passed without one rainy day.33 One reanalysis finds the city’s annual rain rising by about 11 mm a year since 1980.8

Whether the city’s own heat is changing its rain is still being studied. What happens when the rain lands is not in doubt. In the 24 hours to the morning of 5 September 2022, 131.6 mm fell.29 Six lakes overflowed, at least 75 places went under water and nearly 4,000 homes flooded.30 Companies on the Outer Ring Road put their losses at ₹225 crore. The corporation found 696 encroachments blocking the rajakaluves, the big stormwater channels that once carried overflow from lake to lake.42

The ground runs dry

The same concrete that floods in September starves the ground in March. Groundwater meets about 45% of the city’s demand, but with most of the surface sealed, little rain soaks back in.7 In March 2024 the state government said 6,900 of the city’s 13,900 borewells had run dry, some of them drilled to 1,500 feet.32

Chapter5

Neighbours with roots

A tree is easy to count as an obstacle and hard to count as a neighbour. Here are six kinds you have almost certainly walked under, and three trees the city knows by name.

How long does it take to replace one? Decades, if the sapling survives at all. “We should disabuse ourselves of the notion that the environmental and ecological benefits provided by a large mature tree can be easily replaced in a short time frame by planting a number of small saplings,” says the ecologist Harini Nagendra. Many urban saplings never reach maturity.44

Chapter6

The people underneath

Heat, flood and drought land hardest on people who work outdoors, and on the neighbourhoods at the end of the water line.

The neighbours we rarely see

Old trees are housing. Hornbills, barbets, owlets and parakeets nest in hollows that only old or decaying trees provide. Researchers following the city’s birds through citizen-science records found that how well a species copes with Bengaluru depends heavily on what it needs, such as tree holes and open ground.54

When researchers assessed the steel flyover route, they warned it would also affect the endangered slender loris, a small, big-eyed primate that comes out at night.20

Birds come back when water does. At Puttenahalli lake in JP Nagar, in the care of a residents’ trust since 2011, birders have recorded more than 125 species, including spot-billed pelicans and Indian darters. Jakkur lake, looked after with local fishers and the citizen group Jalaposhan, has recorded 197.28

Chapter7

What we can still do

The city cannot un-build itself. It can decide which trees stay, move some of the others, and plant as if it means it.

Moving a tree

When a tree stands in a project’s way, it can sometimes be dug out with its root ball and replanted elsewhere. The results have been uneven. When 10 trees were moved from the first Metro stretch on MG Road in 2007, three survived.27 A trust that followed later Metro transplants estimated that 70–80% survived, though officials kept no records.55 Specialists say more than 80% is achievable when the work is done carefully and the tree is watered and watched for three years.56 It costs anywhere from ₹1,200 to ₹3 lakh a tree.55

Planting a replacement

The standard promise is saplings. For Metro Phase 2, the metro corporation planted 15,845 of a targeted 17,540 saplings, but could not say how many were still alive.16 For Phase 3 it has promised ten native saplings for every tree felled, with five years of monitoring.17 Ten saplings do not add up to one old tree until they have grown for decades, and many never do.44

Chapter8

Look up

Go out this evening and find the oldest tree on your street. Stand under it for a minute.

It has been working since morning. It kept the sun off the road, drew water up from the ground and let it go as cooler air, caught the dust from passing traffic, and held a nest you have probably never noticed. Tomorrow it will do the same, and ask for nothing.

The city lost 19,268 trees in three years, about seventeen a day.23 It is still counting how many it has left, half a century after the law that asked it to.26

The people who planted Bengaluru’s avenues knew they would never sit in their full shade, and they planted anyway. The shade this city has in fifty years depends on what we keep, and what we plant, now.
