Explainer · Type 2 diabetes

The sugar-coated body

Type 2 diabetes begins as sugar that can't get into your cells. It becomes sugar that stays in your blood for years, sticking to proteins and wearing down every blood vessel it flows through.

The one idea to hold: some cells can lock their doors against sugar and some can't. When the lockable ones lock, the ones that can't get flooded. Eyes, kidneys, nerves and artery linings are the ones that can't.

About 7 of every 100 haemoglobin molecules in your red blood cells carry a glucose molecule stuck to them. That is what an HbA1c test counts: how sugar-coated your blood has been over the last 2–3 months. The colours come from Benedict's test, the old lab reagent that turns from blue to green, yellow, orange and brick-red as sugar rises.

Part 1 · How it starts

The key that stops turning

After a meal, glucose floods your blood. Your pancreas releases insulin, which works like a key: it unlocks doors on muscle, fat and liver cells so glucose can move inside and be burned or stored.

In Type 2 diabetes the locks get stiff. This is insulin resistance, and it builds quietly for a decade or more. The pancreas compensates by making more and more insulin, so blood sugar looks normal on tests for years. Diagnosis usually comes only when the insulin-making beta cells start to tire. By then, roughly half of their function is already gone.

Where the fat sits matters more than how much

The strongest driver of insulin resistance is fat stored in the wrong places. Fat under the skin is relatively harmless. Fat packed around the organs, inside the liver and inside the pancreas is not.

A fatty liver stops listening to insulin and keeps releasing glucose even when blood sugar is already high. Fat in the pancreas dulls the beta cells. Each makes the other worse, which is why losing that organ fat can reverse the disease in some people (Part 5).

Why it arrives earlier in India
101 million

Indian adults living with diabetes, plus about 136 million with prediabetes (ICMR-INDIAB, 2023).

At the same body weight, South Asians tend to carry more fat in the belly and liver and less muscle. The disease often appears about a decade earlier and at a lower BMI, which is why screening for Asian Indians often starts at a BMI of 23, not 25.

Part 2 · Why these organs

The cells that can't close the door

Here is the twist. Muscle and fat cells use an insulin-operated door (a transporter called GLUT4). When insulin stops working, those doors stay shut and those cells are protected from the excess.

Other cells use doors that are always open (mainly GLUT1). They take in glucose in proportion to how much is in the blood. When blood sugar stays high, these cells fill with more sugar than they can handle. The lining of every blood vessel works this way, and so do the retina, the kidney's filters and the nerves. This is the main reason diabetes damages these tissues first.

Muscle · fat · liver

Insulin-operated door

When insulin stops working, the door stays shut. Glucose backs up into the blood. The cell itself is spared.

Vessel lining · retina · kidney filter · nerve

Always-open door

The cell can't refuse the extra sugar. Its mitochondria overload and throw off free radicals, which switch on damaging pathways: sorbitol build-up, glycation, inflammation and leaky, thickened vessel walls.

Capillaries · 5–10 µm wide, one cell thick

Small-vessel damage

Tiny vessels leak, thicken and close off. This is the damage that belongs to diabetes specifically: eyes, kidneys and nerves. It tracks blood sugar closely, so lowering sugar prevents it most directly.

Arteries · up to 25 mm wide

Large-vessel damage

Plaque builds faster in the arteries of the heart, brain and legs. This is driven by the whole insulin-resistance package: high blood pressure, high triglycerides, low HDL, inflammation, and sugar. Sugar control alone helps less here.

Part 3 · The slow browning

Sugar that sticks

Glucose is chemically sticky. Left in contact with proteins, it bonds to them without any enzyme involved. It is the same Maillard reaction that browns toast and caramelises onions, running at 37°C instead of 180°C. The higher the sugar and the longer it stays, the further the reaction goes.

Stage 1

A loose touch

Hours · reversible

Glucose latches loosely onto a protein. If sugar falls, it lets go.

Stage 2

A firm bond

Weeks · mostly stable

The bond rearranges into a sturdier form. HbA1c is this stage, measured on haemoglobin.

Stage 3

Permanent cross-links

Months to years · irreversible

Advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) weld proteins together. Collagen in arteries, skin, tendons and the eye's lens stiffens, and AGEs trigger inflammation.

Long-lived proteins such as collagen collect AGEs for decades. This is one reason years of high sugar leave a "metabolic memory" that persists after control improves.

Part 4 · The damage, system by system

Nine places it shows up

Most of this damage is silent for years. Kidney disease, retinopathy and nerve damage usually cause no symptoms until they are advanced, and many people already have early signs when they are first diagnosed. Each card ends with the check that catches it early.

Heart and arteries

~2×risk of heart attack; heart disease is the leading cause of death in Type 2 diabetes

Sugar, high insulin, high blood pressure and the blood-fat pattern of insulin resistance (high triglycerides, low HDL, small dense LDL) inflame artery walls and speed plaque growth. Nerve damage can blunt chest pain, so heart attacks may be silent. The heart muscle can also stiffen, leading to heart failure.

  • Large vessels
  • Fat build-up

CheckBlood pressure at every visit; lipid profile yearly.

Brain and mood

1.5–2×risk of stroke and of dementia

Blocked large arteries cause strokes. Damaged small vessels scar the brain's white matter, and the brain itself can become insulin resistant. Both are linked to faster memory decline. Depression is about twice as common, and it makes managing diabetes harder.

  • Large vessels
  • Small vessels

CheckRaise memory or mood changes early; control blood pressure.

Eyes

1 in 3people with diabetes have some retinopathy

The retina's capillaries balloon, leak and close off. Starved of oxygen, the retina grows fragile new vessels that can bleed and scar. Fluid in the macula blurs central vision. Early stages cause no symptoms. Glycation also clouds the lens, so cataracts come earlier.

  • Small vessels
  • Glycation

CheckDilated eye exam or retinal photo at diagnosis, then every 1–2 years.

Kidneys

~40%develop kidney disease; diabetes is the top cause of kidney failure

Each kidney has about a million tiny filters (glomeruli). High sugar first makes them over-filter, then thicken and scar. The earliest sign is a protein called albumin leaking into urine. Filtering capacity then falls slowly, with no symptoms, until it is advanced.

  • Small vessels

CheckUrine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) and eGFR, yearly.

Nerves

Up to 50%eventually develop nerve damage

The longest nerves fail first, so it starts in the toes and climbs in a "stocking and glove" pattern: numbness, burning, pins and needles. Nerves that run the organs are hit too: slow stomach emptying, dizziness on standing, bladder trouble, and erectile dysfunction in about half of men with diabetes.

  • Nerves
  • Small vessels

CheckMonofilament and vibration test on the feet, yearly.

Feet and legs

19–34%lifetime chance of a foot ulcer

Three problems meet in the feet. Numb skin doesn't feel a blister or a stone in the shoe. Narrowed leg arteries starve the wound of oxygen. High sugar weakens the immune response. A small cut can become an ulcer and then a deep infection. Diabetes is the leading cause of amputation not caused by injury.

  • Nerves
  • Large vessels
  • Immunity

CheckLook at your own feet daily; clinician foot exam yearly.

Liver and pancreas

Over halfof people with Type 2 diabetes have fatty liver disease

Fat builds up in the liver (now called MASLD). A fatty liver resists insulin, releases extra glucose and pumps out triglycerides. It can progress to inflammation, scarring, cirrhosis and liver cancer. Fat in the pancreas further weakens the beta cells, closing the loop.

  • Fat build-up

CheckLiver enzymes and a FIB-4 score; FibroScan if raised.

Immunity, skin and gums

~3×risk of tuberculosis

High sugar slows white blood cells and feeds bacteria and fungi. Urinary, skin and fungal infections are more common and wounds heal slowly. Gum disease is more frequent and severe, and it pushes blood sugar up in return. Dark, velvety patches on the neck (acanthosis nigricans) are a visible sign of insulin resistance.

  • Immunity

CheckDental visit every 6 months; keep vaccinations current.

Muscles, bones and joints

~1 in 8develop frozen shoulder, several times the usual rate

Glycated collagen stiffens tendons and joint capsules: frozen shoulder, trigger finger, stiff hands that can't press flat together. Bones may look dense on scans but are more brittle, so fractures are more likely. Muscle is the body's biggest glucose sink, and losing it with age and inactivity worsens insulin resistance.

  • Glycation

CheckStrength training; it clears glucose without needing insulin.

Part 5 · What changes the course

The gradient runs both ways

Diabetes damage is not a switch that flips at diagnosis. It scales with how high sugar runs and for how long. That means every step down counts. In the UK Prospective Diabetes Study, which followed about 3,600 people with newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes, each 1-point lower HbA1c was linked to these lower risks:

Lower risk associated with each 1 percentage point lower HbA1c. UKPDS 35 (BMJ, 2000), an observational analysis. Heart attacks fall least because large-vessel disease depends heavily on blood pressure and cholesterol, as Part 2 explains.

Remission is possible

Lose the organ fat

In the DiRECT trial, 46% of people in a structured weight-loss programme were in remission after a year, off all diabetes drugs. Among those who lost 15 kg or more, 86% were. It works best within the first few years after diagnosis, while beta cells can still recover.

Protection beyond sugar

Newer drugs guard organs

Two drug families do more than lower glucose. SGLT2 inhibitors reduce heart-failure hospital stays and slow kidney decline. GLP-1 receptor agonists cut heart attacks and strokes and help with weight loss. Which one fits depends on the person, and is a decision for a doctor.

The legacy effect

Early control lasts

When UKPDS participants were followed for 10 more years, those who had tighter control early still had fewer heart attacks and deaths, even after their HbA1c levels converged. The years just after diagnosis carry the most weight.
