A pill, explained in pictures. Then under the hood.

Statins

They tell your liver to make less cholesterol and clean up more of it from your blood.

Flip the pill switch at the bottom. Every picture changes. Dashed "Under the hood" panels go technical.

First, cholesterol isn't the villain

Your body needs some

The trouble is too much LDL in the blood. LDL is the little truck that carries cholesterol around. Too many trucks, and they get stuck in your artery walls.

Meet the truck: one LDL particle

Under the hood

LDL vs HDL
LDL delivers cholesterol out to tissues. HDL carries spare cholesterol back to the liver. Statins mainly cut LDL; HDL rises only a little, roughly 5–10%.
ApoB = particle count
Every LDL particle carries exactly one ApoB-100. So an ApoB blood test counts the trucks directly, while "LDL-C" measures the cargo.
How plaque starts
LDL slips under the artery lining and gets oxidised there. Immune cells called macrophages swallow it and swell into "foam cells". Piles of foam cells are the start of plaque.

Where it comes from

Mostly, you make it

Food adds a trickle. Your liver adds the big stream. So statins go to the liver.

Under the hood

Export as VLDL
Almost every cell can make cholesterol, but the liver is the one that ships it out in bulk. It packs cholesterol and fat into VLDL particles, which shrink into LDL as they give up fat in the bloodstream.
Liver-seeking drug
Swallowed statin goes straight from gut to liver through the portal vein. A transporter pump on liver cells, OATP1B1, pulls it in. Most of the dose is caught on this first pass, so little reaches the rest of the body.

Inside the liver factory

A long assembly line

Dozens of steps. But one machine sets the pace for the whole line.

The statin jams that one machine. Far less cholesterol comes out the end.

Under the hood

~30 reactions
Two-carbon acetyl-CoA units are stitched into 27-carbon cholesterol through nearly 30 enzyme reactions.
HMGCR
HMG-CoA reductase sits in the membrane of the ER (the cell's protein-and-fat workshop). It turns HMG-CoA into mevalonate, spending 2 NADPH. This is the first step that can't be undone, so the cell controls it hardest. Block it, and the whole line starves.
Three dials
The cell tunes HMGCR three ways: how much gets made (gene switch SREBP-2), how fast it's destroyed (excess sterols tag it for disposal), and whether it's switched off (AMPK, the low-energy sensor, phosphorylates it).

Under the hood

The disguise
Every statin carries a dihydroxy-acid "head" shaped like the HMG part of HMG-CoA. A bulky "tail" wedges into a groove beside the active site, so the real substrate can't get in.
The grip
Statins bind with an inhibition constant (Ki) around 0.1–2 nM. The real substrate's Km is about 4 µM. That is a grip thousands of times tighter.
Competitive, reversible
The drug wins the slot by numbers and lets go when levels fall. So dose and half-life matter. Lovastatin and simvastatin are swallowed as inactive lactones and opened into the active acid inside the body.

Upstream means everything downstream

One jam, many products

Mevalonate feeds more than cholesterol. The same line supplies three other products.

Jam the key step and all of it slows, not just cholesterol.

Under the hood

Pleiotropic effects
Prenyl tags pin signalling proteins like Ras and Rho to membranes. With fewer tags, inflammatory signalling in the artery wall calms down. This is part of why statins steady plaque beyond what the LDL drop alone predicts.
The CoQ10 question
Statins lower CoQ10 in blood. A link to muscle aches is suspected but unproven, and supplement trials haven't shown a clear benefit.

How the cell notices

A cholesterol thermostat

Plenty of cholesterol: Insig holds the SCAP + SREBP-2 pair parked in the ER. Nothing happens.

Cholesterol falls: the pair is released, cut, and the fragment flips on the genes.

  1. SenseSCAP binds cholesterol in the ER. When it falls below about 5% of the membrane's lipids, SCAP changes shape and lets go of Insig.
  2. EscortSCAP carries SREBP-2 in tiny vesicles to the Golgi.
  3. CutTwo proteases, S1P then S2P, snip SREBP-2 and free its front half, a transcription factor.
  4. Switch onThe fragment enters the nucleus, binds Sterol Regulatory Elements in DNA and turns up its target genes.

Under the hood

The body pushes back
The same switch also makes more HMGCR (more machines to jam) and more PCSK9 (which destroys receptors). That counter-pull is why doubling a statin dose buys only about 6% more LDL lowering.

The clever part

The liver goes shopping

A few catchers on the liver's surface. Lots of LDL left floating in the blood.

Running short inside, the liver puts out many more catchers and pulls LDL out of your blood.

One catcher's round trip

  1. GrabThe receptor (LDLR) locks onto ApoB-100 and drifts into a pit coated with the protein clathrin.
  2. SwallowThe pit pinches off into a bubble, carrying LDL inside the cell.
  3. Let goThe bubble turns acidic. The receptor folds up and releases its LDL.
  4. RecycleThe receptor returns to the surface; LDL goes to the lysosome and its cholesterol is freed.

Under the hood

~150 round trips
One receptor makes a round trip about every 10 minutes and around 150 trips in its roughly 20-hour life. Brown and Goldstein mapped this cycle and won the 1985 Nobel Prize for it.
PCSK9, the shredder
PCSK9 is a protein the liver releases. It clips onto a receptor so it can't let go in the endosome, and the whole receptor gets dragged to the lysosome and destroyed. Fewer round trips, more LDL left in blood.
Genetic proof
People born with broken LDLR genes (familial hypercholesterolaemia) have very high LDL and early heart disease. People with weak PCSK9 have low LDL for life and far fewer heart attacks.

Higher dose, bigger drop.

Extra LDL keeps sinking into artery walls. The plaque grows. The channel narrows.

Plaque stops growing and turns steadier, less likely to burst. Fewer heart attacks and strokes.

The number that matters

−22%

Major heart attacks, strokes and artery procedures fall about 22% for every 1 mmol/L (≈ 39 mg/dL) drop in LDL. That comes from the Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' 2010 analysis of 26 trials and about 170,000 people. The benefit tracks the LDL drop, however it's achieved, and grows the longer LDL stays low.

The rule of 6

The first dose does most of the work; each doubling after that adds only about 6 points. Switching to a more potent statin moves you further than piling on dose.

The night shift

The factory works hardest while you sleep

Cholesterol synthesis peaks overnight. Statins that clear in a couple of hours are taken in the evening to catch that peak. Long-lasting ones cover the whole day, so timing doesn't matter.

Side effects, honestly

What the trials say

Under the hood

Muscle aches
In 19 blinded trials, over 90% of muscle complaints among statin users happened just as often on placebo (CTT, 2022). Real statin myopathy exists, and severe muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis) is rare, around 1 in 10,000 people a year.
Your genes
A common variant in SLCO1B1, the gene for the OATP1B1 pump, slows uptake into the liver. More statin stays in the blood and reaches muscle. It matters most for high-dose simvastatin.
Interactions
Drugs that block CYP3A4 raise atorvastatin, simvastatin and lovastatin levels. Examples are clarithromycin, some antifungals, some HIV drugs, and large amounts of grapefruit juice. Rosuvastatin and pravastatin are largely spared.
Blood sugar
Risk of new diabetes rises about 9%. That is roughly 1 extra case per 255 people treated for 4 years, set against about 5 heart attacks or strokes prevented in the same group (Sattar et al., 2010).
Liver enzymes
A mild rise in ALT is common and usually harmless. Serious liver injury is very rare.
Stopping
The effect is fully reversible. Stop the pill and LDL returns to where it was within weeks.

Where it came from

Born from a mould

  1. 1973

    Akira Endo at Sankyo screens thousands of fungi and finds compactin in Penicillium citrinum. Some fungi make it as a weapon, starving rival microbes of the sterols they need.

  2. 1985

    Brown and Goldstein win the Nobel Prize for discovering the LDL receptor, which explains why blocking synthesis clears blood LDL.

  3. 1987

    Lovastatin, from Aspergillus terreus, becomes the first statin approved in the US.

  4. 1994

    The 4S trial: simvastatin cuts deaths by about 30% in people with heart disease. Statins become standard care.
