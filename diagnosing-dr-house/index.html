DDx:What does House actually believe?

Gregory House never states a philosophy. He runs one. Every episode he treats a question the same way: list the explanations, distrust what people tell you, test until only one survives.

The mental model for this page: House applies the differential diagnosis to everything, including ethics, religion and people. So we will do the same to him. Below are his presenting symptoms, his method, and a live differential that tests him against eleven philosophies we already know.

Part 2 · Mechanism

How House knows anything

Before asking what House values, look at how he decides what is true. His theory of knowledge is the engine; the rudeness is exhaust.

A differential is a list of hypotheses kept alive only until evidence kills them. That is Karl Popper's picture of science, played out in a hospital corridor. Step 2 is what C.S. Peirce called abduction: guessing the explanation that would make the surprising facts unsurprising. When House orders a treatment "to see what happens," he is running an experiment on a hypothesis.

His ranking of evidence

Indian logic (Nyāya) names four valid means of knowledge, the pramāṇas. House uses all four, but ranks them in almost the reverse order that ordinary life does.

Pratyakṣa · perception

What the body shows

Biopsy, MRI, the rash that appears at 3 a.m. Trusted most, because tissue cannot lie.

Anumāna · inference

Reasoning from signs to causes

Fever plus this, minus that, means this. His core skill.

Upamāna · comparison

"This is like that"

His famous epiphanies. Wilson says something unrelated, House sees the analogy, and the case breaks open.

Śabda · testimony

What the patient says

Trusted least. Hence "Everybody lies," and hence the fellows breaking into patients' homes to read the fridge instead of the patient.

    Part 4 · Complications

    Where the method stops working

    The show is not an advertisement for House's philosophy. It is eight seasons of stress-testing it. Four fault lines keep opening.

    Complication 1

    The liar who says everybody lies

    His rule has no exception clause, so it covers him too. "I'm fine." "I need the Vicodin for the pain, not because I'm addicted." The one testimony House never sends for a biopsy is his own. Sartre had a name for this: bad faith, lying to yourself so you don't have to face a choice.

    Complication 2

    You can't get an "ought" from an "is"

    David Hume's point: facts alone never tell you what to value. In the hospital the goal is handed to House (keep the patient alive), so a pure fact-finding method is enough. Outside the hospital nobody hands him the goal. His method can tell him what is true about his life. It cannot tell him what to want from it.

    Complication 3

    Skill is not wisdom

    Aristotle separated technē, mastery of a craft, from phronēsis, the practical wisdom of living well. House has world-class technē. The drama comes from how little of the second he has, and how clearly everyone around him can see it.

    Complication 4

    Wilson is the counter-argument

    House is built on Sherlock Holmes: House/Homes, Wilson/Watson, apartment 221B, Vicodin for Holmes's cocaine. Wilson plays the other school, closest to care ethics: morality starts in relationships, not in rules or outcomes. House mocks it for eight seasons. Then the ending takes a side.

    Series finale spoiler

    In the last episode, House fakes his own death, ending his medical career, so he can spend Wilson's final months with him. The rationalist's last move is an act of care, and it disproves his own favourite line: "People don't change."

    One line to remember: House runs the scientific method on everything. It works wherever the goal is given, as in a sick patient who needs to live. It fails wherever the goal has to be chosen: meaning, love, and himself.
