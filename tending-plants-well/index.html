Care tag for any plant

How to tend plants well

Most houseplants that die were watered faithfully by someone who cared. One idea fixes that. A plant is a solar-powered pump. Light runs it. Everything you do either feeds the pump or keeps up with it.

The trouble

Plants die of attention more often than neglect

The usual advice is a schedule: water once a week. A schedule ignores the one thing that decides how much water a plant uses, which is how much light it gets. The same plant can drink a pot dry in four days on a sunny sill and take a month in a dim corner.

Water on schedule in low light and the soil never dries. Wet soil has no air in it, and roots need oxygen as much as they need water. They suffocate, then rot. Rotten roots cannot take up water, so the plant wilts. It looks thirsty. So it gets watered again.

The model

A plant is a solar-powered pump

Leaves make the plant's food out of light, air and water. To take in air they open thousands of tiny pores, and water evaporates out through them. That loss pulls more water up from the roots, the way a drink climbs a straw. Almost all the water a plant takes up passes straight through and leaves as vapour.

Light is the throttle

More light means a faster pump, more growth and more water used. Fertiliser cannot stand in for it.

Water is the supply line

It carries minerals up and keeps the cells firm. The plant needs exactly as much as the light makes it use.

Soil is half air

A good mix holds water in its particles and air in the gaps between them. Keep the gaps flooded and the roots drown.

Light

Light comes first, and there is less of it than you think

Your eyes adjust to brightness so well that a room looks evenly lit when it is nothing of the kind. Light falls off steeply with every step back from the glass. A spot two metres into the room can get a tenth of what the windowsill gets, and the windowsill gets a fraction of the open sky.

For a plant, low light is slow starvation. It can live off its reserves for months, which is why the decline seems to come from nowhere.

The shadow test

At midday, hold your hand a foot above the spot. A crisp dark shadow is direct sun. A soft shadow with a clear outline is bright indirect light, which suits most houseplants. A faint blur is low light.

The sky test

Crouch to leaf height and look out. If the plant can see a wide piece of sky, it is in good light. If it sees only walls, it is in low light, however bright the room feels to you.

The phone test

A free light meter app gives a rough lux reading. Measure at leaf height around midday and compare it with the chart above.

Water

Ask the soil, then water properly

  1. Check

    Push a finger into the soil to the second knuckle, about 5 cm. Cool and damp means wait. Dry means water. Lifting the pot works too: a dry pot is surprisingly light.

  2. Soak

    Water slowly over the whole surface until it runs out of the drainage hole. That wets every root and flushes out built-up salts.

  3. Drain

    After ten minutes, empty the saucer. A pot standing in water has no air at the bottom.

  4. Wait

    Leave it until the check says dry again. That could be four days in strong sun and four weeks in a dull spot.

How dry counts as dry depends on the plant.

Succulents and cacti
dry all the way down

Small sips every day are the worst of both. The surface stays wet, the deep roots stay dry, and salts build up in the pot.

Pot and soil

Give the roots air

A hole in the bottom

Without a drainage hole, water pools at the base where you cannot see it. If you love a pot with no hole, keep the plant in a plain nursery pot and stand that inside it.

A loose mix

Garden soil packs down in a pot and squeezes the air out. Use potting mix and open it up with something chunky: perlite, bark or coco chips. For succulents, make half the mix grit.

A snug pot

Go up one size at a time, 2 to 5 cm wider. A small plant in a big pot sits in a mass of wet soil that its roots cannot dry.

Repot when roots circle the inside of the pot or poke out of the hole, usually every year or two, at the start of the growing season. Terracotta breathes and dries fast, which forgives a heavy hand. Plastic and glazed pots hold water longer.

Feeding

Fertiliser is a vitamin, never a meal

Plants make their own food from light. Fertiliser supplies the minerals they build with. The three numbers on every pack are nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, always in that order.

Feed only a plant that is growing in good light, at half the strength on the label, every two to four weeks. A plant in dim light cannot use it, and the unused salts burn the roots. Hold off on any plant that is dry, wilting or freshly repotted.

Symptoms

Read the leaves, then check the soil

Leaves report a problem weeks after it starts, and several causes look alike. The soil tells them apart. Pick what you see.

What to do

Routine

What matters, in order

  1. Light

    Get this right and the plant forgives most mistakes. Get it wrong and nothing below can make up for it.

  2. Watering rhythm

    Follow the soil. Soak, drain, wait.

  3. Pot and mix

    A drainage hole, a loose mix, the right size.

  4. Everything else

    Feeding, humidity, leaf shine. Small gains, worth chasing once the first three are right. Misting raises humidity for a few minutes; grouping plants together does more.

A five-minute round, once a week

Look
New growth is the best sign of health. Check under the leaves for pests.
Feel
Finger in the soil. Water only the pots that ask.
Turn
A quarter turn keeps growth even instead of leaning to the window.
Wipe
Dust blocks light. A damp cloth every few weeks.
Leave
Change one thing at a time and give it three weeks. Plants answer slowly.

Growth follows light and warmth through the year. In bright, warm months the pump runs fast, so water and feed more. In dull, cool or very humid stretches it slows, so water less and stop feeding.

Light runs the pump. Water keeps up with it. Roots need air.
