Is evil a brain disorder?

If a tumour caused the crime, who is responsible?

A killer asked doctors to look inside his head.

Charles Whitman killed his wife and mother, then shot strangers from a university tower. His note described “many unusual and irrational thoughts” and asked for an autopsy. Doctors found a tumour the size of a pecan.

The official inquiry said it could have played a part. Nobody could prove that it did.

A tumour that worked like a switch.

A teacher with no record of offending developed sudden sexual urges towards children. A scan found an egg-sized tumour pressing on the front of his brain. Step through what happened next.

He knew it was wrong. He told doctors the urges overrode his restraint.

Same man, same values. When the tumour went, the behaviour went with it.

The front of the brain is the brake.

Deep inside sits an engine of fear, anger and desire. The front of the brain weighs consequences and says stop. The teacher’s tumour sat on that brake.

In 2018, researchers mapped 17 people who turned to crime after a brain injury.

17 injuries, in 17 different places.

A second set of 23 cases showed the same pattern. An injury here raises the risk. It does not make crime certain.

Do killers’ brains look different?

On average, yes. In 1997, scans of 41 murderers showed a quieter brake than scans of 41 other people. Studies of psychopathic prisoners, the profile that fits many serial killers, find the same thing.

The third scan has the same quiet front. Guess who it belongs to.

A scan can show risk. It cannot show guilt.

All 41 murderers had pleaded insanity, so they are not typical killers.

Biology loads the dice. Childhood rolls them.

Twelve boys in every hundred had two things: a low-activity version of a gene called MAOA, and an abusive childhood. That small group collected 44 of every 100 convictions for violence.

Boys with the same gene and a safe childhood were no more violent than anyone else.

The tumour is only the cause we can see.

Six people did something terrible. Each had something in the brain that made it more likely. Improve the machine and watch who gets excused.

1966Tomorrow

With the naked eye, 1 of 6 is excused.

The people never changed. The machine did. This is neuroscientist David Eagleman’s argument: blame follows our instruments.

Who is driving?

Volunteers flicked a wrist whenever they chose. Their brains began ramping up about half a second before the movement. They reported deciding only 0.2 seconds before.

Some read this as proof that the feeling of choosing arrives late. Later studies suggest the early ramp may be background noise, so the experiment proves less than its fame suggests.

In court, a brain scan cuts both ways.

The same picture says he is less to blame and more likely to do it again. When judges heard a biological explanation for a psychopath’s violence, they took about a year off.

In Tennessee in 2009, a jury heard that a killer carried the MAOA variant and had been abused as a child. It convicted him of manslaughter instead of murder.

So who is responsible? Three answers.

The science is shared. The disagreement is about what blame is for.

Findings

Is evil a brain disorder?

Sometimes a damaged brain produces terrible acts. Most people with the same damage never hurt anyone, and plenty of people who do terrible things show nothing unusual on a scan.

Evil is a judgment about an act. A disorder is a fact about a brain. Scanners can find the second. They are silent on the first.

If a tumour caused the crime, who is responsible?

Nearly everyone agrees the teacher needed a surgeon more than a prison. His brake was cut, and surgery fixed it.

The argument starts with everyone else. If a tumour excuses, why not a gene, or a childhood? Science can say what caused an act. How much blame to attach is still our decision.
