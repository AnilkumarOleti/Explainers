Mobile internet on the move

Your phone changes towers every minute. The internet never finds out.

At 160 km/h you cover 44 metres every second, and no tower reaches far enough to keep up. The connection survives because the network passes you from tower to tower, while one fixed point deep inside it holds on to your address.

Standing still first

Only the first hop is wireless

Start with a phone that isn't moving. When you open a video, your phone doesn't reach the internet through the air. It sends radio waves to the nearest tower, usually a kilometre or two away. From the tower onward everything is cable: fibre to your operator's core network, a gateway there that gave your phone its IP address, then the public internet and the video's server.

So a moving vehicle changes one thing: which tower sits at the end of that first hop. Everything behind the tower stays where it is. The job is to swap that one link quickly, and to do it without the rest of the chain noticing.

The swap

A handover takes five steps and a few hundredths of a second

Each tower covers a patch of ground called a cell. Neighbouring cells overlap on purpose, and the switch from one tower to the next happens inside that overlap. Engineers call the switch a handover.

  1. Listen

    Even in the middle of a video, your phone keeps measuring the tower it is on and every neighbour it can hear, several times a second.

  2. Report

    When a neighbour has been stronger than the current tower by a set margin for a set time, the phone tells its current tower.

  3. Prepare

    The current tower asks the neighbour to reserve capacity for you. The neighbour replies with the details your phone needs to join it.

  4. Switch

    The phone gets the order, drops the old tower and locks on to the new one. This is the only moment you are disconnected, typically for tens of milliseconds. Packets that reach the old tower during the gap are forwarded to the new one.

  5. Reroute

    The new tower tells the core network to send your traffic its way from now on. The old tower frees your slot for someone else.

Speed changes this routine in one way. A 4G phone keeps count of how many cells it has crossed recently. Cross enough of them and it treats itself as fast-moving and shortens its waiting time, because at 200 km/h half a second of hesitation is 28 metres of road.

The fixed point

Your address stays at a gateway, far from the road

The video server sends its packets to one IP address. If that address changed at every tower, every download and every call would break about once a minute. It doesn't change, because the address never belonged to a tower. A gateway in your operator's core network issued it when your phone connected, and keeps it for the whole session.

Between that gateway and whichever tower you are on runs a tunnel, a private forwarding path for your packets. The last step of a handover moves the tower end of the tunnel. The gateway end stays put, and so does the address the internet sees.

The physics

Speed bends the radio signal, slightly

Moving toward a tower squeezes its radio waves to a higher frequency. Moving away stretches them lower. This is the Doppler effect, the same thing that drops the pitch of a train horn as it passes. The size of the shift is the signal's frequency multiplied by your speed and divided by the speed of light.

These are small numbers. A 4G signal is split into narrow channels 15,000 Hz apart, and mid-band 5G uses 30,000 Hz, so the worst case in the table is about 2% of the spacing. The phone measures the shift from known reference signals and cancels it. When you pass a tower the shift flips from plus to minus within a second or two, and the phone follows that as well.

The harder effect is how quickly the radio path itself changes. Signals reach you by bouncing off buildings, ground and other vehicles, and the bounces add up differently every few centimetres. At walking pace the pattern holds for tens of milliseconds. At 200 km/h it reshuffles about every millisecond. The tower picks its data rate from your phone's reports about the channel, and at speed those reports are out of date when they arrive. The tower responds by sending more cautiously, with more error protection and less data in each burst. That is why full bars on a fast train deliver less than the same bars on the platform.

Both standards were written with this in mind. 4G is specified to hold a connection up to 350 km/h and 5G up to 500 km/h. A car at 100 and a train at 200 are well inside the design range.

Where it breaks

The stutters come from four places

The metal box
A coach or car body blocks radio. A modern train coach with metal-coated windows can weaken the signal 100 to 1,000 times (20–30 dB). Your phone copes by sending slower, sturdier transmissions.
Gaps between towers
A handover needs overlap. In open country, cuttings and tunnels, the old signal can vanish before the new tower is ready. The phone then reconnects from scratch, which takes from a few hundred milliseconds to several seconds.
Everyone crosses together
On a train, hundreds of phones reach the same cell edge within the same second. The next tower has to admit all of them at once and split its capacity among them.
A thin line behind the tower
A remote tower may reach the core over a modest link, sometimes a microwave dish instead of fibre. It cannot deliver more than that link carries, however strong your signal is.

Apps hide most of this. A video player keeps many seconds of video in reserve, so a 50-millisecond handover or even a five-second tunnel passes unnoticed. A live call has no reserve beyond a fraction of a second. That is why streaming survives a train journey that makes video calls freeze.

The fixes

How fast vehicles get a better link

Antenna on the roof
Put the antenna outside the metal and repeat the signal inside as Wi-Fi or a cellular booster. The 20–30 dB loss goes away, and one roof unit does the handover for the whole coach.
Towers along the line
Along rail lines and expressways, cells are given long, narrow coverage aimed down the route. Overlaps get longer and handovers happen at predictable spots.
Orders given early
Newer 5G networks can hand the phone its switching order in advance, to carry out by itself once the next tower is strong enough. Losing the old tower before the order arrives stops being a risk.
Satellite
Some trains and most connected aircraft link to satellites through a roof antenna. Low-orbit satellites cross the sky at about 27,000 km/h, so the vehicle's own speed hardly counts, and the same handover idea applies between satellites.

The picture to keep

Only the last kilometre knows you are travelling

Your phone holds one radio link at a time and trades it for the next before the old one fades. Your address sits at a gateway that never moves, and the internet talks to the gateway.
