Kurt Gödel · Vienna · 1931

A true sentence that no rulebook can stamp

Picture mathematics as an office with a rulebook and one rubber stamp, marked Proved. Gödel wrote a sentence the stamp can never land on. Then he showed the sentence is true.

The plan was one rulebook for all of arithmetic

Fix a short list of starting rules. Let a clerk apply them mechanically, forever, stamping whatever follows. In the 1920s David Hilbert and most mathematicians expected the stamp to do two things.

Complete
Every true statement about whole numbers gets stamped, sooner or later.
Consistent
The stamp never lands on both a statement and its opposite.

Königsberg · September 1930On the 8th, Hilbert ends a public speech with the words “We must know. We will know.” One day earlier, at a conference in the same city, a 24-year-old Gödel had said that the plan cannot work.

First trick: give every statement a number

Assign each symbol a code. Take the primes in order (2, 3, 5, 7…), raise each to the code of the symbol in that position, and multiply. Every statement lands on its own whole number, and factoring that number gives the statement back.

Second trick: a statement about its own number

“Has a proof” is now a property of numbers, so a statement about numbers can deny it of any number. Gödel built a statement that denies it of the statement’s own number.

Now ask the rulebook about G

GI will never be stamped.

Suppose the stamp lands on G

  1. G is stamped.
  2. The rulebook can check its own paperwork, so it also stamps “G has a proof”.
  3. That is the opposite of what G says. A statement and its opposite are both stamped.
Inconsistent

From there the stamp lands on everything, 2 + 2 = 5 included.

Suppose it never does

  1. G stays blank forever.
  2. That is exactly what G says will happen.
  3. G is true, and it has no proof.
Incomplete

A true statement the rulebook cannot reach.

A rulebook that never contradicts itself must leave G blank. So G is true, and the rulebook cannot prove it.

Adding G to the rulebook does not close the gap

Make G a starting rule. The enlarged rulebook is a new rulebook, and the same construction hands it a new blank statement of its own.

The rulebook cannot certify itself

“This rulebook never contradicts itself” is also a statement about numbers. Gödel showed that a consistent rulebook can never stamp it. The assurance has to come from a stronger rulebook, which then needs one of its own.

The fine print

Theorem VI of the 1931 paper, in today’s words

Take any rulebook that a machine could check, that can add and multiply whole numbers, and that never contradicts itself. Some statement about whole numbers can be neither proved nor disproved from it.

Theorem XI

Such a rulebook cannot prove that it never contradicts itself.

Gödel’s own proof assumed slightly more than consistency. J. Barkley Rosser removed the extra assumption in 1936.

What the theorem does not say

ClaimRuling
“Mathematics is unreliable.”No. Nothing that was proved comes undone.
“Every system is incomplete.”No. Arithmetic with addition alone is complete, as Presburger showed in 1929. The gap opens once multiplication joins in.
“G can never be known.”No. From outside the rulebook we can see that G is true, provided the rulebook is consistent.
“Minds are more than machines.”Unsettled. The theorem alone does not show it.

Five years later Alan Turing met the same wall in computing. No program can tell, for every program, whether it will ever stop.
