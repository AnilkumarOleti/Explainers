Richard Dawkins · 1976

The Selfish Gene

You are a vehicle.
Your genes are the drivers.

The question

Who is really playing the game of life?

The old answer: animals act "for the good of the species."

Dawkins zoomed in. The thing that truly survives, competes and gets copied is the gene.

The beginning · ~4 billion years ago

One molecule learned to copy itself.

In a warm chemical soup, the copiers competed for building blocks.

Good copiers spread. Bad ones vanished. No plan needed.

The vehicles

Copiers built bodies to ride in.

Dawkins calls them survival machines.

Different shapes. Same job: carry genes safely into the next generation.

The relay

Bodies wear out. Genes move on.

A body lasts one lifetime. A gene gets copied into the next body, and the next.

Some of your genes are far older than humans. Yeast carries versions of them too.

Selfish ≠ mean

A selfish gene can build a kind animal.

A gene "wants" one thing: more copies of itself. Your relatives carry copies of your genes. So helping family can help your genes.

Biologist J.B.S. Haldane joked he'd dive into a river to save two brothers or eight cousins. Try it.

Friends, not just family

I help you. You help me.

Vampire bats feed hungry roost-mates. The favour comes back later.

Cheats who never pay back stop getting help. Niceness pays, if you remember who cheated.

A bonus idea

Ideas copy themselves too.

Dawkins invented the word meme in this book.

Tunes, jokes and fashions jump from brain to brain. The catchiest spread.

The twist

We can cut the strings.

Genes built us. They don't get the last word.

Humans can plan ahead and choose kindness that no gene pays for.

Keep one picture

The body is the car. The gene is the driver.

Gene

The driver. It can last for millions of years.

Body

The car. It's built, used, and wears out.

"Selfish"

Whatever copies itself best, spreads. No feelings involved.

The debate

Critics say the word "selfish" misleads, that genes work in teams, and that selection can act on groups too. The gene's-eye view is still one of biology's most used lenses.
