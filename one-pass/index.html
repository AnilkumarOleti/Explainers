They can’t scroll back.

A reader can skim, pause and reread. A listener gets one pass, at your speed, and every sentence you say pushes the last one out of reach. Nearly every good presentation habit is a fix for that one fact.

The written sentence holds its main point until the last clause and stacks two qualifications before it. On paper that’s fine. Out loud, by the time the point arrives, the listener has already dropped the start. The spoken version says the same thing in three short sentences, with the conclusion first.

Once you see talks as one-pass, the standard advice stops being a list of rules and becomes a set of consequences:

  • Fewer ideasWorking memory holds a handful of things. Pick one idea and a few supports, and cut the rest.
  • Say the point firstListeners can’t jump ahead to find out why a story matters. Tell them up front, then explain.
  • SignpostA reader sees headings and page length. A listener only knows the structure if you say it out loud: “Second of three…”
  • Repeat on purposeIn writing, repetition is padding. In a talk it lets people who drifted for ten seconds catch up.
  • Short sentencesSubject, verb, point. Save the nested clauses for the handout.
Cue 22decide first

Write the one sentence they’ll repeat tomorrow

Before you open a slide tool, finish this sentence: “If they remember one thing, it’s that…” If you can’t, the talk isn’t ready to build, because everything else gets chosen by whether it supports that line.

A good one sentence is a claim, not a topic. “An update on onboarding” is a topic. “Onboarding loses half our new users at one step, and we can fix it” is a claim. A claim gives the audience something to agree or disagree with, and it gives you a test for every slide: does this help prove the sentence?

Who is in the room?

What do they already know? Skip that. What words do they use? Use those.

What do they need?

A decision, a skill, a warning, a reason to care. Name it. It shapes the ending.

What will they doubt?

Their first objection is the evidence you need. Answer it before they raise it.

Cue 33build

Give it a shape they can hear

A reader sees the structure of a document at a glance. A listener only gets structure if you speak it. So a talk has three parts, and each one does a specific job. Here is a short example, marked up the way a stage manager marks a script.

OPEN
“Last quarter, nearly half of our new users quit before they finished setting up.”hook: a fact about their problem, not about you
“I’ll show you where they quit and why, and I’ll ask you to approve one fix.”destination + the ask, in the first minute
“Three parts: where they drop, why they drop, what we change.”the map
BODY
“First: where they drop. It’s one step, the ID check.”signpost + the point, then the proof
“So that’s where. Now, why.”close one door before opening the next
“One user told us: ‘I just wanted to look around first.’”one real voice beats three charts
CLOSE
“So: they quit at the ID check because we ask too early. Moving it after the first purchase fixes that.”recap = your one sentence, word for word
“I’m asking for two sprints to ship it.”end on the ask, not on “any questions?”

This is the old rule, tell them what you’ll tell them, tell them, tell them what you told them, and it works for the reason in Cue 1: every listener drifts at some point, and each repetition is a place to rejoin.

Talking to people who have to decide

If your audience is senior, or busy, or both, lead with the answer and then give the reasons. This is Barbara Minto’s “pyramid principle.” Researchers and analysts are trained to do the opposite: tell the story in the order they discovered it and reveal the finding at the end. That suits a paper. In a meeting, it means the decision-maker spends ten minutes wondering where this is going.

Order you found it
  1. Background
  2. Data and method
  3. What we tried
  4. What we found
  5. Recommendation (if time is left)
Order they need it
  1. Recommendation
  2. Three reasons, strongest first
  3. Evidence for each
  4. Risks and what you ruled out
  5. Method, in the appendix
Cue 44watch the house

Attention sags in the middle, so plan for it

People remember beginnings and endings best. Psychologists call these the primacy and recency effects. The middle of a talk is where attention drifts, and where most of the content usually sits.

A 30-minute talk · schematic

This shows the general shape, not measured values. The popular claim that people can only focus for 10 minutes has weak evidence behind it. The strong start, the sag and the strong finish are well supported.

You can’t remove the sag, but you can break it up. A reset is any change that signals “something new is starting”:

A storyOne specific person, one specific moment.
A questionAsk it, then wait three full seconds.
A demo or objectShow it rather than describing it.
A change of voice“Here’s the part that surprised us.”

And spend the strong moments on purpose. Don’t open with an agenda slide or thanks to the organizers. Open with your hook. Don’t close with a “Questions?” slide. Take questions, then close again with your one sentence, so the last words people hear are yours.

Cue 55slides = scenery

Slides are billboards, not documents

People can’t read and listen at the same time. If the slide is full of text, they read it and stop listening to you. Richard Mayer’s research on multimedia learning found that spoken words with a simple visual beat spoken words plus the same text on screen. So a slide should be readable in about three seconds while you keep talking.

Example slide with illustrative data.

The headline is a sentence

“Q3 retention” is a label. “The Q3 dip is seasonal” is a point. Michael Alley calls this the assertion–evidence slide: a claim on top, visual proof below.

One slide, one point

If a slide makes two points, split it. More slides that each take less time beats fewer slides that are crowded.

Highlight what matters

On a chart, color the one line you’re talking about and grey out everything else. Tell them where to look.

Notes go in the notes

If a slide is there to remind you what to say, it’s your script. Put it in the speaker notes or on a card.

Cue 66GO

Delivery: slower, with pauses, to one person at a time

Most delivery advice is really about giving listeners time to take in each sentence before the next one arrives.

Pausethe highlighter

After your key sentence, stop. Two seconds of silence tells people the last line mattered. It feels long to you and normal to them.

“Half of new users quit at one step.One.”

Paceslower than feels right

Nerves speed you up. A comfortable pace for a talk is roughly 130–150 words a minute. Slow down most for numbers, names and your main point.

Eyesone person per thought

Say a full sentence to one person, then move to someone else. Sweeping your eyes across the room connects with nobody. Talking to one person at a time feels like conversation to everyone.

Nervescall it excitement

Trying to calm down rarely works, because a racing heart doesn’t slow on command. In Alison Wood Brooks’s 2014 experiments, people who told themselves “I am excited” before a speech were rated as more persuasive and confident than people who told themselves “I am calm.” The physical feeling is the same. What changes is what you call it.

Rehearseout loud, standing

Going over it in your head doesn’t count. Silent run-throughs skip the hard sentences and come out short. Rehearse aloud with a timer at least three times. Memorize your first 60 seconds, because that’s when nerves are worst. Don’t memorize the rest word for word; know the order of your points.

Cue 77house lights up

Questions: repeat, answer short, bridge back

Q&A is still part of the talk, and it’s usually where people decide whether they trust you.

Repeat it
“The question is whether this holds for returning users too.”

So the room hears it, and so you get a few seconds to think.

Answer first
“Short answer: mostly, yes. Here’s why…”

Same rule as the talk. Lead with the answer and give reasons after.

Say “I don’t know”
“I don’t know. I’ll check and send it to everyone by Friday.”

Then actually send it. Guessing costs more trust than admitting a gap.

Bridge back
“Which comes back to the main point: the fix is small and it’s early.”

End the Q&A with your one sentence again.
