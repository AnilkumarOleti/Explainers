Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine · 2026

A light switch for neurons

Karl Deisseroth shares the prize with Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel for optogenetics. They borrowed a light sensor from a pond alga, put it into chosen brain cells, and used a flash of blue to turn those cells on.

Awarded “for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics.”

The problem

Nobody could pick one kind of cell

The brain packs many kinds of nerve cell into the same space. To learn what one kind does, you have to drive that kind alone, and quickly. The old tools could not.

Where the part came from

The switch was found in a pond

Chlamydomonas is a single green cell that swims toward light. In the early 1990s Peter Hegemann set out to learn how it sees. With Georg Nagel he found the sensor, a protein they named channelrhodopsin.

How the part works

A gate that light opens

Channelrhodopsin sits in the cell's outer skin. Blue light opens it in a few thousandths of a second and charged particles rush in. In a nerve cell, that rush is what starts a signal.

Fitting instructions

How the switch gets into a brain cell

Karl Deisseroth's lab at Stanford showed in 2005 that the alga's protein works in nerve cells, then turned it into a kit any lab could use.

  1. Copy the gene

    Take the alga's recipe for the gate.

  2. Add an address

    A harmless virus delivers it. A genetic address label means only one kind of cell builds it.

  3. Bring the light

    A fibre as thin as a hair carries blue light to the spot.

  4. Flash

    One flash gives one signal, exactly on cue.

On and off

Blue starts a cell. Yellow stops it.

A second protein, taken from a salt-loving microbe, does the opposite job. Yellow light silences the cell for as long as it shines. The cell below carries both.

Why it matters

From watching the brain to testing it

Recording from the brain shows what happens alongside a behaviour. Switching cells on and off shows what causes it. Labs now use the method to trace the circuits behind memory, fear, hunger, sleep, movement and addiction.

Medicine is at an early stage. In 2021 a man blind from an inherited eye disease regained partial sight after a light-sensitive protein was added to cells in his retina. On the day of the prize one laureate said he knew of only one patient helped so far.

Who did what

Three laureates

  • Peter Hegemann

    Humboldt University of Berlin

    Asked how a pond alga senses light, and spent decades finding out.

  • Georg Nagel

    University of Würzburg

    Proved the sensor is a gate that light opens, and that it works in other cells too.

  • Karl Deisseroth

    Stanford University

    Put the gate into nerve cells and built the method that brain labs use today.

A Nobel Prize goes to at most three people. Others shaped this story. Gero Miesenböck first made nerve cells respond to light through their genes in 2002. Zhuo-Hua Pan put the alga's gate into eye cells in 2004. Ed Boyden was first author of Deisseroth's 2005 paper, and many commentators said he should have shared the prize.

In order

Forty-seven years from wish to prize

  1. 1979Francis Crick writes that brain science needs a way to control one kind of cell and leave the rest alone.
  2. 1990sHegemann studies how a pond alga steers by light.
  3. 2002–03Nagel and Hegemann show channelrhodopsin is a gate that light opens.
  4. 2005Deisseroth's lab makes nerve cells fire with flashes of blue.
  5. 2007The off switch arrives. Yellow light silences cells.
  6. 2021A blind patient regains partial sight.
  7. 2026Nobel Prize, shared equally. 12 million Swedish kronor.
