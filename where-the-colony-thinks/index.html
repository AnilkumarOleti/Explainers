Notes on Hofstadter's Ant Fugue

Where the colony thinks

No single ant is intelligent, yet the colony seems to be. Where does that intelligence live, and does the same answer hold for neurons, companies and markets?

Short answer

In the loop between the ants and the trail they keep rewriting. No ant holds it, and the ground alone doesn't either.

A brain fits that answer almost exactly. A company fits it about halfway. A market fits the mechanism and has no mind to go with it.

The puzzle

One ant knows almost nothing

In the Ant Fugue chapter of Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979), Douglas Hofstadter has an anteater describe his friendship with an ant colony called Aunt Hillary. He talks with her by reading the trails her ants form. The ants take no part in the conversation and could not follow it. He eats a few of them now and then, and the colony counts him a friend anyway.

The joke carries a real question. A worker ant has about 250,000 neurons and follows a handful of rules about smell and touch. A colony builds, farms, fights wars and finds the shortest route to food. Something about the colony knows things that no ant knows. What is that something made of?

The experiment

Watch a colony decide

In 1989 a lab in Brussels gave Argentine ants a bridge with two branches between nest and food, one about twice as long as the other. Within a few minutes most of the traffic was on the short branch. No ant compared the two. Each one laid a little scent as it walked and leaned toward whichever branch smelled stronger. Ants on the short branch got back sooner, so that branch collected scent faster, so more ants chose it.

The colony has settled on the short branch. Replace its ants, or replace its bridge, and see which one costs it the answer.

Double bridge after Goss, Aron, Deneubourg and Pasteels (1989), run on Linepithema humile. Each ant at a fork takes the short branch with probability (k + S)² / ((k + S)² + (k + L)²), where S and L are the scent on each branch at that fork. k = 20 is the value Deneubourg and colleagues fitted to real Argentine ants in 1990. Each generation of ants carries a different paint mark.

  • Replace every ant. A hundred new individuals walk out of the nest onto the old bridge and take the short branch at once. Nothing was lost.
  • Replace the bridge. The same hundred ants walk out onto a clean bridge, with everything each of them ever knew. They split evenly. The colony has forgotten which branch is shorter and has to work it out again. About one run in seven it settles on the long branch, and real colonies did the same in a minority of trials.
  • Close the short branch, wait ten seconds, reopen it. The colony stays on the long branch, as the real ants did when the short branch was added late. The trail now holds an out-of-date answer, and every ant that follows it renews it. Faster fading loosens the grip, but a trail that fades that fast can't hold any decision. A clean bridge is the way out.

The answer

The knowledge sits in the trail, and the trail only works inside a loop

The two buttons settle it. Swap out every ant and the colony still knows. Swap out the ground and it doesn't. The fact that this branch is shorter is stored outside every ant, as a difference in scent between two strips of ground.

The trail on its own is only a stain, though. It means something because ants read it. It stays true because ants keep rewriting it and evaporation erases whatever they stop confirming. The intelligence is that whole loop, and it needs three things.

A local rule

Each part reacts only to what is right next to it. For an ant: go where the scent is stronger.

A shared record

Parts leave marks that other parts can read. For a colony the record is the ground.

Reinforcement with forgetting

Marks that get used grow. Marks that stop being used fade. With no growth nothing is ever decided. With no fading nothing is ever revised.

Hofstadter's own framing is levels of description. At the level of ants there is only walking and smelling. At the level of trails there are signals. At the level of the colony there is a decision. All three describe the same events. Asking which ant holds the decision is like asking which letter in a sentence holds its meaning.

Engineers arrived at the same loop independently. Harvester ants send out foragers faster when foragers are returning with food faster. That is how the internet's TCP sets its sending rate, by how quickly acknowledgements come back.

Does the ant answer hold?

This is the reference case

The memory sits on the ground, outside every ant. Biologists call this stigmergy: coordination through marks left in a shared environment. Pierre-Paul Grassé coined the word in 1959 while watching termites build.

The limits

Three things the ants have that the others may lack

The location of the intelligence transfers. Its quality doesn't come with it. A colony's loop works well because of three conditions, and each of the other systems drops at least one.

Something tuned the loop

An ant's rules look simple, but they are the survivors of a long tournament between colonies. Colonies with bad rules founded no daughter colonies. That is why following the smell adds up to something useful. No comparable process scores a market as a whole, so a market has knowledge without anything like purpose.

The parts can't see the whole

An ant has no picture of the colony, so it can't game it. People do have a picture of their company and their market, and they act on it. Traders buy because they expect other traders to buy. Employees hit the metric and let the goal slide. The loop starts responding to beliefs about itself.

The parts want what the whole wants

In most species a worker ant leaves descendants only through her colony. Your neurons share one body. Employees and traders have ends of their own. This is why a room of very capable people can decide worse than its best member. A colony never does worse than its best ant.

The shared failure

The loop that finds the short branch can also walk itself to death

Army ants that lose the main column follow whoever is in front of them. If the line closes into a ring, they circle until they die of exhaustion. The naturalist William Beebe measured one such mill in 1921 at about 370 metres around. One lap took an ant two and a half hours.

Nothing malfunctioned inside any ant. Reinforcement outran forgetting, and the trail stopped carrying information about anything except itself. A market bubble has the same structure. Buying raises the price, the rising price draws more buying, and for a while the price reports only on the crowd. The third experiment in the plot above is a small version of both.

The verdict

Same address, different tenants

In every one of these systems, what the whole knows is stored in a record its parts share and keep rewriting. How well the whole thinks depends on what tuned the loop, whether the parts can see it, and whether they want what it wants.

Ant colony
The intelligence is in the loop, entirely. Any ant can be replaced.
Brain
The same, with one addition. This loop is dense enough to model itself.
Company
Partly in the loop. The rest is in people's heads and in a plan somebody wrote.
Market
The knowledge is in the loop. There is no goal, so calling it a mind overstates it.
