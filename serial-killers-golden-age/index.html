U.S. crime historyGolden Age · 1970–1999

The open road closes.

For three decades, America produced serial killers at a rate never seen before or since. Then the numbers fell by about 85%. The killers didn't change much. The world they hunted in did.

The mental model

A serial killer needs three things to go on killing.

A “serial” killer is defined by repetition: two or more victims, killed in separate events. Many people commit one murder. What turns one murder into a series is the ability to do it again, and again. That takes three conditions. The golden age was the era when all three were wide open at once.

ACCESS

Can he get a stranger alone?

Someone who can go missing without anyone noticing for hours or days. Hitchhikers, runaways, kids roaming free, women stranded by a broken-down car.

ANONYMITY

Does anything point back to him?

A stranger with no link to the victim, in an era with no DNA matching, no cameras, no phone records. Crimes in different counties never got connected.

RUNWAY

Is he free long enough to repeat it?

Time on the street between killings. Short sentences and early parole meant violent men were back out, often already carrying earlier convictions.

Serial killings ≈ would-be killers × access × anonymity × runway

Because the factors multiply, narrowing each one somewhat shrinks the total a lot. That is how a phenomenon can fall 85% without any single breakthrough.

    Lane by lane

    What closed each lane.

    ACCESS Fewer strangers alone, fewer people who could vanish unnoticed.

    1970s

    • Hitchhiking was an ordinary way to get around, for students especially.
    • Children walked or biked alone for hours, far from home.
    • Stranded on a highway at night meant waiting for whoever stopped.
    • A missing adult often wasn't reported for days.

    Today

    • Hitchhiking has almost disappeared; rideshare trips are logged and traceable.
    • Childhood is far more supervised.
    • Almost everyone carries a phone that can call for help and be located.
    • An hour of silence gets noticed.
    “People don't hitchhike anymore. They have means of reaching out in an emergency situation using cell phones. There are cameras everywhere.”James Alan Fox, criminologist, Northeastern University

    Many golden-age killers built their method around this lane: Ted Bundy, Edmund Kemper and others picked up young women who accepted rides. The Green River Killer targeted women working on a highway strip. These victims were chosen because they could be approached by a stranger and would not be missed quickly.

    ANONYMITY Evidence that can link a stranger to a body, and bodies to each other.

    1970s

    • Blood typing could only narrow a suspect to a large share of the population.
    • Each county investigated its own cases. A killer who crossed a line reset the file.
    • No cameras, no phone location, no card payment trail.

    Today

    • DNA from a single hair or trace of skin can identify one person.
    • National DNA database (CODIS) matches crime scenes to offenders across states.
    • CCTV, licence-plate readers and phone records reconstruct movements.
    • Genetic genealogy identifies suspects through their relatives.

    This lane matters most for the second killing. Today a killer who leaves DNA at the first scene is often identified before he can commit a second. The label “serial killer” requires a second event, so many would-be serial killers are now stopped at one.

    The same tools also reach backward. In 2018 genetic genealogy identified the Golden State Killer, whose crimes date to 1974–86. Cold-case solves like this keep adding killers to the 1970s and 80s bars, which makes the old peak look even higher.

    RUNWAY How long a violent man stays free to act again.

    From the 1980s, America locked up far more people for far longer. Truth-in-sentencing and three-strikes laws in the 1990s cut early release. Many serial killers had earlier convictions for assault, rape or burglary. In the golden age those sentences were short. Later, the same men were more likely to be in prison during the years they would otherwise have killed.

    “Some of the would-be serial killers were likely behind bars as opposed to in the bars looking for victims.”James Alan Fox

    This is the bluntest of the three levers, and it came at a large social cost. It explains part of the decline in serial murder, as it does part of the fall in violent crime overall.

    The other side of the equation

    Were there also more would-be killers?

    The three lanes explain how killers operated. A separate question is whether more people with the impulse were born and raised in that era. There are three candidate explanations, and they vary in strength.

    Well supported

    A general crime wave

    The U.S. murder rate roughly doubled between the early 1960s and 1980, reaching about 10 per 100,000, then fell by more than half by the 2010s. Serial murder rose and fell with it.

    Well supported

    A baby-boom bulge

    Violent crime is mostly committed by men aged about 15 to 35. The huge post-war generation was at that age from the late 1960s to the early 1990s, which matches the peak.

    Contested

    Childhood lead exposure

    Leaded petrol peaked in the 1970s and was phased out between 1975 and 1996. Some researchers link early lead exposure to later impulsivity and violence. The evidence for a link to crime is debated, and the link to serial murder specifically is speculative.

    Checking the count

    Part of the curve reflects how we counted.

    Researchers agree that serial murder rose and then fell. Measurement probably makes both the rise and the fall look steeper than they really were.

    The rise looks bigger

    The term “serial killer” only came into wide use in the 1970s and 80s, through FBI profilers. The FBI's ViCAP system (1985) and later DNA made it possible to link killings that once looked unrelated. Some earlier series were never recognised as series.

    The fall looks bigger

    A killer is only counted once he is caught or identified. Killers active in the 2010s may not be identified for decades. Samuel Little, active from 1970 to 2005, only confessed to 93 murders in 2018. The most recent bars will grow.

    91% → 52%U.S. murders cleared, 1965 → 2024

    Police now solve a much smaller share of murders than in the 1960s. The Murder Accountability Project has used unsolved-case data to flag clusters of killings of women that may be undetected series. So the decline is real, but “better policing” alone does not explain it. The forensic tools improved while investigative capacity was stretched thin.

    In one lineThe window closed

    The golden age was an environment, not an epidemic of monsters.

    Strangers alone on open roads, crimes that left no trace, and violent men released early all came together between 1970 and 2000. Phones, DNA, cameras and prisons closed that combination, one lane at a time.
