Why can't you tickle yourself?

Your brain knows your own touch is coming, so it turns the feeling down before it arrives.

Picture noise-cancelling headphones

They listen to the noise, play its mirror image, and the two wipe each other out. You hear what is left.

Your brain does this to your own touch

When it orders a movement, it keeps a copy of the order. From the copy it guesses the feeling, then takes the guess away from the real touch.

Who moves the feather?

A robot proved it by being late

Volunteers moved a lever with one hand, and a robot stroked their other palm in step. Then the robot began to wait before moving.

The same trick runs all day

Your eyes jump, the world holds still

Your eyes dart about three times a second. The brain expects each jump, so the view stays steady. Nudge the corner of your eyelid and the world wobbles: no order was sent.

A cricket doesn't deafen itself

Its song hits 100 decibels at its own ears. One nerve cell turns its hearing down during each chirp and lets go in the gaps, so it still hears rivals.

Shoving matches escalate

Pairs were told to press back exactly as hard as they were pressed. Your own push feels weaker than it is, so the force climbed 38% a turn. Dark and light bars are the two people.

You only feel what your brain didn't see coming.

The likely reason: your own movements are old news. Turning them down leaves room for the touch that matters, like a spider on your neck.
